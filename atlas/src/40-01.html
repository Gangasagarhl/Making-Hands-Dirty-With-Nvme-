PART IV

Silent Failures: Success That Isn't

The worst failures are the ones that never show up anywhere. The command is sent, the device accepts it, it posts a completion with status 0 (Success), and the analyzer, the kernel log and the SMART log all look clean. But the drive did not do the job: blocks that should be deallocated are still there, a flush did not reach the NAND, a write went to the wrong place, an erase left data behind. This part starts with a full worked chapter on the case you raised: "I wrote 10 GB, trimmed the last 5 GB, the drive said Success, and nothing changed." Then it lists every other kind of silent or semantic failure as an error card (D-01 to D-33), and ends with a chapter on how to catch them. The main idea of this part: a Success completion only means the controller accepted the command. To know the command did its job, you need a separate check that reads the result back.

IV.1 The deallocate that didn't: a full worked case

📌 The question this chapter answers

After writing 10 GiB to a namespace, the host deallocates (trims/discards) the last 5 GiB. The drive completes the command with Success, but it does not actually deallocate. The PEX trace (the Teledyne LeCroy PCIe analyzer capture) looks normal, dmesg shows nothing, and nvme error-log is empty. How do you find out what really happened, and how do you prove it either way?

IV.1.1 Why this chapter exists

Deallocate is the clearest example of a command whose acceptance is visible but whose effect is not. Every step you can see on the wire happens normally: the Dataset Management (DSM) command, the fetch of its range list, the completion queue entry (CQE) and the MSI-X interrupt. The step that matters, the flash translation layer (FTL) dropping the logical-to-physical mapping entries, happens inside the controller and never crosses the PCIe link. So a trace can never prove that deallocation happened. It can only prove that it was requested correctly. Proof has to come from somewhere else: what a later read returns, an error that only appears when a feature is turned on, the namespace utilization counter, or a performance signature.

There is a second, less obvious point. In many cases the NVMe specification allows the drive to "not deallocate" without being wrong. Before calling a drive broken, you must work out which guarantee it actually made. The Identify Namespace fields DLFEAT and NSFEAT tell you that, and this chapter shows how to read them.

  • Trace a discard from fstrim/blkdiscard/file truncate through the Linux v7.2 block layer and nvme_setup_discard() down to the DSM command and its 16-byte range descriptors.
  • Know what Linux v7.2 sends in each case: DSM with the Attribute – Deallocate (AD) bit, or Write Zeroes with the Deallocate (DEAC) bit. Know the quirks that change this (NVME_QUIRK_DEALLOCATE_ZEROES, NVME_QUIRK_DISABLE_WRITE_ZEROES).
  • Say exactly what the NVMe specification promises for deallocated blocks (DLFEAT read behaviour, DULBE, NUSE), and what it does not promise.
  • Explain why the analyzer trace, the kernel log and the error log all look normal.
  • Prove whether deallocation happened, using five independent kinds of evidence, and run the procedure as a CAUTION lab on a spare drive.
📭 What the spec does not say

The spec does not define the drive's internal mapping table, when garbage collection runs, or how fast deallocated space is reclaimed. Deallocation is described by its visible effects: what a read of a deallocated block returns (DLFEAT), whether a read can be made to fail (DULBE), and how the Namespace Utilization (NUSE) count behaves. If a drive gives none of these guarantees, "it didn't deallocate" may be impossible to see from outside, and may not even count as a failure.

IV.1.2 The story, the analogy, and where the analogy breaks

A validation engineer runs a simple script on a 1 TB drive formatted with 4096-byte logical blocks. The script writes a known pattern over LBAs 0 to 2,621,439 (10 GiB), then runs blkdiscard --offset 5GiB --length 5GiB /dev/nvme0n1. The command returns 0. The analyzer captured one DSM command and a Success completion. Then the engineer reads LBA 2,000,000 and gets the original pattern back, not zeroes. Is the drive broken? That depends on DLFEAT. If DLFEAT says reads of deallocated blocks return zeroes (read behaviour 001b), the drive has broken its promise: it either did not deallocate, or it deallocated and then returned stale data. If DLFEAT says 000b ("not reported"), old data is an allowed result and the read-back proves nothing.

🌍 Analogy

You tell a warehouse to "throw away everything on shelves 50 to 99". The clerk signs the form and hands it back. The signed form is the CQE. Whether the shelves are actually empty, you only find out by walking over and looking (a read), by asking for the inventory count (NUSE), or by noticing that the warehouse suddenly has room for new deliveries (better write performance).

🧩 Where the analogy breaks

In a real warehouse an empty shelf always looks empty. In an SSD, the drive may be allowed to show you the old content of a "thrown away" shelf (DLFEAT read behaviour 000b), so looking is not always proof. Also, the drive may legally throw things away later (in the background). The only thing it must get right immediately is what a read returns, and only when it has promised a value.

IV.1.3 The host path in Linux v7.2: who turns "free these blocks" into NVMe commands

There are three common ways the request starts. All of them end in the block layer as a REQ_OP_DISCARD (or REQ_OP_WRITE_ZEROES) request, which the NVMe driver turns into an NVMe command in nvme_setup_cmd().

Where it startsWhat happens before the NVMe driverEasy-to-miss trap

The blocks are freed only in the filesystem's map until the next fstrim (often a weekly timer). File LBAs are also not "the last 5 GiB of the device".

Discard limits: how big pieces can be

drivers/nvme/host/core.c:nvme_update_disk_info() sets the queue limits from Identify data (v7.2, around lines 2123–2170):

  • Maximum discard size: if the controller reports DMRSL (Dataset Management Range Size Limit, from the I/O Command Set specific Identify Controller structure, read in nvme_init_non_mdts_limits()), then max_hw_discard_sectors is DMRSL converted to 512-byte sectors. Otherwise, if ONCS bit 2 (DSM supported) is set, it is UINT_MAX. If ONCS.DSM is clear, it is 0 and discard is disabled.
  • Maximum number of ranges: max_discard_segments = DMRL (Dataset Management Ranges Limit) if non-zero, else NVME_DSM_MAX_RANGES = 256.
  • Granularity: discard_granularity comes from NPDG/NPDGL and NPDA/NPDAL (preferred deallocate granularity and alignment) when the OPTPERF bits say they are valid, otherwise the logical block size. The block layer uses this to round discards, so a discard smaller than the granularity, or not aligned to it, may be trimmed or dropped on the host side before it is ever sent.

You can see all of this in sysfs: /sys/block/nvme0n1/queue/discard_max_bytes, discard_max_hw_bytes, discard_granularity, max_discard_segments, write_zeroes_max_bytes.

Building the DSM command: nvme_setup_discard()

drivers/nvme/host/core.c:nvme_setup_discard() (v7.2 line 840) does four things:

  1. It allocates a range buffer sized for all 256 ranges (sizeof(*range) * NVME_DSM_MAX_RANGES = 4096 bytes), even if only one range is used. The source comment says why: "Some devices do not consider the DSM 'Number of Ranges' field when determining how much data to DMA." This is a real device bug that the driver works around silently (see card D-32). If the allocation fails, it falls back to a per-controller discard_page.
  2. It fills one struct nvme_dsm_range per bio: cattr = 0 (context attributes), nlb = length in logical blocks, slba = starting LBA. Each descriptor is 16 bytes.
  3. It checks that the number of ranges built equals blk_rq_nr_discard_segments(). If not, it does WARN_ON_ONCE and fails the request with BLK_STS_IOERR. This is a host-side error you would see.
  4. It builds the 64-byte command: opcode nvme_cmd_dsm (09h), nr = segments − 1 (a 0's based count in CDW10), attributes = NVME_DSMGMT_AD (bit 2 of CDW11), and attaches the range buffer as a "special payload" (RQF_SPECIAL_PAYLOAD), so the PRP entries point at it.
/* Linux v7.2 drivers/nvme/host/core.c, nvme_setup_discard() (excerpt) */
	memset(cmnd, 0, sizeof(*cmnd));
	cmnd->dsm.opcode = nvme_cmd_dsm;
	cmnd->dsm.nsid = cpu_to_le32(ns->head->ns_id);
	cmnd->dsm.nr = cpu_to_le32(segments - 1);
	cmnd->dsm.attributes = cpu_to_le32(NVME_DSMGMT_AD);

	bvec_set_virt(&req->special_vec, range, alloc_size);
	req->rq_flags |= RQF_SPECIAL_PAYLOAD;

Note that Linux never sets IDR or IDW (Integral Dataset for Read/Write, bits 0 and 1). It sends only AD. Context attributes are always 0.

Write Zeroes and the DEAC bit: nvme_setup_write_zeroes()

REQ_OP_WRITE_ZEROES (from blkdev_issue_zeroout(), blkdiscard -z, fallocate zero-range, or filesystems) is handled by nvme_setup_write_zeroes() (v7.2 line 949):

  • If the controller has NVME_QUIRK_DEALLOCATE_ZEROES, the function simply calls nvme_setup_discard(): a "write zeroes" request is sent as a DSM deallocate. The quirk's comment says the controller "deterministically returns 0's on reads to logical blocks that deallocate was called on". In drivers/nvme/host/pci.c v7.2 this quirk is set for Intel device IDs 0953h (750/P3500/P3600/P3700) and 0A53h (P3520). With this quirk and ONCS.DSM set, max_write_zeroes_sectors becomes UINT_MAX.
  • Otherwise it builds a real Write Zeroes command (opcode 08h) with SLBA and NLB. It sets NVME_WZ_DEAC (bit 9 of the control word, so CDW12 bit 25) only if the request does not have REQ_NOUNMAP and the namespace has NVME_NS_DEAC.
  • NVME_NS_DEAC is set in nvme_update_ns_info_block() (v7.2 around line 2483) only when (dlfeat & 0x7) == 0x1 (deallocated blocks read as zeroes) and dlfeat bit 3 is set (Write Zeroes supports the Deallocate bit). The comment explains: the DEAC bit itself does not require zeroes, "it must be a no-op if reads from deallocated data do not return zeroes".
  • If PI is enabled, it also sets PRACT so the controller generates protection information.

Write Zeroes can also be turned off: nvme_init_non_mdts_limits() sets max_zeroes_sectors to 0 if ONCS bit 3 (Write Zeroes) is clear or the controller has NVME_QUIRK_DISABLE_WRITE_ZEROES ("Broken Write Zeroes"). Many entries in the v7.2 nvme_id_table carry that quirk, including the Intel 600P/P3100 (F1A5h) and the QEMU emulated controller (Intel ID 5845h). When Write Zeroes is off, the block layer falls back to writing real zero-filled buffers, which is slower but cannot fail silently in the same way.

💡 Senior insight: the quirk table is a list of drives that lied

Each entry in nvme_id_table is a drive that once failed silently or behaved against the spec, and someone found out the hard way. NVME_QUIRK_DEALLOCATE_ZEROES goes the other way: the drive guaranteed more than it said in Identify, so Linux trusts it more. When a new drive behaves strangely, read the quirk list first. If your drive's vendor already has entries, look at what they were for.

🔧 What Linux v7.2 does after the CQE

When the DSM completes with status 0, nvme_complete_rq() finishes the request with BLK_STS_OK and nvme_cleanup_cmd() frees the range buffer (or releases discard_page). Nothing checks the result. There is no read-back, no NUSE check, nothing. If the DSM fails, nvme_error_status() maps the status: Invalid Field / Invalid Opcode → BLK_STS_NOTSUPP, LBA Out of Range → BLK_STS_TARGET, others → BLK_STS_IOERR, and nvme_log_error() prints a rate-limited line such as nvme0n1: Dataset Management(0x9) @ LBA …, … blocks, … (sct 0x0 / sc 0x2). So you only get a log line when the drive refuses. A drive that accepts and then does nothing leaves no trace at all.

🏭 Implementation-dependent: the QEMU model skips ranges silently

The QEMU NVMe controller in the local snapshot (hw/nvme/ctrl.c:nvme_dsm_cb()) processes each range in turn. A range whose nlb is larger than n->dmrsl is skipped with only a trace event (pci_nvme_dsm_single_range_limit_exceeded, then goto next). A range that is out of bounds is also skipped (pci_nvme_err_invalid_lba_range, goto next). The command still completes with Success. QEMU also sets dlfeat = 0x9 (reads of deallocated blocks return zeroes, Write Zeroes supports DEAC) in nvme_ns_init(), and passes the discard to its block backend with blk_aio_pdiscard(). If that backend ignores discards (an option of the QEMU drive configuration, verify the default for your QEMU version), you get exactly the case in this chapter: DSM Success, DLFEAT promises zeroes, and a read-back returns old data. That makes QEMU a safe place to practise the proof procedure before using real hardware.

IV.1.5 What the PEX trace contains, and why it looks perfect

A PEX trace is a Teledyne LeCroy PCIe protocol-analyzer capture of the link between the root port (or switch) and the SSD. It records every TLP and DLLP that crosses that link, with timestamps, and the analyzer software decodes the NVMe structures inside the payloads: the 64-byte SQE in the completion data of the device's fetch, the DSM range list, the 16-byte CQE, and data buffers. For the trim of the last 5 GiB, this is everything the capture holds. Every packet is correct. A drive that deallocated and a drive that did nothing produce the same sequence, byte for byte, apart from timestamps.

Why it looks perfect. The analyzer's protocol checks (TLP format, ordering, completion timeouts, credit flow, NVMe field decoding) all pass, because nothing here breaks a rule. The SQE is well-formed, the range list is fetched with the right length, the CQE matches the CID, the phase bit is right, and the interrupt follows the CQE. NVMe has no status code that means "accepted but I will not deallocate". The FTL change, which is the thing that failed, happens in controller memory and never produces a TLP. Timing does not help either: a drive that only logs the request and unmaps later in the background (legal) completes as fast as a drive that does nothing.

What the trace does tell you. (a) The deallocate was sent, which rules out the host-side drops in D-04. (b) It carried the LBAs you expected; wrong LBAs point to host bugs such as partition offsets, dm-crypt, LVM or granularity rounding. (c) It was fetched completely and accepted. It cannot tell you (d): did the FTL change.

The proof comes from later commands whose results are visible as payloads. If you keep the analyzer running while you run the checks from section IV.1.7, the same PEX file can contain the proof. That is the best bug report you can hand a drive vendor: one capture with the promise, the trim and the broken result.

  7. The controller posts a Success CQE

    A 16-byte completion with status 0. This means the command was accepted and processed without an error the controller chose to report. It does not mean the blocks are now deallocated.

    Where: host memory, CQ3PCIe: Yes, device → host · MWr 16 B
    DW2: SQHD=0x12 SQID=3 DW3: CID=0x1047 P=1 Status=0x0000
  8. MSI-X interrupt; Linux completes the request

    The handler calls nvme_handle_cqe(), the request ends with BLK_STS_OK, the range buffer is freed, blkdiscard exits 0. No log line is printed.

    Where: CPU, nvme_irq → nvme_handle_cqePCIe: Yes · MWr 4 B to MSI-X address
    dmesg: (nothing) nvme error-log: (no new entry)
  9. Evidence queries: Identify Namespace, log pages, and Reads

    The host sends normal admin and I/O commands to measure the result: Identify Namespace (NUSE, DLFEAT, NSFEAT), Get Features 05h (DULBE), SMART log, Get LBA Status if supported, and Reads of sample LBAs from inside, at the edges of, and outside the range.

    Where: admin and I/O queuesPCIe: Yes · normal command traffic
    nvme id-ns /dev/nvme0n1 -H | grep -iE 'nuse|dlfeat|nsfeat' nvme read /dev/nvme0n1 -s 2000000 -c 0 -z 4096 -d out.bin
  10. Read-back data or a DULBE error comes back: this is the evidence

    The NAND→controller step is internal. The data (or error status) the controller returns is on the wire and in your buffer. Compare it with the DLFEAT promise: zeroes, FFh, an error 287h (DULBE enabled), or the old pattern.

    Where: host memory, read buffer / CQE statusPCIe: Yes · MWr data + CQE
    DLFEAT RB=001b and data = old pattern → PROVEN failure DLFEAT RB=000b and data = old pattern → allowed; use DULBE / NUSE instead

🐞 Decision procedure
  1. Did it leave the host? If there is no DSM/Write Zeroes in blktrace or the analyzer, the bug is host-side (D-04). Stop and fix the mount option, dm layer or discard_max_bytes.
  2. Were the ranges right? Decode the range list. If the LBAs are wrong, it is a partition/LVM/offset bug, not a drive bug.
  3. Was it rejected? A non-zero status shows in dmesg via nvme_log_error() and in nvme error-log. Not silent.
  4. What did the drive promise? nvme id-ns -H: DLFEAT RB, NSFEAT DULBE/THIN, NPDG/NPDA. nvme id-ctrl -H: ONCS, OACS.GLSS. NVM Identify Controller (nvme nvm-id-ctrl, if your nvme-cli has it): DMRL, DMRSL.
  5. Pick the strongest rung (1 > 2 > 3 > 4 > 5) the promises allow, and repeat with a known-good reference drive or the QEMU model as a control.
  6. Classify: promise broken (RB=001b, old data back) → D-01/D-03, a spec violation. Partial (edges or some ranges only) → D-02. No promise, accounting unchanged → "deallocate not effective", a quality or performance issue for the vendor, not a conformance failure.

IV.1.8 Worked example with numbers synthetic

Drive: 1.0 TB, one namespace, LBA format 4096 + 0 (lba_shift 12). Identify Namespace: NSZE = NCAP = 244,190,646. NSFEAT = 05h (THIN + DULBE). DLFEAT = 09h (RB 001b + Write Zeroes DEAC). NPDG = NPDA = 7 (0's based → 8 blocks = 32 KiB). NVM Identify Controller: DMRL = 0, DMRSL = 0.

LBAs written = 10 GiB / 4096 = 2,621,440 (LBA 0 … 2,621,439)
Range to deallocate = LBA 1,310,720 (0x140000), NLB = 1,310,720 (0x140000)
Granularity check: 1,310,720 mod 8 = 0 and 1,310,720 mod 8 = 0 → aligned
  • NUSE expected — before: ≥ 2,621,440 (plus any earlier data); after: before − 1,310,720, within one granule (8 blocks) per range edge.
  • DLFEAT 09h — reads of deallocated blocks must return 00h, and Linux will set DEAC on Write Zeroes for this namespace.

Observations on the "lying" drive: DSM completes in 11 µs with status 0. nvme id-ns NUSE before = 2,621,440, after = 2,621,440 (unchanged, even after 60 s idle). Reads of LBA 1,310,720, 2,000,000 and 2,621,439 return the original pattern. With DULBE enabled, reading LBA 2,000,000 returns Success, not 287h. Reads of LBA 2,621,440 (never written) do return 287h, which proves DULBE works and the drive tracks unwritten blocks. Conclusion: the drive promised zeroes (RB 001b) and returned old data. Its own allocation tracking (DULBE, NUSE) shows the range is still mapped. The deallocate was accepted and not performed. This is a conformance failure, card D-01, and the evidence package is: the trace (rungs 0), Identify before/after, read-back dumps, and the DULBE control read.

🧪 Lab IV.1 — Prove or disprove a deallocate on a spare drive CAUTION LAB
⚠️ Destroys data

Every write, discard and Set Features below acts on the whole namespace. Use a spare drive or a QEMU NVMe device with nothing mounted, and double-check the device name (nvme list, lsblk). Enabling DULBE makes reads of unwritten blocks fail for all users of the namespace. Turn it off again at the end. Never run this on a disk holding a filesystem you care about.

Objective. Write 10 GiB of verifiable data, deallocate the last 5 GiB, and collect at least three independent kinds of evidence about whether deallocation took effect.

Setup. Linux with nvme-cli, fio, util-linux (blkdiscard), optionally blktrace. Note the namespace's LBA size (nvme id-ns -H, "in use" LBA format). The example assumes 4096-byte LBAs; recompute LBA numbers for 512.

# 0. What does the drive promise?  (read-only)
DEV=/dev/nvme0n1; CTRL=/dev/nvme0
nvme id-ctrl $CTRL -H | grep -iE 'oncs|oacs|vwc'
nvme id-ns   $DEV  -H | grep -iE 'nsze|ncap|nuse|nsfeat|dlfeat|npdg|npda|in use'
cat /sys/block/nvme0n1/queue/{discard_max_bytes,discard_granularity,max_discard_segments,write_zeroes_max_bytes}
nvme get-feature $CTRL -f 0x05 -n 1 -H          # Error Recovery (TLER, DULBE)

# 1. Write 10 GiB of self-verifying data (header with offset + checksum per block)
fio --name=fill --filename=$DEV --rw=write --bs=128k --size=10G --direct=1 \
    --verify=crc32c --do_verify=0 --ioengine=libaio --iodepth=16
nvme id-ns $DEV | grep -i nuse            # record NUSE_1

# 2. Optional: start blktrace in another shell to capture the discard
#    blktrace -d $DEV -o - | blkparse -i - | grep ' D '

# 3. Deallocate the last 5 GiB (LBA 1310720, 1310720 blocks)
blkdiscard --offset 5GiB --length 5GiB $DEV ; echo "exit=$?"
sleep 30
nvme id-ns $DEV | grep -i nuse            # record NUSE_2

# 4. Read-back: inside, both edges, and just outside the range
for L in 1310719 1310720 2000000 2621439; do
  nvme read $DEV -s $L -c 0 -z 4096 -d /tmp/lba_$L.bin
  echo "LBA $L:"; od -A d -t x1 /tmp/lba_$L.bin | head -2
done

# 5. DULBE (only if NSFEAT bit 2 = 1): enable, read, disable
nvme set-feature $CTRL -f 0x05 -n 1 -v 0x10000
nvme read $DEV -s 2000000 -c 0 -z 4096 -d /dev/null   # expect status 0x287 if deallocated
nvme read $DEV -s 2621440 -c 0 -z 4096 -d /dev/null   # never written: control, expect 0x287
nvme set-feature $CTRL -f 0x05 -n 1 -v 0x0

# 6. fio verify of the first half must still pass (deallocate must not touch it)
fio --name=check --filename=$DEV --rw=read --bs=128k --size=5G --direct=1 \
    --verify=crc32c --verify_only --ioengine=libaio

Observations to record: DLFEAT RB, NSFEAT bits, NUSE_1 and NUSE_2, the first bytes of each read-back, DULBE statuses, the fio verify result for the first half, and (if captured) the discard line from blkparse with its sector and size.

Questions. (1) If LBA 1,310,719 (just outside) reads back as zeroes, what went wrong? (2) If NUSE does not change but read-backs are zero, is the drive failing? (3) Why is the never-written LBA a necessary control for step 5?

Solution

(1) The deallocate covered too much. Either the host rounded the range out (granularity or offset error, check blkparse) or the drive deallocated outside the range. Both are serious, see D-02. (2) Not necessarily. NUSE may be reported as NCAP on drives that do not track allocation, or update lazily. Zero read-back with RB=001b means the promise was kept. (3) It proves DULBE is really enabled and implemented. Without it, "Success" on LBA 2,000,000 could mean "DULBE is ignored" rather than "block still allocated".

Extension. Repeat with blkdiscard -z (Write Zeroes; with DLFEAT 09h Linux sets DEAC) and compare. Repeat in QEMU with a backend that ignores discards to reproduce a "lying" controller, and with one that honours them as a control.

IV.1.9 Common mistakes

  • Treating the Success CQE as proof. It shows acceptance. For DSM it is not even a promise of action.
  • Calling old data on read-back a failure when DLFEAT RB = 000b. That is allowed. Use DULBE or NUSE instead.
  • Testing through a filesystem. File extents are not device LBAs, discard may be deferred or not sent at all, and the filesystem may never read freed blocks again. Test on the raw namespace.
  • Ignoring granularity. Ranges not aligned to NPDA/NPDG may be partly deallocated by design. Test aligned and misaligned cases separately.
  • Measuring right after the CQE only. Background unmapping is legal for the internal space accounting. Check NUSE again after idle time. Read results must be correct immediately.
  • Leaving DULBE enabled. A filesystem reading never-written metadata areas will see I/O errors.

Quiz

  1. Which Identify Namespace field decides whether a read-back of old data after a deallocate is a spec violation?

    Answer

    DLFEAT bits 2:0 (read behaviour). 001b promises zeroes, 010b promises FFh, 000b promises nothing.

  2. Under what exact condition does Linux v7.2 set the DEAC bit in Write Zeroes?

    Answer

    When (dlfeat & 7) == 1 and dlfeat bit 3 is set (so NVME_NS_DEAC is set), and the request does not carry REQ_NOUNMAP. With NVME_QUIRK_DEALLOCATE_ZEROES it sends a DSM instead of Write Zeroes at all.

  3. Where in a PCIe trace do the LBAs of a deallocate appear?

    Answer

    Only in the CplD that returns the range list to the device's MRd at PRP1. They are not in the SQE.

  4. Why does nvme_setup_discard() always allocate 4096 bytes for ranges?

    Answer

    Some devices ignore the NR field and DMA-read the maximum of 256 ranges. A small buffer would make them read past its end (card D-32).

  5. Which status code does a DULBE-enabled read of a deallocated LBA return, and what does Linux turn it into?

    Answer

    287h "Deallocated or Unwritten Logical Block" (SCT 2h). nvme_error_status() maps it to BLK_STS_MEDIUM.

Interview questions

  1. Beginner Why can't a protocol analyzer show whether a trim worked?
    Model answerThe analyzer sees the PCIe link. The DSM command, its range list and the completion are on the link, but the FTL change is inside the controller. A drive that unmaps and one that does nothing produce identical traffic.
  2. Intermediate A customer says "fstrim says 400 GiB trimmed but performance never recovers." Walk through your investigation.
    Model answerFirst confirm discards reach the device (blktrace for D requests, tracepoint nvme_setup_cmd with attributes=4, dm layers' discard passthrough, discard_max_bytes). Then check promises (DLFEAT, NSFEAT, NPDG), then do a raw-device controlled test: write, deallocate, read-back per DLFEAT, DULBE probe, NUSE delta. Finally a performance A/B with a control. fstrim's number is the request size, not the effect.
  3. Senior When is "deallocate not performed" a conformance bug and when is it a quality issue?
    Model answerConformance: when an observable promise is broken: DLFEAT RB 001b/010b with old data returned, DULBE enabled and supported but a deallocated block reads Success, Write Zeroes (with or without DEAC) not returning zeroes. Quality: with RB 000b and no DULBE/NUSE tracking, nothing observable is promised, so a no-op deallocate only costs space and performance. That is a vendor-qualification problem, and you prove it with performance and write-amplification A/B tests.

Summary. A deallocate is visible on the wire only as a request and an acceptance. Whether it took effect is visible only through promised read behaviour (DLFEAT), errors you can turn on (DULBE), allocation accounting (NUSE, Get LBA Status 02h), or long-term performance. Rule out host-side drops first, then use the strongest evidence the drive supports. The next chapter turns each variant of this problem into an error card.
