IV.3 Durability and data-integrity failures: acknowledged but not true

These failures break the most basic contract of a storage device: what I wrote and was told is safe, I will read back unchanged, from the place I wrote it. Every card here is silent at the moment it happens. The drive returned Success for the Write, the Flush, or the Read. The damage shows up later, often far from the cause: a database refuses to start, a filesystem check finds orphaned blocks, a checksum mismatch appears months later. Field studies show these are not theoretical:

  • Power faults: Zheng, Tucek, Qin and Lillibridge (FAST '13) cut power to 15 commodity SSDs from five vendors over more than 3,000 fault-injection cycles. 13 of the 15 showed failures such as bit corruption, shorn writes, unserializable writes, metadata corruption and total device failure. The extended ACM TOCS 2016 version reports 14 of 17 SSDs from six vendors failing, including devices sold as enterprise-class.
  • Corruption in production: Bairavasundaram et al. (FAST '08) analysed corruption across 1.53 million disk drives in NetApp systems over 41 months and found more than 400,000 checksum mismatches, which were not independent within a disk or a system. That study is about hard disks, but its taxonomy (checksum mismatches, lost writes, misdirected writes) applies to any block device. Krioukov et al. (FAST '08, "Parity Lost and Parity Regained") analysed how lost and misdirected writes defeat RAID parity schemes.
  • Drives that ignore flushes: Rajimwale et al. (DSN 2011, "Coerced Cache Eviction and Discreet-Mode Journaling") built a file-system technique specifically for disks that do not honour cache flushes correctly.
  • Flash in the field: Schroeder, Lagisetty and Merchant (FAST '16, Google) found that 20–63% of drives saw at least one uncorrectable error within four years, and that raw bit error rate does not predict uncorrectable errors well. Meza et al. (SIGMETRICS '15, Facebook) found that SSD failure rates do not rise steadily with wear but go through distinct periods. Maneas et al. (FAST '20, 1.4 million enterprise SSDs at NetApp) studied factors including firmware versions.

These studies count detected errors. The silent ones in this chapter are, by definition, under-counted. They are only found where an end-to-end checksum exists.

D-08Flush ignored / volatile write cache that lies SilentNVMe command
In a PEX trace
Partly visible Flush SQE (OPC 00h) and its CQE are visible, so you can show the host flushed. A Flush that always completes in microseconds after many MiB of writes is a clue, not proof. With VWC=0 you see no Flushes at all. The data loss needs a power-cut ledger test. Detail: Flush SQE (OPC 00h, NSID) and its Success CQE. How long the Flush took is the only clue: a Flush after many MiB of cached writes that completes in microseconds every time is suspicious, but not proof (the drive may have PLP). With VWC = 0 you will see no Flush commands at all.
What happens
Either (a) Flush completes Success before the cached data is on NAND, or (b) the drive reports VWC = 0 (no volatile cache) while it really buffers writes in DRAM or SRAM without power-loss protection. In case (b) Linux never sends a Flush at all, because the queue is set up without a write cache.
How it shows up
Nothing until an unexpected power loss. Then data that fsync()/fdatasync() reported as durable is missing or old, and journals replay inconsistent states.
Spec basis
NVM Command Set Flush; Identify Controller VWC; Set/Get Features 06h (Volatile Write Cache, WCE bit 0). A completed Flush is a durability promise for all previously completed writes.
Typical causes
Firmware completing Flush early for benchmark scores; a flush that only drains the controller's front-end buffer, not the FTL metadata; a missing VWC report; capacitors (PLP) degraded or absent on a SKU that shares firmware with a PLP model. A widely reported informal 2022 test (by Russ Bishop, covered in the press) claimed that two of four consumer NVMe SSDs lost flushed data on power cuts uncertain (not peer-reviewed; details not independently verified here).
How to detect / prove
Power-cut test with a host-side ledger (see Lab IV.3): write records, fsync/Flush, log the acknowledged sequence number to a different machine, cut power at random points, power up, check that every acknowledged record is present. Any acknowledged-but-missing record after Flush = proof. Check nvme id-ctrl -H VWC and nvme get-feature -f 6. On Linux, /sys/block/nvme0n1/queue/write_cache shows "write back" or "write through". "write through" on a drive you suspect caches is case (b).
Linux v7.2 source
core.c:nvme_update_ns_info_block() sets BLK_FEAT_WRITE_CACHE | BLK_FEAT_FUA only if ctrl->vwc & NVME_CTRL_VWC_PRESENT and not info->no_vwc; core.c:nvme_setup_flush() builds the Flush; core.c:nvme_ns_info_from_id_cs_indep() reads NVME_NS_VWC_NOT_PRESENT.
Test to provoke it
EXPERT: controlled power interruption (programmable PSU or a PCIe slot power switch / interposer) during a write+fsync workload, hundreds of cycles, varied timing relative to the Flush completion. Compare with the same drive with WCE disabled (nvme set-feature -f 6 -v 0) as a control.
D-09FUA not honoured SilentNVMe command
In a PEX trace
Partly visible The FUA bit (Write CDW12 bit 30) and the completion latency are visible. FUA writes as fast as cached writes on a drive without PLP is a clue. Proof needs a power-cut test. Detail: Write SQE CDW12 bit 30 = 1. FUA writes that complete exactly as fast as non-FUA writes on a drive without PLP are a strong hint.
What happens
A Write with FUA = 1 completes while its data is still only in volatile cache. Filesystems and databases use FUA for journal commit blocks (Linux turns REQ_FUA into NVME_RW_FUA), so a lost FUA write loses the commit record of a transaction the application already saw as complete.
How it shows up
Only after power loss: the last committed transactions are missing even though the commit write completed.
Spec basis
Read/Write command Force Unit Access bit (CDW12 bit 30). Durable at completion, independent of the write cache.
Typical causes
Firmware treating FUA as a hint, or making the data durable but not the mapping-table update that points to it (after power-up the LBA maps to the old page).
How to detect / prove
Power-cut ledger test using only FUA writes and no Flush (use a small program that writes with O_DIRECT|O_DSYNC; Linux can then mark the write REQ_FUA when the queue advertises FUA. Confirm with the nvme_setup_cmd tracepoint that the control field has bit 14 set before trusting the test). Any acknowledged FUA write missing after power-up = proof.
Linux v7.2 source
core.c:nvme_setup_rw(): if (req->cmd_flags & REQ_FUA) control |= NVME_RW_FUA;. FUA is advertised to the block layer only with a write cache (BLK_FEAT_FUA in nvme_update_ns_info_block()). Otherwise the block layer never sets it.
Test to provoke it
EXPERT: as D-08 but with O_DSYNC writes. Compare the FUA write latency distribution with WCE on and off: if they are the same and the drive has no PLP, investigate.
D-10Acknowledged writes lost on power loss (PLP failure) SilentDevice internal
In a PEX trace
Not visible Not visible: the trace before the cut is normal, and after power-up you see only a normal (possibly slower) initialization. Use a power-cut ledger and the SMART Unsafe Shutdowns counter. Detail: Nothing before the cut. After power-up: normal initialization, possibly a longer time to CSTS.RDY = 1 (the drive rebuilding its map), then Reads returning old or wrong data.
What happens
On a drive that claims power-loss protection, or with the cache disabled, writes that completed before the power cut are missing or old after power-up. Also includes losing data written long before the cut, when an FTL metadata update in flight corrupts the mapping of older data. Zheng et al. classify this as metadata corruption.
How it shows up
Nothing at power-up in most cases. Sometimes the SMART log Unsafe Shutdowns counter increments (it should on every unexpected power loss), and sometimes Media and Data Integrity Errors rise if the drive notices. A drive that comes back with no namespace, read-only mode, or a Critical Warning bit set is the "total device failure" end of the range.
Spec basis
No single NVMe field promises PLP. Durability rests on Flush/FUA and the VWC report. SMART / Health log: Unsafe Shutdowns, Critical Warning bits (including "volatile memory backup device has failed"), Media and Data Integrity Errors.
Typical causes
Hold-up capacitors aged or out of tolerance; firmware not finishing the dump within the hold-up time; mapping table checkpoint corruption; power cut during an internal operation (GC, wear levelling) that moved live data.
How to detect / prove
Power-cut ledger test with verification of all previously written data, not just the latest records, because older data can be lost too. nvme smart-log before and after each cycle: Unsafe Shutdowns must increase by exactly one per cut (if it does not, the drive's power-loss detection itself is broken). Check the critical warning bits.
Linux v7.2 source
Linux has no visibility. On orderly shutdown it sets CC.SHN and waits for CSTS.SHST (see the shutdown timeout parameter shutdown_timeout in core.c). An unexpected power loss skips this entirely.
Test to provoke it
EXPERT: hundreds to thousands of randomized power cuts (the FAST '13 study used more than 3,000 cycles across devices) with mixed sequential, random and flush-heavy workloads, including cuts during idle (background GC). Record temperature, as hold-up energy varies with it.
D-11Torn (shorn) write: atomicity broken SilentData integrity
In a PEX trace
Not visible Not visible: the write traffic is normal and the tear happens inside the drive at the cut. After power-up, Read payloads show mixed old/new blocks only if you read them back and know the expected content. Use record checksums. Detail: Normal Writes. The torn state is visible only in later Read payloads.
What happens
A write that should be atomic persists only partly: some logical blocks new, some old, or even part of one logical block new (a "shorn write" in Zheng et al.'s terms). Databases that rely on atomic 4 KiB or 16 KiB page writes (to skip double-write buffers) then see half-written pages.
How it shows up
After power loss (or after an error/reset during the write), reads return a mix. Page checksums in the application fail, but the drive reports nothing.
Spec basis
AWUN/AWUPF (Identify Controller, 0's based in logical blocks), NAWUN/NAWUPF/NABSPF (Identify Namespace, valid when NSFEAT bit 1 is set). A write ≤ NAWUPF that does not cross an NABSPF boundary must be all-or-nothing even across power failure. A single logical block is always expected to be atomic.
Typical causes
NAND page programming interrupted; firmware splitting a host write across two internal units with separate mapping updates; advertising NAWUPF larger than really guaranteed.
How to detect / prove
Write records of exactly NAWUPF size, aligned to NABSPF, each carrying a sequence number and checksum in every logical block (so a mix is detectable). After a power cut every record must be entirely old or entirely new. Also test records of NAWUPF + 1 blocks to confirm the boundary (tearing is allowed there).
Linux v7.2 source
core.c:nvme_configure_atomic_write() uses NAWUPF (and NABSPF as the boundary) only when NSFEAT has the atomics bit. Otherwise it logs "AWUPF ignored, only NAWUPF accepted" and uses one logical block. nvme_update_disk_info() limits physical_block_size to the atomic size, because "Linux filesystems assume writing a single physical block is an atomic operation".
Test to provoke it
EXPERT: power-cut loop with atomic-size records. Also CAUTION: controller reset (nvme reset) or an FLR in the middle of large writes, then verify the records, because tearing can also follow resets, not only power loss.
D-12Unserializable persistence: write order not preserved across a power cut SilentData integrity
In a PEX trace
Partly visible The trace proves the host side of the contract (Writes completed, Flush SQE/CQE, then the commit Write), which is half the evidence. The out-of-order persistence itself is not visible. Detail: Correct ordering on the host side: Writes completed, Flush SQE/CQE, then the commit Write. Capturing the trace proves the host kept its side of the contract.
What happens
After power loss, a later write (acknowledged and followed by Flush) is present, but an earlier one, also flushed before it, is missing. The persisted state matches no order in which the host could have seen the writes complete. Zheng et al. call these "unserializable writes".
How it shows up
Silent. Journaling and log-structured software break, because they assume "if the commit record is there, the data blocks before the flush are there too".
Spec basis
NVMe does not order independent commands, so writes in flight together may persist in any order. But Flush is a barrier: everything completed before it must be durable when it completes. A state with post-flush data present and pre-flush data missing violates the Flush contract.
Typical causes
Flush implemented per internal channel or die rather than globally. Mapping journal entries written out of order. The flush-ignoring behaviour of D-08 combined with a cache that evicts out of order.
How to detect / prove
Ledger with epochs: epoch N data → Flush → commit record N → Flush → epoch N+1. After a cut, if commit record N is present, every block of epoch N must verify. Note the distinction: missing data from an epoch whose Flush had not completed is legal.
Linux v7.2 source
Block layer flush machinery generates REQ_OP_FLUSH → core.c:nvme_setup_flush(). Linux does not wait for anything beyond the Flush CQE.
Test to provoke it
EXPERT: power-cut loop with the epoch ledger. Run it with high queue depth and many concurrent streams so the drive's internal parallelism is busy.
D-13Lost write (dropped write) without any power event SilentData integrity
In a PEX trace
Visible If the Write and the later Read are both captured: the device fetched the new data (MRd/CplD of the write buffer), posted Success, and later returned the old version in the Read data payload. That is proof on the wire. Detail: Write SQE, data MRd/CplD (the device fetched the new data), Success CQE. The later Read returns the old payload. The trace proves the device received the new data, which pins the fault inside the device.
What happens
A Write completes Success, no power event or reset follows, and a later Read returns the previous content of the block. The write was dropped (never programmed, or programmed but the mapping not updated).
How it shows up
Silent. A per-block checksum stored with the block cannot catch it, because the old block is self-consistent. Only a checksum or version stored elsewhere (in the parent pointer, as in ZFS/Btrfs, or in an application index) catches it. This is the case Krioukov et al. analysed for RAID.
Spec basis
Write command semantics. With PI Type 1, the reference tag only checks the LBA, not the version, so PI does not detect lost writes either.
Typical causes
FTL mapping-update race (two writes to the same LBA in flight, the older one's mapping update applied last); write-cache bugs on overlapping writes; firmware error recovery that retries a failed program and silently gives up.
How to detect / prove
Write a version number into each block and keep the expected version in host memory (fio --verify with --verify_header-style headers, or a custom tool that stores per-LBA generation counters). Read back after overwrites. Old version = lost write. Stress overlapping writes to the same LBA at high queue depth.
Linux v7.2 source
No detection in the driver. Linux allows overlapping writes in flight (the block layer does not serialize them), so an application that issues them must not depend on order.
Test to provoke it
CAUTION: fio --rw=randwrite --bs=4k --size=1G --iodepth=64 --verify=crc32c --verify_backlog=1024 with --norandommap (allows overwrites) plus a custom generation-counter checker. Long runs (hours), near-full drive to force GC.
D-14Misdirected write: data written to the wrong LBA SilentData integrity
In a PEX trace
Partly visible The Write SQE SLBA shows where the host asked to write. The misdirection is visible only if you also capture a Read of the wrong destination returning that data (with an LBA header inside the payload). Detail: The Write SQE's SLBA shows what the host asked for. If the SLBA is already wrong, it is a host bug. If it is right and the data shows up elsewhere, it is the device.
What happens
The data for LBA X lands at LBA Y. Two corruptions result: X keeps old data (a lost write), and Y is overwritten with data that belongs elsewhere. Zheng et al. describe "flying writes" after power faults, data appearing at an unexpected location, which is the power-fault version of this.
How it shows up
Silent at the device. Detectable by checksums that embed the block's own address (a header containing the LBA, or PI reference tag with Type 1/2).
Spec basis
Write semantics; End-to-end Data Protection: with PI Type 1 the Reference Tag must match the lower bits of the LBA and the controller checks it when PRCHK includes the reference tag. A misdirected write then fails with Reference Tag Check Error (status 284h) on the read, or at write time if the controller re-checks.
Typical causes
FTL mapping corruption; namespace offset bugs (multi-namespace); bit flips in internal address paths without parity; on the host side, wrong SLBA from a stacking-driver bug.
How to detect / prove
Write blocks whose content includes their own LBA (fio verify headers include the offset). On read, check that the header offset equals the LBA read. A block containing another block's header = misdirected write, and the header tells you the intended destination.
Linux v7.2 source
core.c:nvme_set_ref_tag() fills the reference tag for PI Type 1/2 (16-bit and 64-bit guard formats); core.c:nvme_error_status() maps NVME_SC_REFTAG_CHECK to BLK_STS_PROTECTION.
Test to provoke it
CAUTION: full-drive fio write with --verify=crc32c (headers carry the offset), then a full read-verify, repeated after GC-heavy random overwrites and after power cuts (EXPERT). On a PI-formatted namespace, also confirm that a deliberately wrong SLBA via passthrough is caught.
D-15Stale data returned on read SilentData integrity
In a PEX trace
Visible With timestamps: the Write CQE MWr is earlier than the Read SQ doorbell MWr, and the Read data payload carries the old version. An analyzer capture gives exactly that ordering proof. Detail: Write CQE MWr timestamp earlier than the Read's SQ doorbell MWr timestamp, and the Read data payload carries the old version. An analyzer with timestamps gives exactly that proof.
What happens
A Read returns an older version of a block although a newer Write to that LBA has completed (no power event). Unlike D-13, the new data may be on media: a later Read, after a cache eviction or a reset, returns the correct new data. So the error comes and goes.
How it shows up
Silent, intermittent, and very hard to reproduce. Applications see "my write disappeared, then came back".
Spec basis
A Read that starts after a Write's completion must return that Write's data (or newer). NVMe does not order commands that are in flight at the same time, so a Read issued before the Write completed may legally return either version.
Typical causes
Read cache / read-ahead buffer not invalidated by a write to the same LBA; multi-queue race in firmware (write on one SQ, read on another); with multi-path/multi-controller subsystems, a second controller serving from a stale cache.
How to detect / prove
Write–complete–read loops with version numbers on the same LBAs from different queues (different CPUs) and, for multi-controller subsystems, via different controllers. Record the completion time of the write and the submission time of the read to show the read started after the write completed. That ordering is what turns "odd" into "proven".
Linux v7.2 source
Requests are spread over per-CPU hardware queues by blk-mq (block/blk-mq.c), so cross-queue read-after-write is normal Linux traffic.
Test to provoke it
CAUTION: small hot set (for example 64 LBAs), one thread per CPU, each doing write(version)→wait for completion→read from another CPU→compare. Run with the drive both near-empty and full.
D-16Wrong data / bit flips returned without an error SilentData integrity
In a PEX trace
Partly visible The Read data payload with the wrong bytes is visible, and LCRC protected the hop, so the trace proves the device sent them. Recognising them as wrong needs the expected data (a captured earlier Write or a checksum). Detail: The Read data MWr payload already contains the wrong bytes, which proves the device sent them (PCIe LCRC protected the hop). If the trace shows correct data and the buffer is wrong, the fault is in host memory or an IOMMU/DMA mapping problem.
What happens
A Read completes Success but the data differs from what was written by a few bits or by a whole chunk. The drive's ECC either miscorrected (decoded to a wrong but valid codeword), or the corruption happened outside the ECC-protected path: controller SRAM/DRAM without parity, a firmware buffer handling bug, or the PCIe/host side (very unlikely to be undetected on PCIe because of LCRC, but possible in host memory).
How it shows up
Silent at the drive: no media error status, no SMART Media Errors increment. Only an end-to-end checksum catches it.
Spec basis
Read semantics. SMART/Health log Media and Data Integrity Errors counts only errors the controller detected. End-to-end Data Protection (PI guard CRC) covers the whole path from host buffer to media and back when PRCHK guard is enabled.
Typical causes
ECC miscorrection at high raw bit error rates; DRAM bit flips in the drive's buffer without ECC; firmware bugs in buffer management or in compression/encryption engines; host memory errors (rule out with ECC RAM and EDAC counters).
How to detect / prove
Checksummed reads: fio --verify=crc32c or sha256, a checksumming filesystem (ZFS/Btrfs scrub), PI with guard checking. Keep the bad buffer and compare it bit by bit with the expected one: a few scattered bit flips point to media/ECC, a block-sized chunk of other valid data points to D-14/D-15. Check host memory EDAC counters to rule out the host.
Linux v7.2 source
With a PI namespace and CONFIG_BLK_DEV_INTEGRITY, core.c:nvme_init_integrity() registers the profile so the block layer generates and verifies guard tags. Without it, Linux sets PRACT and the controller handles PI alone (core.c:nvme_setup_rw()), so host-to-controller transfer is not covered.
Test to provoke it
CAUTION: long fio verify runs at high temperature, after many P/E cycles (endurance tests), and with data retention bakes (write, power off, store warm, read-verify). Compare the detected-error counters with the verify failures: every verify failure without a matching Media Errors increment is a silent corruption.
D-17Retries mask failing media DegradedDevice + host
In a PEX trace
Partly visible Device-internal retries are visible only as longer gaps between SQE fetch and data MWr. Host retries are visible as an error CQE (no DNR) followed by re-submission of the same SLBA/NLB. The media wear itself is not visible. Detail: Device-internal retries: only longer gaps between the Read SQE fetch and the data MWr. Host retries: the same command (same SLBA/NLB, new CID or same tag) re-submitted after an error CQE without DNR.
What happens
Data is returned correctly, but only after many internal read retries (read-level shifts, soft decoding) or after the host retried a failed command. Every layer reports success, while the media is wearing out and latency tails grow. Eventually it becomes an uncorrectable error. Schroeder et al. found uncorrectable errors in 20–63% of drives within four years, and that raw bit error rate is a poor predictor of them, so you cannot rely on a single "health" number.
How it shows up
Higher p99/p99.9 read latency on specific LBA ranges. SMART Percentage Used and Available Spare change slowly. On the host side, Linux retries a non-DNR error up to max_retries (default 5) times. A request that eventually succeeds produces no error to the application, and only the per-namespace command_retries_count in sysfs counts it.
Spec basis
CQE status Do Not Retry (DNR) bit and Command Retry Delay (CRD, with CRDT1–3 in Identify Controller). SMART/Health log Media and Data Integrity Errors, Available Spare, Percentage Used. Vendor or OCP logs may expose internal retry and ECC-correction counts impl.
Typical causes
Wear-out, read disturb, retention loss (data written long ago or at temperature extremes); host-visible retries also come from transient link or controller issues.
How to detect / prove
Latency heat map per LBA region (fio with --write_lat_log/--log_avg_msec, or bpftrace on block completion latency by sector). Read the per-path namespace counters in the diag sysfs group, …/diag/command_retries_count and …/diag/command_error_count (Linux v7.2 sysfs.c, nvme_ns_diag_attr_group; with native multipath they appear on the hidden path disk such as nvme0c0n1, not on the head nvme0n1). Track SMART over time. Compare Verify-command latency (opcode 0Ch, reads media without transfer) across regions.
Linux v7.2 source
core.c:nvme_decide_disposition() (RETRY unless DNR set, blk_noretry_request(), or retries >= nvme_max_retries); core.c:nvme_retry_req() (honours CRD via ctrl->crdt[], increments ns->retries); sysfs.c:command_retries_count_show(); module parameter nvme_core.max_retries.
Test to provoke it
SAFE to observe: periodic full-drive read scans with latency logging. CAUTION: use the kernel's NVMe fault injection (drivers/nvme/host/fault_inject.c, debugfs fault_inject) to inject retryable status codes and confirm that only the retry counter shows them.
🧪 Lab IV.3 — Power-cut durability ledger EXPERT LAB
⚠️ Hardware risk

Cutting power to a running SSD can corrupt or kill it, and switching slot power can harm the host if done wrongly. Use a spare drive, a dedicated test host, and a power-switching method designed for it (a programmable supply feeding a powered riser or interposer, or a controllable slot-power switch). Never cut the host's own power while the system disk is the device under test.

Objective. Prove or disprove D-08 to D-12 for one drive.

Setup. Device under test (DUT) on a separately powered riser. The test host writes; a second machine (the ledger) receives acknowledgements over the network, because the ledger must survive the cut.

loop forever:
    rec = {seq, epoch, lba, crc32c(payload), payload}
    write(rec) to LBA; on completion:
        if mode == FUA:   use O_DSYNC write              # D-09
        if mode == FLUSH: every K records: fdatasync()   # D-08 / D-12
        send(seq, lba, "durable") to ledger  ONLY after the durability point
    at random time T: ledger tells power controller: CUT
power on, wait for CSTS.RDY, read nvme smart-log (Unsafe Shutdowns must be +1)
verify:
    for every seq the ledger marked durable: record present, crc ok      # D-08/09/10
    every record either fully old or fully new (atomic-size records)    # D-11
    if commit(N) present then all of epoch N present                     # D-12
    no record found at an LBA other than its own                         # D-14

Observations. For each cycle: time from last durability point to cut, number of missing durable records, torn records, Unsafe Shutdowns delta, time to CSTS.RDY.

Solution / interpretation

Zero missing durable records over hundreds of cycles is a pass for that workload. One missing record after a completed Flush or FUA is a proven durability failure. Report it with the ledger extract, the power-cut timestamp and the SMART log. Torn records only above NAWUPF are allowed. Unsafe Shutdowns not incrementing is a separate bug in power-loss detection.

Extension. Repeat with WCE disabled (Feature 06h = 0) as a control, at high and low ambient temperature, and with the drive nearly full so GC runs during the cut.
