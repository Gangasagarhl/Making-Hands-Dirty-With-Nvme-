Mathematics Appendix

R12 Mathematics Appendix

R12.1 Why this chapter exists

Engineers argue less when they compute more. Most performance disagreements end the moment someone writes the right formula with the right variables. Most configuration bugs (wrong doorbell offset, wrong NLB, wrong PRP count) are arithmetic. This appendix is the reference.

  • Compute effective PCIe bandwidth for any generation and width.
  • Estimate TLP efficiency for a given MPS and header size.
  • Use Little's law to relate queue depth, IOPS and latency.
  • Compute percentiles and reason about tail amplification.
  • Compute write amplification, TBW and DWPD.
  • Compute LBA/byte conversions, doorbell offsets, PRP entry counts, MDTS limits and tag budgets.

Prerequisites: PCIe physical layer (Part IV), performance engineering (Part XXVIII).

🧒 Kid analogy

If a bus holds 40 children and comes every 10 minutes, then at most 240 children per hour can ride it. You don't need to watch the bus all day to know that — you can work it out. Formulas let you know limits without watching.

🧩 Where the analogy breaks

Real systems have many "buses" in series (software, link, controller, media), and the slowest stage — which may change with workload — sets the limit. Formulas give bounds; measurements tell you which bound you hit.

R12.2 Bandwidth per PCIe generation

BW_lane (bytes/s) = R × E / 8 ; BW_link = BW_lane × W
  • R — raw signalling rate per lane in GT/s (2.5, 5, 8, 16, 32, 64; Gen7 128 version).
  • E — encoding efficiency: 8/10 for Gen1–2 (8b/10b), 128/130 for Gen3–5 (128b/130b). Gen6 uses PAM4 signalling (64 GT/s means 32 GBaud × 2 bits) and FLIT mode; for a TLP-byte bound use the fraction of each 256-byte flit available to TLPs (236/256) derived.
  • W — link width in lanes (1, 2, 4, 8, 16).
  • /8 — bits to bytes.

Plain English: each lane carries R billion symbols per second; encoding spends some of them on overhead; multiply by lanes. This is per direction — PCIe is full duplex.

⚠️ Warning

Marketing "GB/s" numbers for PCIe are often bidirectional totals or raw rates. Always ask: per direction? before or after encoding? before or after protocol overhead?

R12.3 TLP efficiency

η_TLP = P / (P + O) ; O ≈ F + H + LCRC (+ ECRC)
  • P — payload bytes per TLP (≤ MPS for writes/completions).
  • H — header bytes: 12 (3 DW) or 16 (4 DW, 64-bit address).
  • F — framing + sequence number: Gen1–2: STP(1) + seq(2) + END(1) = 4; Gen3–5: 4-byte STP token that includes the sequence number derived.
  • LCRC — 4 bytes; ECRC — 4 bytes when TD=1.

Plain English: each packet pays a fixed tax; bigger packets amortise it. For non-FLIT links O ≈ 20 bytes (3-DW) or 24 bytes (4-DW).

Worked example: Gen4 x4 read-data stream at MPS 256 with 64-bit addresses: 7.88 × 0.914 ≈ 7.20 GB/s. Subtract a few percent for Ack and UpdateFC DLLPs (8 bytes each including framing derived), SKP ordered sets and the opposite-direction completions; realistic sequential-read ceilings land around 6.8–7.1 GB/s impl.

R12.4 IOPS and throughput

Throughput = IOPS × S
  • IOPS — completed I/Os per second.
  • S — bytes per I/O.

Worked example: 1 000 000 IOPS × 4096 B = 4.096 GB/s. Conversely, a link-bound ceiling of 7.2 GB/s supports at most 7.2e9 / 4096 ≈ 1.76 M IOPS of 4 KiB reads — beyond that the link, not the media, is the limit derived.

R12.5 Little's law and queue depth

L = λ × W ⇒ QD = IOPS × latency
  • L — average number of items in the system (outstanding I/Os = effective queue depth).
  • λ — average arrival (= completion, in steady state) rate, I/Os per second.
  • W — average time each item spends in the system (latency), seconds.

Plain English: if 400 000 requests finish every second and each takes 80 µs, then on average 32 are in flight. It holds for any stable system regardless of arrival distribution (Little, 1961).

Worked examples:

  • QD 32, mean latency 80 µs ⇒ IOPS = 32 / 80e-6 = 400 000.
  • Target 1 M IOPS with 100 µs latency ⇒ need QD = 1e6 × 1e-4 = 100 outstanding across all queues.
  • Saturation: if IOPS plateaus at 800 k, increasing QD from 64 to 128 raises latency from 80 µs to 160 µs — the "more QD only adds latency" regime.
💡 Senior insight

Little's law applies to every subsystem boundary: to the tags in flight on the link, to commands inside the controller, to requests in the block layer. Applying it per boundary tells you where requests are actually waiting.

R12.6 Utilisation and queueing delay

U = λ × S_t ; W ≈ S_t / (1 − U) (M/M/1 model)
  • U — utilisation of a single server (0…1).
  • S_t — mean service time of the server.
  • W — mean time in system including queueing.

Plain English: as a resource approaches 100% busy, waiting time explodes. At 50% busy you wait about 2× the service time; at 90%, 10×; at 99%, 100×.

Worked example: a die with 60 µs read service time receiving 12 000 reads/s: U = 12 000 × 60e-6 = 0.72 ⇒ W ≈ 60 / 0.28 ≈ 214 µs. M/M/1 assumes exponential arrivals/service — real SSDs deviate; use it for intuition, not prediction conceptual.

R12.7 Latency percentiles and tail amplification

p-th percentile (nearest-rank) = x_(k), k = ⌈ p/100 × N ⌉
  • x_(k) — k-th smallest of N sorted samples.
  • p — percentile (e.g. 99).
  • N — number of samples.

Worked example: 20 sorted latencies (µs): 70, 71, 72, 72, 73, 74, 75, 75, 76, 77, 78, 79, 80, 82, 85, 88, 95, 110, 240, 1900. Median (p50): k = 10 → 77 µs. p90: k = 18 → 110 µs. p95: k = 19 → 240 µs. p99: k = ⌈19.8⌉ = 20 → 1900 µs. The mean is 177 µs — dominated by one outlier, which is why means are poor latency summaries. Tools differ in interpolation method impl; with 20 samples, p99 is just "the maximum".

P(at least one slow) = 1 − (1 − q)^n
  • q — probability a single I/O exceeds the threshold (e.g. 0.01 for p99).
  • n — number of independent I/Os a request fans out to.

Worked example: a database query touching 100 pages on independent I/Os, each with a 1% chance of exceeding p99: 1 − 0.99^100 ≈ 63.4% of queries see at least one p99-slow I/O. Tail latency at the device becomes typical latency for the application (Dean and Barroso, "The Tail at Scale").

R12.8 Write amplification

WAF = NAND bytes written / host bytes written
  • NAND bytes written — including GC relocation, metadata, and parity; available only via vendor telemetry/logs impl.
  • host bytes written — from SMART Data Units Written (each unit = 1000 × 512 bytes).

Worked example: host wrote 100 TB; NAND programmed 250 TB ⇒ WAF = 2.5. Sequential workloads on a fresh drive approach 1.0; small random overwrites at high fill levels with little over-provisioning can exceed 3–5 impl.

TBW ≈ C × PE / WAF (approximate endurance)
  • C — raw capacity (or user capacity as a simplification).
  • PE — rated program/erase cycles of the NAND.

Worked example: 3.84 TB, PE 3000, WAF 2.5 ⇒ ≈ 4608 TB written. Vendors specify TBW under defined workloads (JEDEC JESD218/JESD219 methodology); your WAF may differ derived.

R12.9 DWPD and TBW

TBW = DWPD × C × 365 × Y ; DWPD = TBW / (C × 365 × Y)
  • DWPD — drive writes per day (full-capacity writes per day over the warranty).
  • C — user capacity (TB).
  • Y — warranty years.

Worked example: 3.84 TB, 1 DWPD, 5 years ⇒ 3.84 × 365 × 5 = 7008 TB ≈ 7.0 PB TBW. A 600 TBW, 1 TB, 5-year client drive ⇒ DWPD = 600 / (1 × 1825) ≈ 0.33.

SMART check: Percentage Used (byte 5 of the Health log) is a vendor estimate of life consumed; can exceed 100 verified.

R12.10 LBA ↔ bytes

byte_offset = SLBA × B ; bytes = (NLB + 1) × B ; capacity = NSZE × B
  • SLBA — Starting LBA (CDW10–11, 64-bit).
  • NLB — Number of Logical Blocks, zero-based (CDW12[15:0]).
  • B — LBA data size = 2^LBADS from the active LBA format (e.g. LBADS 9 → 512, 12 → 4096).
  • NSZE — Namespace Size in LBAs (Identify Namespace).

Worked examples: 4 KiB read on 512 B format: NLB = 4096/512 − 1 = 7. On 4 KiB format: NLB = 0. Byte offset 1 GiB on 512 B format: SLBA = 2^30 / 512 = 2 097 152 = 20_0000h. Max per command by NLB field alone: 65 536 blocks (32 MiB at 512 B) — but MDTS usually limits lower.

MaxTransfer = 2^MDTS × 2^(12 + CAP.MPSMIN)
  • MDTS — Identify Controller byte 77; 0 means no limit reported.
  • CAP.MPSMIN — minimum memory page size exponent.

Worked example: MDTS = 5, MPSMIN = 0 ⇒ 32 × 4096 = 128 KiB per command.

R12.12 Ring arithmetic

entries_pending = (tail − head + N) mod N ; full ⇔ (tail + 1) mod N = head
  • N — queue size in entries (QSIZE + 1, since QSIZE is zero-based).

Worked example: N = 64, head = 60, tail = 3 ⇒ pending = (3 − 60 + 64) mod 64 = 7. Memory: SQ = N × 64 B (64 entries = 4 KiB); CQ = N × 16 B (64 entries = 1 KiB). Queue size limit: N ≤ CAP.MQES + 1.

R12.13 PRP entry count

pages = ⌈ (o + L) / PS ⌉ ; list entries n = pages − 1 (when pages > 2)
list pages = 1 if n ≤ E ; else ⌈ (n − 1) / (E − 1) ⌉ , E = PS / 8
  • o — byte offset of the buffer start within its first page (PRP1 offset).
  • L — transfer length in bytes.
  • PS — memory page size = 2^(12 + CC.MPS).
  • E — PRP entries per list page (8 bytes each); when a list page is not the last, its final entry chains to the next list page.

R12.14 TLP counts per I/O

N_dataTLP ≥ ⌈ L / MPS ⌉ (aligned) ; N_MRd ≥ ⌈ L / MRRS ⌉ ; N_CplD per MRd ≥ ⌈ MRRS / C ⌉
  • L — bytes; MPS — Max Payload Size; MRRS — Max Read Request Size; C — completer's completion chunk size (≥ RCB, ≤ MPS) impl.

Worked example: a 4 KiB NVMe Read at MPS 256 with PRP1 only: 1 doorbell + 1 SQE MRd + 1 CplD + 16 data MWr + 1 CQE MWr + 1 MSI-X + 1 CQ-head doorbell = 22 TLPs. A 4 KiB Write at MRRS 512 and 256-byte completions: 1 + 1 + 1 + 8 MRd + 16 CplD + 1 + 1 + 1 = 30 TLPs.

R12.15 Tag budget (outstanding reads)

N_tags ≥ BW × RTT / MRRS
  • BW — target read bandwidth (B/s).
  • RTT — request-to-completion round trip (s).
  • MRRS — bytes per read request.

Plain English: to keep the pipe full you need enough reads in flight to cover the round trip (bandwidth-delay product).

Worked examples: Gen4 x4, 7.88 GB/s, RTT 1 µs, MRRS 512 ⇒ ≈ 15.4 tags — 8-bit tags (256) are plenty. Gen5 x16, 63 GB/s, RTT 2 µs, MRRS 512 ⇒ ≈ 246 tags — at the 8-bit limit, which is why 10-bit tags (Gen4+, optional) and 14-bit tags in Gen6 FLIT mode exist derived.

R12.16 Bit-error arithmetic

errors/s ≈ R_bits × W × BER
  • R_bits — bit rate per lane (bits/s); W — lanes; BER — bit error ratio.

Worked example: 16 Gb/s per lane, x4, BER 1e-12 ⇒ 0.064 errors/s ≈ one every 16 s across the link. Each detected error typically costs a NAK + replay (a correctable error). The PCIe specification's BER target for Gen3–5 is 10⁻¹² verified; Gen6 relies on FEC because the raw PAM4 BER is far higher version.

R12.17 Timeouts and timing fields

T_ready_max = CAP.TO × 500 ms

Worked example: CAP.TO = 3Ch (60) ⇒ host should wait up to 30 s for CSTS.RDY to change after toggling CC.EN.

Interrupt rate ≈ IOPS_per_vector / A (with aggregation threshold A, if the timer does not fire first)

Worked example: 200 k IOPS on one vector with threshold 8 ⇒ ≈ 25 k interrupts/s; each completion waits up to the aggregation time when traffic is light.

R12.18 Power and energy (bonus)

Energy per I/O = P_active / IOPS

Worked example: 8 W at 800 k IOPS ⇒ 10 µJ per I/O. At QD1, 15 k IOPS and 5 W ⇒ 333 µJ per I/O: efficiency depends strongly on load derived.

R12.19 Common mistakes

  • Using GT/s as GB/s; forgetting encoding; forgetting per-direction.
  • Forgetting NLB and QSIZE are zero-based.
  • Applying Little's law to a non-steady system (warm-up, bursts).
  • Averaging percentiles across servers (p99 of p99s is not a p99).
  • Mixing TB (10¹²) and TiB (2⁴⁰): 1 TB ≈ 0.909 TiB.
🐞 Debugging connection

When measured throughput is far below the formula ceiling, compute the intermediate bounds: link (R12.2–3), tags (R12.15), Little's law (R12.5) and media. The first bound you hit is the bottleneck hypothesis.

🧪 Lab R12.1 — Check the formulas against your SSD SAFE
# Large sequential read (read-only) to measure link-bound throughput
sudo fio --name=seq --filename=/dev/nvme0n1 --rw=read --bs=1m --iodepth=32 \
    --ioengine=io_uring --direct=1 --runtime=20 --time_based --readonly
# Small random reads at several depths to test Little's law
for qd in 1 4 16 64; do
  sudo fio --name=r$qd --filename=/dev/nvme0n1 --rw=randread --bs=4k --iodepth=$qd \
      --ioengine=io_uring --direct=1 --runtime=15 --time_based --readonly \
      --output-format=terse | awk -F';' '{print "'"$qd"'", $8, $40}'
done
⚠️ Warning

Use --readonly and --rw=read/randread only. Pointing a write job at a raw device destroys data. The terse field positions shown are illustrative; check your fio version's terse format documentation before relying on column numbers version.

Questions: Does IOPS × mean latency ≈ QD? How close is sequential throughput to the R12.3 estimate for your LnkSta?

Solution

Little's law should hold within a few percent at steady state (QD here is per job; fio's own latency includes software overhead consistently). Sequential throughput usually lands at 85–92% of the encoded link rate when link-bound; well below that indicates media/controller limits or a down-trained link.

Quiz

  1. Gen3 x8 per-direction encoded bandwidth?

    Answer

    8 × 128/130 / 8 × 8 = 7.88 GB/s.

  2. QD 16, 200 k IOPS. Mean latency?

    Answer

    16 / 200 000 = 80 µs.

  3. CQ head doorbell for queue 5, DSTRD 0?

    Answer

    1000h + 11 × 4 = 102Ch.

  4. 12 KiB transfer starting at offset 0 with 4 KiB pages: PRP layout?

    Answer

    3 pages: PRP1 = page 0, PRP2 → list with 2 entries.

Interview questions

  1. Senior Our Gen5 x4 SSD reads at 9 GB/s. Is that link-bound?
    Model answer

    Ceiling ≈ 15.75 GB/s encoded, ≈ 14.3 GB/s after MPS-256 overhead. 9 GB/s is ~63% — not link-bound; look at controller/media parallelism, QD, tag limits or MRRS-related fetch efficiency (for writes), and confirm LnkSta shows 32 GT/s x4.

  2. Principal Derive why 10-bit tags became necessary.
    Model answer

    Bandwidth-delay product: at Gen5 x16 (~63 GB/s) with µs-scale RTT and 512-byte reads, required outstanding requests approach or exceed 256 — the 8-bit tag space. Longer RTT through switches/retimers makes it worse.

R12.20 Summary

Bandwidth = rate × encoding × lanes; efficiency = payload / (payload + overhead); QD = IOPS × latency; tails amplify with fan-out; endurance = capacity × cycles / WAF; NLB and QSIZE are zero-based; doorbells are 1000h + index × stride. Next: example data appendix.

Status field layout in DW3: bit 16 P; bits 24:17 SC; bits 27:25 SCT; bits 29:28 CRD (Command Retry Delay, 1.4+ version); bit 30 M (More); bit 31 DNR. Decoding recipe: status = DW3 >> 17; SC = status & FFh; SCT = (status >> 8) & 7; DNR = DW3 bit 31.

# Python 3 — decode a CQE from 16 hex bytes
import struct
def decode_cqe(hexstr):
    b = bytes.fromhex(hexstr.replace(" ", ""))
    dw0, dw1, dw2, dw3 = struct.unpack("<4I", b)
    return {"result": dw0, "sqhd": dw2 & 0xFFFF, "sqid": dw2 >> 16,
            "cid": dw3 & 0xFFFF, "p": (dw3 >> 16) & 1,
            "sc": (dw3 >> 17) & 0xFF, "sct": (dw3 >> 25) & 7,
            "crd": (dw3 >> 28) & 3, "more": (dw3 >> 30) & 1, "dnr": dw3 >> 31}
print(decode_cqe("00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 09 00 03 00 13 00 03 05"))
# {'result': 0, 'sqhd': 9, 'sqid': 3, 'cid': 19, 'p': 1, 'sc': 129, 'sct': 2, 'crd': 0, 'more': 0, 'dnr': 0}

# Python 3 — decode the first DW of a non-FLIT TLP header
FMT_TYPE = {0x00:"MRd32",0x20:"MRd64",0x40:"MWr32",0x60:"MWr64",0x0A:"Cpl",0x4A:"CplD",
            0x04:"CfgRd0",0x44:"CfgWr0",0x05:"CfgRd1",0x45:"CfgWr1"}
def decode_dw0(b):            # b = 4 header bytes, transmission order
    ft = b[0]; tc = (b[1] >> 4) & 7
    td, ep = b[2] >> 7, (b[2] >> 6) & 1
    attr = ((b[1] >> 2) & 1) << 2 | ((b[2] >> 4) & 3)
    length = ((b[2] & 3) << 8) | b[3]
    if (ft & 0x1F) in (0x10,0x11,0x12,0x13,0x14,0x15,0x16,0x17): name = "Msg"   # routing variants
    else: name = FMT_TYPE.get(ft, "fmt/type %02X" % ft)
    return name, tc, td, ep, attr, (length or 1024)        # length 0 encodes 1024 DW
print(decode_dw0(bytes.fromhex("60000040")))   # ('MWr64', 0, 0, 0, 0, 64)

R13.12 Common mistakes when decoding

  • Reading TLP header bytes as little-endian DWs (they are shown MSB-first in transmission order).
  • Reading SQE DWs as big-endian (they are little-endian in memory).
  • Forgetting that Length 0 in a TLP means 1024 DW.
  • Decoding opcodes without knowing the queue type (admin vs I/O).
  • Treating Byte Count in a CplD as "this TLP's bytes" — it is the remaining byte count including this TLP.

Solution

Class code must be 01 08 02 for an NVMe controller. Your capability offsets will differ from this appendix — the list order is implementation-dependent. Bus/Device/Function and BAR addresses depend on your platform's enumeration.

Quiz

  1. DW3 = 0005_0021h. Decode.

    Answer

    CID 0021h; P = 1; status = 0005h >> 1 = 2 → SC 02h (Invalid Field in Command), SCT 0, DNR 0.

  2. TLP DW0 = 20 00 00 00. Length?

    Answer

    MRd64 with Length field 0 = 1024 DW (4096 bytes).

  3. Message Control 0x001F with bit 15 clear. How many vectors and are they enabled?

    Answer

    32 vectors (1Fh + 1); MSI-X not enabled.

Interview questions

  1. Intermediate Walk me through decoding a CplD header and how you match it to its request.
    Model answer

    Fmt/Type 4Ah; Length; Completer ID; status; Byte Count (remaining); Requester ID + Tag form the key matching the original MRd; Lower Address gives the byte address of the first payload byte's low 7 bits — consistent with the request address and earlier partial completions.
