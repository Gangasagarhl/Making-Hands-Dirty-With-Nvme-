PART XXVIII

Performance Engineering

Performance is where protocol knowledge meets arithmetic. This Part teaches the vocabulary (latency, IOPS, throughput, queue depth, outstanding commands, CPU overhead, PCIe/controller/NAND bandwidth, tail latency), the equations that tie them together (above all Little's law), the latency budget of a single I/O, benchmarking discipline with fio, the causality rules that keep performance claims honest, and finally the exact PCIe bandwidth arithmetic for Gen3 through Gen6 links. All numbers labelled synthetic are teaching values, not measurements of any product.

85 Performance Fundamentals: Latency, IOPS, Throughput and Their Limits

85.1 Why this chapter exists

"This SSD does 1.5 million IOPS" is meaningless without block size, read/write mix, queue depth, number of jobs, latency percentiles, preconditioning state and platform. Engineers who cannot decompose a performance number into its resources cannot tell whether a result is a device limit, a link limit, a CPU limit, or an artefact of the test.

85.2 Learning objectives (deep-dive module 53)

  • Beginner: define latency, IOPS, throughput, queue depth, and convert between IOPS and bandwidth.
  • Intermediate: explain why sequential and random workloads behave differently and why queue depth matters.
  • Advanced: identify the bottleneck resource from a set of measurements (link, controller, NAND, CPU, queueing).
  • Senior: explain why high IOPS can coexist with bad latency and why high bandwidth can hide latency problems; defend claims with causality evidence.
  • Trace-analysis: derive per-phase latency and link utilization from a PCIe trace.
  • Debugging: map a performance symptom to Failure Library entries F25–F27.
  • Validation: design repeatable benchmarks with defined percentiles and tolerance bands.

85.3 Story and analogy

🧒 Kid analogy — the pizza kitchen

A pizza shop gets phone orders. Latency is how long you wait for your pizza. IOPS is how many pizzas leave per minute. Throughput is how many kilograms of pizza leave per minute (big pizzas = more kilograms for the same count). Queue depth is how many orders are in the kitchen at once. With one order at a time, the ovens sit idle while the cook rolls dough; with ten orders at once, every oven is busy — more pizzas per minute. But with a hundred orders at once, the ovens are already full, so new orders just wait on the counter: pizzas per minute stop growing, while your personal wait keeps growing.

🧩 Where the analogy breaks

Represents: concurrency, service time, queueing, saturation. Hides: an SSD has many parallel "ovens" (NAND dies/planes) whose availability depends on where the data lives; writes are absorbed by caches and later cost background work (garbage collection); the delivery road (PCIe) has its own capacity and overhead per trip; the phone operator (CPU) can be the bottleneck. Misleading when: you assume every order takes the same time — storage service times have long tails (read retries, GC, power-state exits).

85.5 Core equations

Throughput = IOPS × BlockSize
  • Throughput — payload bytes per second (B/s)
  • IOPS — completed I/O operations per second
  • BlockSize — bytes per I/O
L = λ · W (Little's law — Chapter 86)
  • L — average number of outstanding I/Os
  • λ — average completion rate (IOPS) in steady state
  • W — average latency (s)
IOPSmax ≤ min( Nparallel / S , BWlink,eff / bytesper IO on link , Cores × IOPSper core , device limit )
  • Nparallel/S — internal parallel units divided by service time per I/O (media-bound ceiling)
  • BWlink,eff — effective link payload bandwidth; bytesper IO on link includes TLP and NVMe overhead (Ch 90)
  • IOPSper core — CPU-bound ceiling per core for the software stack
Utilization ρ = λ / μ ; for a simple single-server queue (M/M/1, illustrative) W = 1 / (μ − λ)
  • μ — service rate capacity (IOPS the resource can do flat out)
  • ρ — fraction of capacity in use; as ρ → 1, waiting time grows without bound. Real SSDs are many-server systems with non-exponential service times; the formula illustrates the shape, not exact values conceptual

85.8 Why queue depth matters

At QD1, a device with dozens of independent NAND dies uses one at a time: IOPS ≈ 1/W. Raising QD lets independent commands overlap, so IOPS rises roughly linearly — until a resource saturates. Past the knee, more QD adds only queueing delay: IOPS flat, latency rising in proportion (Little's law). The knee is the operating point you usually want.

85.9 Why high IOPS can still have bad latency — and high bandwidth can hide latency problems

  • High IOPS, bad latency: IOPS is a rate; latency is per request. At saturation (QD 512 above), IOPS is at its maximum while every request waits behind hundreds of others. Also, averages hide tails: 99 % fast requests and 1 % at 2 ms produce a fine mean and IOPS, but a database waiting on many reads per transaction hits the slow 1 % constantly.
  • High bandwidth hiding latency: large sequential transfers with deep queues keep the link full even if each command takes milliseconds; GB/s looks perfect. The same device may be terrible for small synchronous I/O (fsync-heavy logs). Bandwidth tests also amortize per-command problems (a slow doorbell path, a power-state exit) that dominate QD1 latency.
💡 Senior insight

Always report three numbers together: the rate (IOPS or GB/s), the concurrency (outstanding I/Os), and the latency distribution (p50/p99/p99.9). Any two let you check the third with Little's law; a mismatch means the measurement is wrong or the system is not in steady state.

85.10 Percentiles: computing and reading the tail

The model below draws 200,000 synthetic read latencies (~80 µs typical, 1 % delayed by 0.5–2 ms, 0.05 % by 20 ms) and computes percentiles; it also contains the Little's law helper and the latency budget used in Chapter 87.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Performance models (SIMULATION): Little's law, percentiles, latency budget.
All numbers are SYNTHETIC teaching values, not measurements of any product."""
import random, statistics

def littles(L=None, lam=None, W=None):
    """L = lambda * W. Give any two, get the third (units: ops, ops/s, s)."""
    if L is None:   return lam * W
    if lam is None: return L / W
    return L / lam

def percentile(sorted_vals, p):
    k = (len(sorted_vals) - 1) * p / 100
    lo, hi = int(k), min(int(k) + 1, len(sorted_vals) - 1)
    return sorted_vals[lo] + (sorted_vals[hi] - sorted_vals[lo]) * (k - lo)

def synthetic_latencies(n=200_000, seed=7):
    """Mostly ~80 us reads, 1% hit a 'GC-like' 2 ms stall, 0.05% hit 20 ms."""
    r = random.Random(seed)
    out = []
    for _ in range(n):
        x = r.gauss(80, 8)
        u = r.random()
        if u < 0.0005:   x += 20_000
        elif u < 0.0105: x += r.uniform(500, 2_000)
        out.append(max(x, 20))
    return sorted(out)

BUDGET = [  # (component, microseconds) - SYNTHETIC 4 KiB random read, QD1
    ("application + syscall", 1.5), ("block layer / blk-mq", 1.0),
    ("NVMe driver: build SQE, ring doorbell", 0.5), ("doorbell MWr reaches device", 0.5),
    ("SQE fetch (MRd + CplD round trip)", 1.0), ("controller FW: decode, FTL lookup", 3.0),
    ("NAND read (tR) + ECC decode", 60.0), ("data DMA 4 KiB over Gen4 x4", 0.6),
    ("CQE write + MSI-X", 0.5), ("interrupt -> completion -> wake app", 4.0)]

def show_budget(budget):
    tot = sum(v for _, v in budget)
    for name, v in sorted(budget, key=lambda x: -x[1]):
        print(f"  {name:40s} {v:6.1f} us  {100*v/tot:5.1f}%")
    print(f"  {'TOTAL':40s} {tot:6.1f} us -> QD1 ceiling {1e6/tot:,.0f} IOPS")
    return tot

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("Little: QD32 @ 80us ->", f"{littles(L=32, W=80e-6):,.0f} IOPS")
    print("Little: 1M IOPS @ 100us needs L =", littles(lam=1e6, W=100e-6))
    print("Little: 500k IOPS at QD256 -> W =", f"{littles(L=256, lam=500e3)*1e6:.0f} us")
    lat = synthetic_latencies()
    print(f"mean {statistics.fmean(lat):.1f}  p50 {percentile(lat,50):.1f}  p95 {percentile(lat,95):.1f}"
          f"  p99 {percentile(lat,99):.1f}  p99.9 {percentile(lat,99.9):.1f}  p99.99 {percentile(lat,99.99):.1f} us")
    print("Budget A (TLC-like media):"); show_budget(BUDGET)
    fast = [(n, 8.0 if n.startswith("NAND") else v) for n, v in BUDGET]
    print("Budget B (media 8 us, e.g. low-latency media/cache hit):"); show_budget(fast)
    poll = [(n, 1.0 if n.startswith("interrupt") else v) for n, v in fast]
    print("Budget C (B + polled completion):"); show_budget(poll)

86 Little's Law and Queue Depth

86.1 Why this chapter exists

Little's law is the single most useful equation in storage performance. It is exact for long-run averages of any stable system — no assumptions about distributions — and it turns three hard-to-reason-about quantities into one line of arithmetic.

  • State and apply L = λ·W with correct units.
  • Compute the concurrency needed for a target IOPS and latency.
  • Use the law to sanity-check benchmark output and detect broken measurements.
  • Apply it inside the PCIe layer: outstanding read requests (tags) needed to fill a link.
🧒 Kid analogy — the water slide

If 2 kids per minute go down a slide (λ) and each ride takes 3 minutes from top of the stairs to the splash (W), then on average 6 kids are on the stairs-and-slide at any moment (L = 2 × 3). Want more kids per minute without making the slide faster? You must allow more kids on it at once.

🧩 Where the analogy breaks

The law holds for averages over long, stable periods. It does not say anything about percentiles, and it does not hold over a short window where the queue is growing or draining (e.g. the first second of a benchmark, or during a GC storm).

86.2 The law

L = λ · W
  • L — mean number of I/Os in the system (outstanding: submitted, not completed)
  • λ — mean throughput of completions (IOPS), equal to arrival rate in steady state
  • W — mean time each I/O spends in the system (latency at the same measurement boundary as L)
📘 Boundary rule

L and W must be measured at the same boundary. If W is fio's completion latency (clat, from submission to completion), L is the number in flight between those points. If W is the device-side D2C time from blktrace, L is the number dispatched to the device. Mixing boundaries produces nonsense.

86.4 Little's law inside PCIe: outstanding read requests

For writes, the SSD pulls data with MRds. To sustain a payload bandwidth B with read-completion round-trip time R and request size MRRS:

Noutstanding MRd = B × R / MRRS
  • B — target pull bandwidth (B/s)
  • R — MRd → last CplD round-trip time (s)
  • MRRS — bytes per read request

Example derived: Gen4 x4 ≈ 7.06 GB/s, R = 1 µs, MRRS = 512 B → N ≈ 13.8 outstanding requests; at R = 2 µs (a busy root complex or a switch in the path) → ≈27.6. At Gen5 x4 with R = 2 µs: ≈55 outstanding. This is why tag space (8-bit, 10-bit tags) and MRRS matter for write throughput at higher generations — the device must have enough tags and internal buffering to keep that many requests in flight.

87 The Latency Budget and Tail Latency

87.1 Why this chapter exists

A latency number without a breakdown cannot be improved. The latency budget assigns every microsecond of one I/O to a component — software, driver, queue, PCIe, controller, media, completion, interrupt — so that you know which one to attack and what improvement is even possible.

  • Identify the dominant component; change one component and recompute.
  • Explain where tail latency comes from in each component.
