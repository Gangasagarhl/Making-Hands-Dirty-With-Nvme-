A PCIe transmitter may only send a packet when it knows the receiver has room for it. This chapter teaches the credit system that enforces that rule — six credit types, their initialisation, infinite credits, UpdateFC — and shows how credits quietly cap the throughput of a fast NVMe SSD long before any error appears.

Why this chapter exists

In Chapter 15 the Data Link Layer made the link reliable: every TLP arrives intact, in order, exactly once. That is necessary but not sufficient. A receiver can only accept a Transaction Layer Packet (TLP) if it has buffer space to put it in. If a PCI Express (PCIe) receiver simply dropped TLPs when its buffers were full, the Ack/Nak machinery would have to retry them, wasting bandwidth and adding latency. PCIe instead uses credit-based flow control: the receiver tells the transmitter in advance how much buffer space it has, and the transmitter never sends more than that.

Flow control is where a lot of "mystery" performance problems live. A Gen4 x4 NVM Express (NVMe) SSD that reaches only 5 GB/s of its 7 GB/s, with zero errors in every log, is very often credit-limited: the Root Port is returning credits too slowly, or advertising too few, or the device is not issuing enough read requests to cover the latency. Nothing is broken; the pipe is simply not full. Without the mental model in this chapter, such problems are invisible.

Learning objectives

  • Explain why PCIe uses credits rather than drop-and-retry, and why credits are per link (hop-by-hop) and per Virtual Channel (VC).

  • Calculate the credits any TLP consumes.
  • Describe flow-control initialisation with InitFC1/InitFC2 DLLPs, and the meaning of an advertised value of zero (infinite credits).

The story

A bakery sends trays of buns to a café down the street. The café has three kinds of shelves: one for bread deliveries that need no reply, one for order forms that need a reply, and one for replies coming back. Every morning the café gives the baker a handful of tickets for each shelf: "You may send me 8 trays and 128 buns on the bread shelf." The baker hands over one ticket with every tray. When the café sells buns and frees a shelf, a runner brings the tickets back. If the baker runs out of tickets, the baker waits — no matter how many buns are ready. Nothing is ever thrown away, because nothing is ever sent without a place to put it.

A container port and a rail yard. The rail yard has fixed numbers of wagon slots (one per container, regardless of size) and a fixed length of track (measured in 16-metre sections). It issues the port a quota of both, separately for three categories of cargo: freight that needs no reply, booking requests, and confirmations. The port may only dispatch a container when it holds a wagon slot and enough track length for it. As the yard unloads, it telegraphs a running total — "since this morning I have freed 1,052 slots and 16,500 metres" — and the port subtracts what it has used to know what is left.

Where the analogy breaks. (1) Real yards can overflow and spill; a correct PCIe receiver can never overflow, because the transmitter's arithmetic makes it impossible — an overflow is a protocol error. (2) The tickets in PCIe are not physical tokens; they are counters on both sides that must stay consistent. Lose a running-total message and the next one corrects everything; lose a physical ticket and it is gone. (3) The yard's three categories are independent shelves; in PCIe the categories also interact through ordering rules — posted cargo must be able to overtake blocked requests, or the system can deadlock. (4) The analogy has one port and one yard; a real PCIe path has credits on every link, and a slow last hop backs up every link before it.

Beginner explanation

Every PCIe receiver has buffers for incoming TLPs. Those buffers are divided by the type of traffic, because the three kinds of TLP behave differently:

  • Posted requests — Memory Writes (MWr) and Messages — need no answer.
  • Non-Posted requests — Memory Reads (MRd), configuration reads and writes, I/O, AtomicOps — expect a completion.
  • Completions — Cpl and CplD — answer non-posted requests.

Each category has separate space for headers and for data. That gives six counters. The receiver tells the transmitter, during link start-up, how many headers and how many 16-byte data units it can hold in each category. Those are credits. Each TLP spends one header credit plus enough data credits to cover its payload. When the receiver finishes with a TLP and frees its buffer space, it tells the transmitter with an UpdateFC DLLP, and the credits can be spent again.

A receiver can also say "infinite": "I will always have room for this type; don't count." Endpoints such as NVMe SSDs do this for completions, because they only ever receive completions for reads they themselves issued — so they can reserve the space before issuing the read.

The important consequences for an NVMe engineer:

  1. Credits are per link. Each hop has its own credits. A switch receives with its credits and sends with the next link's credits.
  2. When a transmitter runs out of credits it simply waits. There is no error, no log, no retry. This is backpressure.
  3. Throughput is limited by credits ÷ time-to-return-credits. Too few credits or too slow a return, and the link idles even though it is healthy.

Engineering explanation

Why credits, not drop-and-retry

PCIe inherited the PCI programming model, in which writes are fire-and-forget and reads are expected to complete. A drop-and-retry design would make every receiver-busy event cost a Nak and a replay round trip, and worse, it would interact badly with ordering: a dropped posted write followed by a delivered later write would violate producer–consumer ordering until the retry arrived. Credit-based flow control guarantees that a TLP sent is a TLP accepted, so the only reason to retransmit is corruption (handled by the Data Link Layer) and the receiver's ordering logic sees TLPs in their original order.

Counters on both sides

For each credit type, the specification describes the bookkeeping with a small set of counters. On the transmitter:

  • CREDITS_CONSUMED — total credits of this type consumed by TLPs sent since initialisation (modulo the field size).
  • CREDIT_LIMIT — the most recent value received in an InitFC or UpdateFC DLLP for this type: the total credits the receiver has ever made available.

On the receiver:

  • CREDITS_ALLOCATED — total credits the receiver has made available (initial value plus everything freed since). This is what UpdateFC DLLPs carry.
  • CREDITS_RECEIVED — optional; total credits consumed by TLPs received, used to detect a transmitter that overruns its limit (Receiver Overflow).

The transmitter may send a TLP only if:

(CREDIT_LIMIT − (CREDITS_CONSUMED + REQ)) mod 2n ≤ 2n / 2

The modular form exists because the counters wrap. In plain words: "the TLP fits if the credits I'd have used after sending it do not exceed the limit". Both the header test and the data test must pass. Normative The test applies separately to each credit type and each VC; the exact wording is in the flow-control section of the PCI Express Base Specification.

Why UpdateFC carries a running total

An UpdateFC DLLP carries the receiver's CREDITS_ALLOCATED — an absolute, wrapping counter — not "here are 16 more credits". If an UpdateFC is lost to a DLLP CRC error, the next one carries a larger total and the transmitter's CREDIT_LIMIT jumps straight to the correct value. No retransmission is needed and the two sides can never drift apart. That is why flow-control DLLPs, like Acks, are safe to drop.

Initialisation

Flow control is initialised by the Data Link Layer in DL_Init, before any TLP can be sent (Chapter 15):

  1. FC_INIT1. Each side sends InitFC1-P, InitFC1-NP and InitFC1-Cpl for VC0, in that order, repeatedly, each carrying its initial header and data credits for that category. It records the peer's values as they arrive.
  2. FC_INIT2. Once it has the peer's values for all three, it sends InitFC2-P, InitFC2-NP, InitFC2-Cpl (same values). This tells the peer "I have your numbers".
  3. Done. Receipt of InitFC2, UpdateFC or a TLP from the peer completes initialisation; the link is DL_Active and TLPs may flow.

VC0 is initialised automatically by hardware. Other VCs are initialised when software enables them through the Virtual Channel capability — rare on NVMe systems, which nearly always use VC0 only.

Infinite credits

An initial advertisement of zero in the HdrFC or DataFC field means infinite credits for that type: the transmitter never checks it. After advertising infinite, the receiver does not send UpdateFC values for that type (or sends zero). The rule most relevant to us: an Endpoint must advertise infinite Completion credits (CplH and CplD). Uncertain The specification also sets conditions under which a Root Complex advertises infinite completion credits, and minimum initial values for every finite type (for example, posted data credits at least sufficient for one maximum-size payload). Confirm the exact table "Minimum Initial Flow Control Advertisements" in your revision.

Why must an Endpoint accept every completion? Because a completion can only come back for a request the Endpoint itself issued. The Endpoint knows how much data it asked for, so it must reserve buffer space before issuing the read. If an Endpoint could backpressure completions, then completions waiting in a switch could block posted writes behind them (completions and posted requests share paths), and the fabric could deadlock. Making completion acceptance unconditional at the requester removes a whole class of deadlocks.

The Lab SSD's limit on how much it can read from host memory is not a credit it receives — it is its own completion buffer. With MRRS = 512 bytes and, say, 64 outstanding reads, it must have 32 KiB of completion buffer reserved. Its tag allocator (Chapter 13) and its completion buffer size together set its maximum read concurrency. The NPH credits the Root Port advertises set how many reads can be in the Root Port's queue at once.

Scaled Flow Control

The classic fields are 8 bits for header credits (up to 127 outstanding, since only half the space is usable) and 12 bits for data credits (up to 2047 × 16 bytes ≈ 32 KiB). At 16 GT/s and beyond, with long latencies through switches and retimers, that can be too few. PCIe 4.0 introduced Scaled Flow Control: the HdrScale and DataScale fields in FC DLLPs multiply the meaning of the credit fields, negotiated through the Data Link Feature exchange. Version-dependent Scaled Flow Control is required at 16.0 GT/s and above Uncertain — confirm the requirement and scale factors in your revision.

UpdateFC timing

The receiver sends UpdateFC when it frees buffer space. Two rules shape when: it must send an UpdateFC promptly when credits for a type had been insufficient for a maximum-size TLP and have now become sufficient (so a stalled transmitter is released quickly), and it must send UpdateFCs periodically for every finite type even without change, as a keep-alive against lost DLLPs. The specification gives a recommended maximum interval. Implementation-dependent Within those rules, how eagerly a receiver returns credits — after every TLP, after every N bytes, only when the buffer drains — is a design choice, and it directly affects throughput.

Backpressure and propagation

When a receiver stops freeing buffers (because, for example, the Root Complex's memory controller is congested), it stops returning credits. The transmitter on that link exhausts its credits and stops. If that transmitter is a switch port, the switch's ingress buffers fill, it stops returning credits on its upstream link, and so on back to the original sender. Backpressure travels hop by hop toward the source at the speed of buffer filling. This is flow control working correctly; the question is always why the final receiver is slow.

Starvation, and why posted traffic must never wait for non-posted

Flow control is per category so that one category's congestion does not block another. The ordering rules of Chapter 14 require that posted requests be able to pass non-posted requests and completions in the cases that matter for deadlock avoidance. Consider a switch whose NPH credits on an outgoing link are exhausted. If a posted MWr behind a stuck MRd could not move, and the completion that would unstick the MRd depended on that MWr being delivered (a producer–consumer pattern), the system would deadlock. Separate credit pools plus the passing rules prevent this. In implementation terms, a transmitter must not let a TLP that is blocked for lack of credits in one category block TLPs in another category that the ordering rules allow to pass it.

Credit starvation is the common, non-deadlock case: one type is chronically short. Typical NVMe examples:

  • PD starvation at the Root Port during large reads: the SSD's MWr stream of read data is limited by how fast the Root Complex drains writes into memory.
  • NPH starvation at the Root Port during large writes: the SSD wants many MRds outstanding to fetch write data, but the Root Port only advertises a handful of NPH credits.
  • Posted credits at the device: a device with very few PH credits can throttle the host's doorbell and MSI-X-table writes, raising submission latency under heavy multi-queue load.

The ladder

The café gives the baker tickets for trays and buns on each shelf. No ticket, no delivery. Tickets come back when shelves empty.

Each receiver advertises header and data credits per category (P, NP, Cpl). Transmitters spend credits per TLP and wait when out. UpdateFC DLLPs return credits as buffers free.

Each port has receive FIFOs/RAMs partitioned by category and header/data, counters for allocated and consumed credits, an arbiter that checks credits before scheduling a TLP, and DLLP logic that emits UpdateFC as buffers drain.

The PCI Express Base Specification's flow-control section defines the credit types, units (one header; 16 bytes), the gating rule, initialisation via InitFC1/InitFC2, infinite advertisement (0), minimum advertisements, Endpoint completion-credit rules, Scaled Flow Control and UpdateFC requirements.

On the wire: InitFC1/InitFC2 DLLPs at link-up, then UpdateFC-P/NP/Cpl DLLPs interleaved with TLPs in the opposite direction to the TLPs whose credits they return.

An analyzer shows HdrFC/DataFC values; good analyzers compute available credits per type over time. A starved link shows the transmitter idle while one type sits at zero available until the next UpdateFC arrives.

Throughput below expectation with no errors → compute credit-limited bandwidth from the advertised credits and measured loop time; if it matches the observed ceiling, the bottleneck is credit return (receiver draining), not the link or the device.

Hardware view

In silicon, credits are the visible face of receive buffers. A Root Port designer chooses how many headers and how much data RAM to provision for each category, for each port, knowing that many ports share a die area budget. Typical design trade-offs Implementation-dependent:

  • Posted data gets the most space in Root Ports, because devices write large amounts of data (NVMe reads, network receive) toward memory.
  • Non-posted headers limit how many device reads can be queued at the Root Complex; the data for those reads comes from memory and is returned as completions, so NPD can be small.
  • Completion space in an Endpoint is sized by the device's own maximum outstanding read bytes.
  • Shared pools: some designs share one RAM between categories and advertise conservatively; newer revisions add explicit shared-credit mechanisms for some modes Version-dependent.

The credit loop is a hardware latency chain: TLP serialisation, receiver pipeline, residence in the buffer until the next stage (memory controller, IOMMU, switch crossbar) takes it, UpdateFC scheduling (sometimes batched), DLLP serialisation and transmitter pipeline. Each retimer adds a few tens of nanoseconds in each direction; each switch adds its own full loop on each link.

On the device side, an NVMe controller has an internal "PCIe application interface" between the PCIe core and the NVMe engine. Many cores expose credit availability on that interface, so the NVMe engine's DMA scheduler can choose to send a CQE (small, posted) while large data writes are blocked — subject to the ordering rules, which the core enforces.

Specification view

Flow control is defined in the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 — Transaction Layer Specification, Flow Control section, with the FC DLLP formats and initialisation state machine in the PCI Express Base Specification — Data Link Layer Specification chapter, and the errors in the PCI Express Base Specification — Advanced Error Reporting Extended Capability. The NVMe specifications (NVMe Base Specification 2.4, NVMe over PCIe Transport Specification 1.4) do not define link-level flow control; the NVMe 2.4 rate-limiting features control bandwidth and IOPS allocation between controllers at a higher level and must not be confused with PCIe credits.

Six credit types (PH, PD, NPH, NPD, CplH, CplD). One header credit per TLP header; one data credit per 16 bytes (4 DW). Initialisation by InitFC1 and InitFC2 DLLPs for P, NP and Cpl; VC0 initialised automatically. An advertised value of 0 means infinite. Flow control is per link and per VC. UpdateFC carries the receiver's running total. Receiver Overflow and Flow Control Protocol Error are uncorrectable errors.

The bandwidth formula BW ≈ C × 16 / Tloop, the credit numbers needed for Gen4 x4, the argument that endpoint completion credits are infinite to avoid deadlock, and the classification of NVMe traffic into credit types are reasoned from the rules.

The number of credits advertised (above the minimums), buffer partitioning, credit-return policy, UpdateFC batching within the rules, whether CREDITS_RECEIVED overflow checking is implemented, and any vendor-specific credit counters.

Scaled Flow Control and the Data Link Feature exchange (PCIe 4.0 and later). Flit Mode (PCIe 6.0 and later) carries flow-control information differently and introduces shared-credit options; consult the Flit Mode flow-control rules rather than assuming classic DLLP behaviour.

Check in the specification: the minimum initial advertisement table; the exact Endpoint and Root Complex rules for infinite completion credits; the UpdateFC timer recommendation; header-credit accounting for TLP prefixes; Scaled Flow Control requirements and scale factors per data rate. The specification does not tell you how many credits a product should advertise for good performance, nor the credit loop time of any particular platform.

Never assume: that credits are end-to-end (they are per hop); that a stalled transmitter has logged anything (it has not); that "infinite" completion credits mean infinite completion bandwidth (the requester's buffers and tags still limit it); or that a Gen5 device in a Gen4 slot uses the same credit numbers it would at Gen5.

Protocol view

Flow-control DLLPs always travel in the opposite direction to the TLPs they govern: the Root Port's UpdateFC-P DLLPs (going down to the SSD) release the SSD's posted writes (going up). Every credit type in each direction is an independent conversation.

Requests and their completions use different credit pools in different directions. For an NVMe write command, the SSD's MRd for write data consumes the Root Port's NPH credits (SSD → Root Port direction), and the Root Port's CplD carrying the data consumes the SSD's completion credits (Root Port → SSD direction) — which the SSD advertised as infinite. For an NVMe read command, the SSD's MWr of read data consumes the Root Port's PH and PD credits. Every doorbell the host writes consumes the SSD's PH and PD credits.

Reasoning: the limiting resource is the Root Port's NPH credits, not the SSD's tags (Chapter 13) — the SSD's tag pool is nearly unused. Note that NPH credits return when the Root Port forwards the request into the memory system, not when the completion is sent, so the loop here is short; the deeper limit is how many reads the Root Complex will keep in flight. On real platforms this is fixed by the silicon; the practical lever is the device's MRRS (fewer, larger reads consume fewer NPH credits per byte) — another reason firmware and BIOS set MRRS deliberately.

Linux has no standard interface to PCIe credit counters. What it can show is the configuration that shapes credit use, the errors, and the throughput that results.

What this proves: MPS/MRRS values (which determine credits per TLP and TLPs per byte), the presence of flow-control errors, NUMA locality. What it cannot prove: how many credits are advertised, how fast they return, or whether a throughput ceiling is credit-limited. Some CPUs and switches expose per-port PCIe performance-monitoring counters (sometimes including credit-stall events) through perf uncore events; their names and meaning are platform-specific Implementation-dependent — consult the platform's uncore performance-monitoring documentation before trusting them.

Code / pseudocode

This simulation models one direction of the lab link: the Lab SSD sending 256-byte MWr TLPs gated by PH and PD credits, with credits returned after a configurable drain time plus UpdateFC flight time. It prints throughput against advertised PD credits and credit loop time. Standard library only.

"""Credit-limited throughput on one PCIe link direction (simulation, standard library only).

Models posted Memory Write TLPs (MWr) from the Lab SSD to the Root Port, gated by
Posted Header (PH) and Posted Data (PD) credits. The receiver frees each TLP's
buffer space after `drain_ns` and the UpdateFC DLLP reaches the transmitter
`return_ns` later. Times are in nanoseconds and illustrative only."""

FC_UNIT = 16                      # bytes per data credit (4 DW)

def simulate(payload=256, ph_credits=32, pd_credits=256, link_gbps=7.88,
             drain_ns=300.0, return_ns=200.0, n_tlps=2000):
    hdr_bytes = 16 + 6 + 2         # 4-DW header + seq/LCRC + framing token
    tlp_ns = (payload + hdr_bytes) / link_gbps          # bytes / (GB/s) = ns
    pd_per_tlp = -(-payload // FC_UNIT)                 # ceiling division
    consumed_h = consumed_d = 0                         # CREDITS_CONSUMED
    limit_h, limit_d = ph_credits, pd_credits           # CREDIT_LIMIT
    returns = []                                        # (time, hdr, data) UpdateFC arrivals
    t = 0.0
    stalled_ns = 0.0
    for _ in range(n_tlps):
        while True:
            # apply every credit return that has arrived by time t
            for r in [r for r in returns if r[0] <= t]:
                returns.remove(r)
                limit_h += r[1]
                limit_d += r[2]
            if consumed_h + 1 <= limit_h and consumed_d + pd_per_tlp <= limit_d:
                break
            nxt = min(r[0] for r in returns)            # wait for the next UpdateFC
            stalled_ns += nxt - t
            t = nxt
        consumed_h += 1
        consumed_d += pd_per_tlp
        t += tlp_ns                                     # TLP occupies the link
        returns.append((t + drain_ns + return_ns, 1, pd_per_tlp))
    data = n_tlps * payload
    return data / t, stalled_ns / t                     # GB/s, fraction of time stalled

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("PD credits  bytes  loop(ns)  throughput(GB/s)  stalled")
    for pd in (16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512):
        for loop in (500, 1000):
            gbps, st = simulate(ph_credits=pd // 16 + 1, pd_credits=pd,
                                drain_ns=loop - 200, return_ns=200)
            print(f"{pd:10d} {pd*FC_UNIT:6d} {loop:9d} {gbps:17.2f} {st:8.0%}")

Pseudocode: transmitter gating

for each VC, for each type T in {P, NP, Cpl}:
    if advertised_hdr[T] == INFINITE: skip header check for T
    if advertised_data[T] == INFINITE: skip data check for T

function can_send(tlp):
    T   = category(tlp)                      # P, NP or Cpl
    h   = 1
    d   = ceil(payload_bytes(tlp) / 16)
    ok_h = INFINITE_H[T] or ((LIMIT_H[T] - (CONSUMED_H[T] + h)) mod 2^nh) <= 2^nh / 2
    ok_d = INFINITE_D[T] or d == 0 or ((LIMIT_D[T] - (CONSUMED_D[T] + d)) mod 2^nd) <= 2^nd / 2
    return ok_h and ok_d

scheduler loop:
    pick next TLP allowed by ordering rules (posted may bypass blocked NP/Cpl where permitted)
    if can_send(tlp): CONSUMED_H[T] += 1; CONSUMED_D[T] += d; hand TLP to Data Link Layer
    else: try another category's TLP that ordering permits; never block P behind NP

on UpdateFC(T, hdr, data):
    LIMIT_H[T] = hdr; LIMIT_D[T] = data      # absolute running totals, not increments

Worked example

Problem. The Lab SSD can produce read data at 7 GB/s. The Root Port advertises PH = 32 and PD = 256. Measured credit loop (MWr leaving the SSD to the UpdateFC returning those credits being processed) is 1.2 µs. Does the SSD hit 7 GB/s? If not, what PD advertisement would be needed?

Step 1 — data-credit bound. 256 × 16 B = 4096 B per 1.2 µs ≈ 3.41 GB/s.

Step 2 — header-credit bound. 32 headers × 256 B = 8192 B per 1.2 µs ≈ 6.8 GB/s. Header credits are less restrictive here.

Step 3 — link bound. Gen4 x4 ≈ 7.88 GB/s raw; with 256-byte payloads and 24 bytes of per-TLP overhead (header, sequence number, LCRC, framing), payload efficiency ≈ 256/280 ≈ 91 % → ≈ 7.2 GB/s, minus a little for DLLPs travelling the same direction.

Step 4 — result. min(3.41, 6.8, 7.2) ≈ 3.4 GB/s. The SSD is credit-limited at about half its capability.

Step 5 — required credits. 7 GB/s × 1.2 µs = 8400 B → 525 PD credits; headers: 8400 / 256 ≈ 33 PH. Alternatively, cut the loop: at 0.55 µs, 256 PD credits suffice (4096 B / 0.55 µs ≈ 7.4 GB/s).

Interpretation. You usually cannot change the Root Port's advertisement. You can influence the loop: NUMA-local buffers, IOMMU mode, avoiding contention. Note that 525 still fits comfortably within half of the 12-bit unscaled field (2047); the field itself becomes the limit only with much longer loops or much faster links — on wide Gen5 links and at Gen6 speeds Scaled Flow Control becomes necessary for the same latency. Derived

Common mistakes

  • "Credits are end-to-end." No — every link has its own; a switch has separate credits on its upstream and each downstream link.
  • "Data credits are bytes" or "are DWs". One data credit is 16 bytes (4 DW).
  • "A Memory Read consumes data credits for the data it requests." The MRd itself has no payload; it consumes one NPH. The data comes back as CplD in the other direction against completion credits.
  • "Zero means none." An initial advertisement of zero means infinite.
  • "UpdateFC says how many credits were freed." It carries the running total; subtract the previous value to get the increment.
  • Confusing Ack and UpdateFC. An Ack retires a TLP from the replay buffer; it says nothing about receiver buffer space.
  • Blaming the device for a throughput ceiling that the Root Port's credit return sets. Always compute the credit bound first.
  • Confusing NVMe 2.4 rate limiting with PCIe flow control. Different layers, different purposes.

Debugging connection

Credit problems rarely announce themselves. They show as:

  • A throughput plateau that does not move with queue depth. If adding outstanding commands no longer helps, something below NVMe is saturated: link, credits, or the memory system.
  • A plateau that changes with platform, socket or slot but not with the device. Points to the Root Complex side of the credit loop.
  • Burst–idle patterns in a capture. Transmitter stalls aligned with UpdateFC arrivals.
  • Write throughput far below read throughput on the same link: the device's MRd stream is limited by NPH credits or by the Root Complex's read concurrency.
  • Uncorrectable Flow Control Protocol Error or Receiver Overflow: a logic bug in one port; capture the DLLPs around the event.

Disambiguating experiment: change MPS from 256 to 128 bytes on a lab machine (via BIOS or the kernel's pci=pcie_bus_safe policy, then reboot). If throughput falls roughly in proportion to header-credit-limited bandwidth, the bottleneck is header credits; if it barely changes, data credits or the link are the limit. Always restore settings afterwards.

Senior engineer insight

Think of credits not as "buffer sizes" but as bytes in flight. Every link must keep bandwidth × loop latency bytes in flight to run at full speed — exactly Little's Law, applied to one hop. When a platform adds a retimer, a switch, or an IOMMU that slows memory writes, the loop grows and the same credits deliver less bandwidth. That is why an SSD can benchmark at full speed in one server and lose a third of its throughput behind a switch in another, with identical firmware and zero errors.

What are the initial advertisements per category at each supported speed? What is the worst-case credit loop under memory contention? Does UpdateFC batching ever delay returns beyond the recommended interval? Is Scaled Flow Control negotiated? Under mixed read/write NVMe traffic from many devices, does any category starve? These belong in a design review, not a post-production escalation.

Challenge

Add UpdateFC batching to the simulator: the receiver returns credits only when at least 64 credits are pending. Show how batching changes throughput at small credit counts.

Hint

With batching, the effective loop gets longer by the time it takes to accumulate a batch, and the transmitter sees fewer, larger returns — the burst–idle pattern from Trace 3.

Solution

What a senior engineer notices

That the simulator's "credit loop" hides the real question — what the receiver is waiting for. A senior engineer asks which part of the loop dominates (buffer residence, not wire time, almost always) and whether it varies with load.

Failure variants

  • A credit leak: the receiver forgets to return credits for one TLP type after a rare event; throughput decays over hours then collapses to zero. Detect by trending; prove with an analyzer comparing consumed and returned totals.
  • Asymmetric MPS (Root Port 128, device 256 programmed incorrectly): the device sends 256-byte TLPs that the Root Port treats as Malformed — an error, not a credit stall.

Extension

Model a switch: two links in series, each with its own credits and loop. Show that the slower hop sets throughput and that backpressure propagates upstream.

  2. A transmitter has CREDIT_LIMIT (PD) = 0x010 and CREDITS_CONSUMED = 0xFF8 (12-bit field). Can it send a 256-byte MWr? Show the arithmetic.

  4. The Root Port advertises NPH = 8. Memory latency is 800 ns; MRRS is 512 bytes. Estimate the best-case read bandwidth the SSD can achieve when fetching write data (assume the Root Port returns NPH credits only when the completion is sent).
  5. List the credit types consumed in each direction during one 4 KiB NVMe Read on the lab system, from doorbell to MSI-X.
Model answers

(1) 1 PH + 4 PD; 1 PH + 1 PD; 16 TLPs × (1 PH + 16 PD) = 16 PH + 256 PD. (2) Required 16. (0x010 − (0xFF8 + 16)) mod 4096 = (16 − 4088 − 16) mod 4096 = (−4088) mod 4096 = 8 ≤ 2048 → yes; after sending, 8 credits remain (the counters wrapped). (3) A completion can only arrive for a request the Endpoint issued, so it can reserve space in advance. Quiz

  1. How many bytes does one PCIe data credit represent?

    • 4
    • 8
    • 16
    • 64
    Answer and reasoning

    One data credit = 4 DW = 16 bytes.

  2. Which credit type does a Memory Read request consume?

    • PH
    • NPH only
    • NPH and NPD
    • CplH
    Answer and reasoning

    An MRd has no payload: one NPH. Its data returns as CplD against completion credits in the other direction.

  3. An InitFC1-Cpl with HdrFC = 0 and DataFC = 0 means:

    • No completions may be sent
    • The link is not ready
    • An error
    • Infinite completion credits
    Answer and reasoning

    Zero in an initial advertisement means infinite; Endpoints advertise this for completions.

  4. An UpdateFC DLLP is lost to a CRC error. What happens?

    • The next UpdateFC carries the running total and corrects the transmitter's limit
    • The receiver retransmits it after a Nak
    • The credits are lost permanently
    • The link retrains
    Answer and reasoning

    Running totals make DLLP loss harmless (apart from a short delay).

  5. Credits in PCIe are maintained:

    • End to end between requester and completer
    • Only at the Root Complex
    • Per link, per VC, per type
    • Per tag
    Answer and reasoning

    Hop-by-hop, per Virtual Channel, for each of six types.

  6. Which statement about credit starvation is true?

    • It always logs a correctable error
    • It causes the transmitter to wait, with no error logged
    • It causes a Nak
    • It is impossible with infinite credits anywhere on the link
    Answer and reasoning

    Starvation is legal backpressure; only rule violations produce errors.

  7. Open: Give the formula for credit-limited bandwidth and define its variables.

    Model answer

    BW ≈ C × 16 B / Tloop, where C is the data credits advertised for the category, 16 B the size of a credit, and Tloop the time from consuming a credit to having it returned and processed at the transmitter.

  8. Open: Why must posted requests not be blocked behind non-posted requests that lack credits?

    Model answer

    Because the completion that would free the non-posted path may depend on that posted write being delivered (producer–consumer and peer patterns); blocking it can form a circular wait — a deadlock. Separate credit pools plus the ordering rules' passing permissions prevent it.

  9. Open: An SSD reaches 7 GB/s reading in server A and 4 GB/s in server B, zero errors in both. What is your first hypothesis and first measurement?

    Model answer

    Credit or memory-system limit on server B's Root Complex side (longer credit loop or fewer credits). First, confirm link speed/width and MPS are identical; then measure with NUMA-local versus remote buffers and, if available, an analyzer to see burst–idle patterns aligned with UpdateFC-P.

Interview questions

  1. Beginner: What are the six flow-control credit types?

    Model answer

    Posted Header, Posted Data, Non-Posted Header, Non-Posted Data, Completion Header, Completion Data — per VC, per link.

  2. Beginner: What is the difference between an Ack and an UpdateFC?

    Model answer

    Ack is a reliability mechanism: the TLP arrived intact and can leave the replay buffer. UpdateFC is a buffer-space mechanism: the receiver has freed space and the transmitter may send more of that type.

  3. Intermediate: Why are completion credits infinite at Endpoints, and what limits completion traffic then?

    Model answer

    Deadlock avoidance and the fact that the Endpoint controls how much completion data it can receive by limiting its own outstanding reads. The limit is its tag pool and completion buffer.

  4. Advanced: Explain how an IOMMU can reduce NVMe read throughput without any IOMMU fault.

    Model answer

    Each DMA write must be translated; translation misses require page-table walks that delay the Root Complex's draining of posted writes. Slower draining lengthens the PD credit loop, so the device stalls on credits. Throughput falls, no errors are logged.

  5. Senior: Design a validation test that catches a credit leak in a new Root Port.

    Model answer

    Use an exerciser to drive long-duration mixed traffic (all types, all sizes, including rare TLPs like Messages and AtomicOps, plus error injection, power-state transitions and resets of the downstream device), and an analyzer to compute consumed versus returned credits per type over time. Assert that available credits return to the initial values whenever traffic quiesces. Run across all supported speeds, with and without Scaled Flow Control.

  6. Senior: A customer asks you to "increase PCIe credits" to fix NVMe throughput. How do you respond?

    Model answer

    Credits are fixed by silicon; first prove the throughput is credit-limited by computing the bound from measured loop time and comparing with link and device limits. If credits are the bound, the levers are shortening the loop (NUMA locality, IOMMU configuration, removing contention), MPS/MRRS choices, and platform topology (avoid switch hops), not changing advertisements.

Child: "The café gives the baker tickets for how many trays fit on its shelves. The baker can't send a tray without a ticket. When the café empties a shelf, it sends tickets back."

Junior: "The host's doorbell write uses the SSD's posted credits. The SSD's fetch of the command uses the Root Port's non-posted header credits; the 64 bytes come back as a completion, which the SSD always accepts. The SSD writes 4 KiB of data as sixteen 256-byte posted writes, each using one posted header and 16 posted data credits at the Root Port, then the completion entry and the MSI-X write use one more of each."

Senior: "Full bandwidth needs bandwidth × loop latency bytes in flight on every hop. Doubling the data rate doubles the bytes needed; retimers and switches lengthen the loop. Without scaled credits and buffers sized for the worst loop, a faster link delivers the same throughput as a slower one. Evaluate a platform by its credit loop under memory contention, not by its link rate."

Chapter summary

PCIe never sends a TLP the receiver has no room for. Each receiver advertises credits for six types — PH, PD, NPH, NPD, CplH, CplD — per VC, at link-up via InitFC1/InitFC2 DLLPs, and returns them as buffers drain with UpdateFC DLLPs carrying running totals. A header credit is one TLP header; a data credit is 16 bytes. Zero means infinite; Endpoints accept all completions. A transmitter without credits simply waits, so credit problems appear as throughput ceilings and burst–idle patterns, not errors. Credit-limited bandwidth ≈ credits × 16 bytes ÷ credit loop time. For NVMe, the read-data path depends on the Root Port's posted credits and memory draining speed; the write-data path depends on how many reads the Root Port will accept (NPH) and the device's own completion buffering. Rule violations — Receiver Overflow, Flow Control Protocol Error — are uncorrectable errors.
