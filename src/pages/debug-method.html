This page teaches the one skill that separates a senior NVMe and PCI Express (PCIe) engineer from someone who has merely read the specifications: a disciplined way of moving from "something is wrong" to "this is the root cause, here is the evidence, here is the fix, and here is the test that will catch it next time". You will learn an eleven-step chain (symptom → timeline → expected behaviour → first deviation → layer → evidence → hypotheses → elimination → root cause → fix → regression test) and a five-word habit (observation, inference, hypothesis, evidence, root cause) that you will use on every trace, log and bug report for the rest of the course.

Why this part exists

Every earlier part of the course taught you how things work: how a link trains, how a Transaction Layer Packet (TLP) is built, how a Submission Queue Entry (SQE) is fetched, how a completion and an interrupt come back. None of that, on its own, lets you debug. Debugging is a different activity. It is reasoning under uncertainty with incomplete, noisy, sometimes contradictory evidence, under time pressure, while other people are confidently telling you what the answer is.

Most failed investigations do not fail because the engineer lacked protocol knowledge. They fail because the engineer:

  • treated the loudest error message as the cause, when it was the fifth consequence of something quieter;
  • mixed up what they saw with what they concluded, and then defended the conclusion as if it were an observation;
  • changed three things at once, saw the problem disappear, and never learned which change mattered;
  • fixed the symptom and wrote no test, so the same bug came back six months later under a different name.

This part makes the method explicit, gives it vocabulary, and drills it with worked examples. The Failure Library then applies it to thirty concrete failure classes; Performance Engineering applies it to "slow" instead of "broken"; Validation Engineering turns the regression-test step into a discipline of its own.

Learning objectives

  • State the eleven steps of the debugging chain and explain what goes wrong when each one is skipped.
  • Classify any statement about a failure as an observation, an inference, a hypothesis, a piece of evidence, or a root-cause claim, and rewrite sloppy statements into the right category.
  • Build a single merged timeline from a protocol-analyzer trace, the Linux kernel log, NVMe log pages and a workload tool, and align their clocks.
  • Identify the first abnormal event in a trace and distinguish it from the most visible abnormal event.
  • Localise a failure to a layer (physical, data link, transaction, configuration, NVMe transport, NVMe controller, command set, media, driver, application) using evidence rather than guesswork.
  • Generate competing hypotheses and design a disambiguating experiment that separates them.
  • State a root cause with an explicit confidence level, separate it from contributing factors, and write a regression test that would have caught it.

The story

Imagine the school's toy train set stops working. Everyone runs over. One child shouts: "The engine is broken!" because the engine is the thing that is not moving. Another says: "It's the batteries!" because last time it was the batteries.

A good detective child does something different. First she writes down only what she can see: the engine is not moving; the little light on the engine is off; the track has a piece that is slightly lifted. Then she asks: "When did it last work?" Someone remembers it worked before lunch, and after lunch Sam was building a bridge next to the track.

She asks what should happen: power goes from the box, through the track, into the engine. She follows the track from the box and finds the first place where things are not right: the lifted piece is right after the box, before the bridge, before the engine. The engine is fine. The batteries are fine. The first broken thing is the track joint Sam bumped.

She pushes the joint down. The train runs. And then she does one more clever thing: she puts a little piece of tape on the joint and tells everyone, "Check this joint every time you build near the track." That tape is a regression test.

An emergency-room doctor works the same way. The patient arrives with a symptom ("chest pain"). The doctor separates what the patient reports, what the instruments measure (heart rate, ECG trace, blood tests), and what she suspects. She builds a timeline ("when did it start, what were you doing"). She compares against normal values. She looks for the earliest abnormal reading, not the most dramatic one. She keeps a list of possible diagnoses (a differential diagnosis) and orders the specific test that best separates them, not every test available. She treats, confirms the treatment worked, and the hospital adds a protocol so the next patient is triaged faster.

The doctor analogy hides three important differences. First, you can often reproduce the failure: you can rerun the workload, swap the slot, change one register, which medicine rarely allows. Use that power; it is the strongest evidence you can get. Second, your instruments can lie in specific, knowable ways: an analyzer can drop packets when its buffer fills, a kernel log is written after the fact by software that may itself be confused, and a timestamp from the host and one from the analyzer come from different clocks. Third, the "patient" is two independently specified technologies, NVMe and PCIe, joined by a third (the NVMe over PCIe Transport specification). A symptom in one is often caused in another, and the specifications assign responsibility to different agents. The train-set story hides something too: real failures are frequently intermittent, and there may be no lifted track to see at the moment you look.

The debugging chain

Here is the framework you will use for every investigation in this course. It is not bureaucracy; each arrow exists because skipping it produces a specific, common, expensive mistake.

Symptom

A symptom is a report from outside the system: "the database stalls for 30 seconds", "fio shows 2.1 GB/s instead of 7", "the drive disappears after suspend". Record it verbatim, with who saw it, when, on which machine, with which build. Do not translate it yet. "The SSD timed out" is already an interpretation; the person may have seen a log line containing the word timeout, which could come from the block layer, the NVMe driver or the PCIe Completion Timeout mechanism, three different things.

Skipping it costs you: you solve a different problem from the one reported.

Timeline

Gather every source of events and put them on one time axis: analyzer trace, kernel log (dmesg or the journal), NVMe log pages (Error Information, SMART / Health Information, Telemetry where supported), Advanced Error Reporting (AER) registers, workload tool output, power or reset events, firmware logs. Align clocks using an anchor event visible in two sources at once (for example, a reset, a link-down, or a specific command identifier).

Skipping it costs you: you cannot tell cause from effect, because you do not know which came first.

Expected behaviour

Before you can say something is abnormal, write down what normal is. For an NVMe Read on PCIe the expected sequence is: doorbell write from the host, SQE fetch (Memory Read Request, MRd) from the device, Completion with Data (CplD) from the host, data written to host memory by the device (Memory Write Requests, MWr), Completion Queue Entry (CQE) written by the device, MSI-X interrupt write by the device, and later a Completion Queue head doorbell from the host. Write it from the specification and from a known-good capture (a "golden trace", see Synthetic vs Real Traces, Diffing and Golden Traces), not from memory.

Skipping it costs you: you flag normal behaviour as a bug (for example, a completion split at the Read Completion Boundary) and miss abnormal behaviour that looks routine.

First deviation

Walk the timeline forward from a point where everything was known to be good and stop at the earliest event that differs from expected. This is the first abnormal event. Everything after it may be consequence. The famous trap: a 30-second NVMe I/O timeout followed by a controller reset produces a dramatic burst of log lines, but the first abnormal event happened 30 seconds earlier and was quiet, perhaps a single missing CplD.

Skipping it costs you: you debug the cascade instead of the cause.

Layer

Ask which layer owns the first deviation. A missing Ack DLLP is a data link layer event; a Completion Timeout is a transaction layer event at the requester; a wrong Physical Region Page (PRP) entry is an NVMe command construction error by the host driver; an Unrecovered Read Error is a media event inside the controller. The layer tells you which specification applies, which agent is responsible, and which instrument can see it.

Skipping it costs you: you look for evidence in the wrong place, and you route the bug to the wrong team.

Evidence

For the layer you chose, list what has actually been captured and what could be captured. Be honest about gaps: if the analyzer was attached between the switch and the SSD, it did not see traffic between the root port and the switch.

Hypotheses

Write down every plausible explanation for the first deviation, including uncomfortable ones ("our driver is wrong", "the analyzer is wrong"). Two to five hypotheses is typical. A single hypothesis is a warning sign: it means you stopped thinking.

Elimination

For each pair of hypotheses, find an experiment whose outcome would differ depending on which is true. This is a disambiguating experiment. "Rerun and see if it happens again" usually does not disambiguate anything. "Force the link to Gen3 and rerun; if errors vanish, signal integrity at Gen4 is implicated; if they persist, it is not" does.

Root cause

The surviving hypothesis, stated precisely, with the evidence that supports it, the evidence that would refute it, and a confidence level. A root cause is a mechanism, not a component: "the driver reuses a Command Identifier before its previous completion is consumed when the queue wraps" is a root cause; "the driver" is not.

Fix

A change that removes the mechanism. Verify it two ways: the original symptom is gone under the original reproduction, and the first abnormal event no longer appears in a fresh trace. If you only check the symptom, you may have hidden the bug (for example, by lengthening a timeout).

Regression test

A test that fails on the unfixed system and passes on the fixed one, added permanently to the test suite. If you cannot write one, ask why: often it means you have not really found the mechanism. Validation Engineering treats this in depth.

Protocol systems are designed to recover. PCIe replays bad TLPs, retrains links, and reports errors; the NVMe driver aborts and resets; the block layer retries. Each recovery mechanism generates its own events. By the time a human notices, the log is dominated by recovery noise. The cause is almost always the earliest and quietest event. Seniors scroll backwards from the first loud error until they find the last moment everything was normal.

Observation, inference, hypothesis, evidence, root cause

The chain above tells you what to do. This five-part habit tells you how to talk and think at every step. Every debugging exercise in this course requires these five headings.

The central concept on the ladder: the first abnormal event

When dominoes fall, the one everybody looks at is the last big one that crashes into the vase. The one that matters is the first one someone's elbow touched.

In a system with retries and recovery, one fault produces a chain of later errors. The first abnormal event is the earliest observation that differs from the expected behaviour; later errors are candidates for being consequences of it.

Hardware agents keep state: a replay buffer, a completion-timeout timer per outstanding request, a controller's internal command table. When one agent misbehaves, other agents' timers expire later and they report what they saw. The earliest hardware event that differs is closest to the misbehaving agent.

The specifications define which agent detects what: in PCIe, the requester detects a Completion Timeout; the receiver detects a Malformed TLP; the data link layer detects a bad Link Cyclic Redundancy Check (LCRC). In NVMe, the controller sets a status code in the CQE; the host detects a missing CQE only through its own timer, which the NVMe specification does not define (it is a host policy). Knowing who detects what tells you that a reported error is often far from its cause.

On the wire, the first abnormal event is often an absence (a missing CplD, a missing Ack, a missing CQE write) or a small anomaly (a NAK DLLP, an unexpected tag, a doorbell value beyond the queue size) well before any error message.

In an analyzer you mark the first abnormal row, then check that every later abnormal row can be explained by it. If one cannot, you may have two problems, or you chose the wrong first event.

Debugging starts at the first abnormal event: its layer, its agent, its preconditions. Fixes aimed at later events usually suppress symptoms (longer timeouts, more retries) rather than causes.

Aligning clocks with anchor events

Pick an event visible in two sources. Good anchors:

  • Controller reset: the host writes Controller Configuration (CC) with Enable (EN) cleared at offset 14h; the analyzer sees an MWr to BAR0+0x14; the kernel logs the reset. Two clocks, one event.
  • Link down / retrain: the analyzer sees electrical idle and training ordered sets; the kernel may log a link change or AER Surprise Down.
  • A marker I/O: issue a distinctive command at a known moment (for example, a Read of a specific unusual LBA) and find it in the trace by its Starting LBA field inside the fetched SQE.

Compute the offset (analyzer time minus host time) at the anchor; apply it to all host events. Note the uncertainty: kernel log timestamps are taken when the message is generated, which can be milliseconds after the hardware event. Write the uncertainty into the timeline ("±2 ms").

Clock drift matters in long captures. A 50 parts-per-million difference is 3 ms over a minute. Use two anchors, one near each end, if the capture is long and the timing matters.

Defining expected behaviour

"Expected" has three sources, in decreasing authority:

  1. The specification (Normative statements): what must happen. Example: a non-posted request must receive a completion or the requester's Completion Timeout mechanism (when enabled) eventually fires.
  2. The design of the specific product (Implementation-dependent): how this controller and this driver chose to behave within the specification. Example: this controller fetches up to 8 SQEs per MRd; this driver rings the doorbell once per batch.
  3. A golden trace of the same workload on a known-good build: what actually happens when things work. It captures implementation choices you did not know to write down.

Expected behaviour must include allowed variation. A completer may split a read completion at Read Completion Boundary (RCB) boundaries; a controller may fetch several SQEs in one MRd; MSI-X writes may be coalesced; CQ head doorbells may be batched. None of these is abnormal. If your "expected" model forbids them, every capture will look broken.

Finding the first deviation

Forward scan, backward scan

Two scanning strategies work together:

  • Backward scan: start at the first loud error and walk backwards until you reach a stretch of clearly normal behaviour. The boundary is your candidate first deviation.
  • Forward scan: start from a known-good point (a fresh reset, the start of the workload) and compare each event against the expected sequence. The first mismatch is your candidate.

If the two scans disagree, you have learned something: either there are two problems, or your expected model is incomplete.

Absences are events

Many first deviations are things that did not happen: a missing completion, a missing Ack, a missing interrupt, a doorbell never rung. Analyzers do not highlight absences. You find them by pairing: every non-posted request should pair with a completion (by Requester ID and Tag); every SQ tail doorbell should be followed by SQE fetches covering the new entries; every CQE should be followed eventually by a CQ head doorbell. Unpaired items are candidates.

Hypotheses and elimination

Generating hypotheses

For the first deviation, ask: which agents touched this event, and what could each of them have done wrong? For a missing CplD to a device MRd, the agents are the device (did it send a well-formed request to a valid address?), the link (did the request arrive?), the root complex and IOMMU (did they accept and route it?), and host memory management (was the address mapped?). That immediately gives four hypotheses.

Add the two hypotheses people forget:

  • The instrument is wrong: the analyzer dropped events, was probing a different link, or its trigger missed the start.
  • Your expected model is wrong: the behaviour is legal and you misunderstood the specification or the design.

Designing disambiguating experiments

Elimination techniques

Swap
Change exactly one component (drive, slot, cable, host, firmware, driver build). If the failure follows the component, it is implicated. Cheap and powerful, but only one change at a time.
Bisection
Between a good version and a bad version (of firmware, kernel, configuration), test the midpoint. Each step halves the search space. Ten steps search a thousand changes.
Stress amplification
Make a rare failure common: raise queue depth, shorten timeouts in a lab, increase temperature, force more resets. A 1-in-a-week bug becomes 1-in-an-hour, and every experiment becomes faster.
Simplification
Remove everything not needed to reproduce: one queue, one namespace, one I/O size, no filesystem. Each removal that keeps the failure narrows the hypothesis space.
Prediction
Use the leading hypothesis to predict something not yet observed ("if this is CID reuse, we should see two SQEs with CID 0x0042 outstanding at once"), then look. Confirmed predictions are much stronger evidence than explanations made after the fact.

"The failure happened on three machines that all had firmware B" is correlation. It becomes evidence only when you also check the machines with firmware A under the same workload, and preferably flip one machine from A to B and back.

Root cause, fix and regression test

Root cause versus contributing factors

A root cause is the mechanism whose removal prevents the failure. Contributing factors make it more likely or more severe. Example: a driver bug that reuses a Command Identifier (CID) is the root cause; high queue depth, a slow namespace and a short host timeout are contributing factors. Write both down; fix the root cause; consider whether contributing factors deserve their own hardening.

Asking "why" until it stops being useful

"Five whys" is a useful prompt, not a ritual. Stop when the next "why" leaves your system (physics, a third-party design decision) or when the answer is a change you can make and test.

  1. Why did the I/O time out? No CQE was posted for CID 0x0042.
  2. Why no CQE? The controller never fetched the SQE that carried it.
  3. Why was it never fetched? The SQ tail doorbell written was 0x0400 (1024) on a 1024-entry queue, an invalid value, so the controller ignored the write.
  4. Why was 1024 written? The driver incremented the tail and wrapped with if (tail > depth) instead of if (tail == depth).
  5. Why did tests not catch it? No test filled the queue to exactly its last slot. → Regression test: fill each queue to depth − 1 and wrap repeatedly.

Verifying the fix

  • The original reproduction no longer shows the symptom, run for at least several times the original mean time to failure.
  • A fresh capture shows the first abnormal event is gone, not merely that recovery became faster.
  • Reverting the fix brings the failure back (the strongest check; skip it only when it is unsafe or very expensive).
  • No new first abnormal event appears elsewhere (fixes can move problems).

A good regression test

It fails on the unfixed build, passes on the fixed one, runs automatically, is cheap enough to run often, and checks the mechanism (the doorbell value never equals the queue size) as well as the symptom (no timeouts). See Validation Engineering — regression.

Each example uses the five headings. All traces are synthetic and use the course's reference lab system (Gen4 x4, Lab SSD at 01:00.0, Requester ID 0x0100, root port 00:01.1, BAR0 at 0xFE60_0000, MPS 256 bytes).

Example 1: "The SSD randomly freezes for 30 seconds"

Symptom (verbatim): "Under our database benchmark, every few hours all I/O to nvme0 stops for about 30 seconds, then continues."

#DirTimeTypeFieldsMeaning

8811Dev→Host+760 nsMRd64Addr=0x1_2340_FF80 Len=16 Tag=0x10Fetch SQE 1022

Observation

Doorbell write to SQ1 tail with value 0x400 (1024). SQ1 was created with 1024 entries (the Create I/O Submission Queue command's Queue Size is zero-based, 1023, in the captured admin traffic). No SQE fetch follows. Thirty seconds later, the host writes CC.

Inference

A valid tail value lies in 0…1023. 1024 is out of range. The NVMe Base Specification treats writing an invalid doorbell value as an error the controller may report through an asynchronous event (Invalid Doorbell Write Value) Uncertain — check the Base Specification's asynchronous event information definitions for the exact name and whether reporting is required. Either way, the controller did not fetch the entry; the host's 30-second timer fired.

Hypotheses

  • H1: driver wrap logic writes depth instead of 0.
  • H2: driver memory corruption of the tail variable.
  • H3: analyzer decode error (wrong BAR mapping configured in the analyzer).

Evidence

H3: check the analyzer's BAR settings against lspci; the other doorbells decode correctly, so H3 is weak. H1 vs H2: H1 predicts the bad value is always exactly 1024 and always follows 1023; H2 predicts random values. Search all captures: every bad doorbell is 0x400 following 0x3FF. Code review finds if (++tail > q->depth) tail = 0;.

Root cause

Confirmed: off-by-one in the tail wrap. Fix to if (++tail == q->depth). Regression test: a queue-wrap test that submits depth × 10 commands with the queue kept nearly full, asserting no doorbell value ≥ depth (checked from an analyzer capture or a driver trace) and no timeouts.

Example 2: "Throughput is half what the datasheet says"

Symptom: "fio sequential read, 128 KiB, QD 32: 3.4 GB/s. Datasheet: 7.0 GB/s."

Observation

lspci -vvv -s 01:00.0 (read-only) shows Link Capabilities 16 GT/s x4 and Link Status 8 GT/s x4. AER correctable status at the device shows no bits set. The same drive in another machine reaches 6.9 GB/s.

Inference

Gen3 x4 has a theoretical effective ceiling near 3.5–3.6 GB/s for 256-byte payloads (see Performance — bandwidth calculations), so 3.4 GB/s is consistent with a link-limited workload. The drive itself can do more (other machine). The link trained, or retrained, at 8 GT/s.

Hypotheses

  • H1: the root port or slot supports only 8 GT/s (platform limit).
  • H2: signal integrity forced a lower speed during training or a later recovery.
  • H3: firmware or BIOS setting caps the target link speed.

Evidence

Check the root port's Link Capabilities (H1): reports 16 GT/s. Check Link Control 2 Target Link Speed at the root port (H3): set to 8 GT/s. A BIOS option "PCIe speed: Gen3" was enabled during an earlier compatibility test. H2 would leave Target Link Speed at 16 GT/s.

Root cause

High: platform configuration caps the target link speed. Fix: restore the BIOS setting, reboot, verify Link Status 16 GT/s and throughput. Regression test: the deployment check asserts negotiated speed and width equal the minimum of both ends' capabilities, for every NVMe device, at boot.

Example 3: "Read data is occasionally wrong"

Symptom: "Our integrity checker reports about one corrupted 4 KiB block per 10 million reads; the corrupted block contains data that belongs to a different file."

Observation

The capture triggered on the checker's failure shows a 16 KiB Read (CID 0x0193). Its SQE has PRP1 = 0x2_0004_1000 and PRP2 = 0x1_5000_0000 (a PRP list pointer). The controller fetches the PRP list: entries 0x2_0004_2000, 0x2_0004_3000, 0x2_0004_3000. Data MWr TLPs go to those addresses; the page 0x2_0004_3000 is written twice, and the page the application expected at the fourth position is never written.

Inference

The controller did exactly what the PRP list said. The host built a list with a duplicated entry. The fourth buffer page keeps stale content from its previous use, which is why "data from another file" appears.

Hypotheses

  • H1: driver PRP-list builder bug at a specific alignment or segment boundary.
  • H2: the PRP list page was modified by the host after the doorbell, before the controller fetched it (a lifetime/race bug).
  • H3: controller fetched a stale list due to its own caching (controller bug).

Evidence

H2 and H3 predict the list in host memory at doorbell time was correct. A driver trace records the list content at submission: it already contains the duplicate. H1 survives. The scatter-gather input had a 4 KiB segment followed by two 2 KiB segments in the same page; the builder advanced the page pointer per segment instead of per page boundary.

Root cause

High: PRP-list construction bug for sub-page segments. Regression test: a property-based test that builds PRP lists for randomly generated segment layouts and checks, in software, that the pages listed cover exactly the buffer, once each, in order. See Chapter 36 — PRP and Chapter 38 — PRP/SGL debugging lab.

Example 4: "Completions arrive, but the application still waits"

Symptom: "After a controller reset in our test, some I/O completes only after 30 seconds, then everything is normal."

#DirTimeTypeFieldsMeaning
5120Dev→Host+0MWr64Addr=0x1_2341_0230 Len=4 (16 B)CQE for CID 0x0007 to CQ1 slot 35, phase 1
—Dev→Host+0 … +30 s(none)no MWr to 0xFEE0_xxxxFirst abnormal event: no MSI-X message for vector 1
5121Host→Dev+30.0 sMRd32Addr=0xFE60_001CHost reads CSTS (timeout handler)

Observation

The CQE was written with the correct phase. No MSI-X message followed. A config space snapshot after the event shows MSI-X Enable = 1, Function Mask = 0, and the MSI-X table entry for vector 1 has its Mask bit set.

Inference

A masked vector does not send its message; the pending bit is set in the Pending Bit Array instead (behaviour defined by the PCIe MSI-X capability). The device behaved correctly given the mask. The host found the CQE only when its timeout handler polled the CQ.

Hypotheses

  • H1: after reset, the host restored the MSI-X table but left vector 1 masked.
  • H2: the device's MSI-X table was reset by the controller reset and the host did not reprogram it.
  • H3: interrupt remapping in the IOMMU dropped the message (would leave the mask clear).

Evidence

The mask bit is set (contradicts H3). A controller reset (CC.EN 1 → 0) is an NVMe-level reset; whether it touches PCIe MSI-X table state is defined by which reset it is. An NVMe controller reset should not reset PCIe configuration or MSI-X table state, whereas a Function Level Reset does Derived; check this against Chapter 48 and Chapter 49. The capture shows no FLR, so H2 is weak. Driver trace shows the reset path masks vectors, and the unmask happens only for vectors whose queues were "active" before the reset; queue 1 was idle at the time.

Root cause

High: host reset path leaves idle queues' vectors masked. Regression test: reset under mixed idle/active queues, then issue one command per queue and require each completion within 1 s, with no reliance on timeout polling.

Code: a first-deviation finder

This small tool encodes two ideas from this page: pairing (every non-posted request needs a completion; every doorbell value must be in range) and reporting the earliest violation, not every violation. It runs on a synthetic event list; adapting it to an analyzer export is an exercise.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Find the first abnormal event in a simplified, synthetic PCIe/NVMe event list.
Standard library only. Events are dicts sorted by time (ns)."""

CTO_NS = 50_000_000          # assumed Completion Timeout window for this example (50 ms)
QUEUE_DEPTH = {1: 1024, 2: 1024}   # SQ id -> entries (from Create I/O SQ)
SQ_TAIL_DB = {0xFE601008: 1, 0xFE601010: 2}   # doorbell address -> SQ id (DSTRD = 0)

events = [
    {"t": 0,      "dir": "down", "type": "MWr", "addr": 0xFE601008, "data": 0x3FF},
    {"t": 760,    "dir": "up",   "type": "MRd", "tag": 0x10, "rid": 0x0100},
    {"t": 1310,   "dir": "down", "type": "CplD", "tag": 0x10, "rid": 0x0100},
    {"t": 2000,   "dir": "up",   "type": "MRd", "tag": 0x11, "rid": 0x0100},
    {"t": 4020,   "dir": "down", "type": "MWr", "addr": 0xFE601008, "data": 0x400},
    {"t": 9_000_000, "dir": "down", "type": "CplD", "tag": 0x11, "rid": 0x0100},
    {"t": 80_000_000, "dir": "down", "type": "MWr", "addr": 0xFE600014, "data": 0x0},
]

def find_violations(evts):
    outstanding = {}   # (requester id, tag) -> request time
    found = []
    for e in evts:
        # 1. any outstanding request older than the timeout window is a violation at its deadline
        for key, t0 in list(outstanding.items()):
            if e["t"] - t0 > CTO_NS:
                found.append((t0 + CTO_NS, f"no completion for RID {key[0]:#06x} tag {key[1]:#04x} within {CTO_NS} ns"))
                del outstanding[key]
        if e["type"] == "MRd":
            outstanding[(e["rid"], e["tag"])] = e["t"]
        elif e["type"] == "CplD":
            key = (e["rid"], e["tag"])  # CplD carries the original Requester ID
            if key in outstanding:
                del outstanding[key]
            else:
                found.append((e["t"], f"unexpected completion tag {e['tag']:#04x}"))
        elif e["type"] == "MWr" and e.get("addr") in SQ_TAIL_DB:
            sq = SQ_TAIL_DB[e["addr"]]
            if not 0 <= e["data"] < QUEUE_DEPTH[sq]:
                found.append((e["t"], f"SQ{sq} tail doorbell {e['data']} outside 0..{QUEUE_DEPTH[sq]-1}"))
    return sorted(found)

violations = find_violations(events)
if violations:
    t, why = violations[0]
    print(f"FIRST abnormal event at t={t} ns: {why}")
    for t, why in violations[1:]:
        print(f"  later (possibly consequential) at t={t} ns: {why}")
else:
    print("no violations against this simple model")

Running it prints the out-of-range doorbell at 4 020 ns as the first abnormal event. Note what the model cannot see: whether the CplD for tag 0x11 arriving at 9 ms is a problem depends on the expected latency, which this model does not encode. A real checker needs a richer expected model, and it inherits every limitation of its input capture.

Common mistakes and anti-patterns

Debugging the loudest message
The controller reset is a consequence. Look 30 seconds earlier.
Confirmation bias
Searching only for evidence that supports the favourite hypothesis. Counter it by writing down, before each experiment, what result would refute it.
Shotgun changes
Updating firmware, BIOS and kernel together. The problem may vanish, and you will learn nothing; it may also come back.
Trusting a single instrument
Analyzers drop data at buffer limits; kernel logs are rate-limited; AER bits are sticky and do not count.
"Cannot reproduce" as a conclusion
It is an observation about your reproduction, not about the bug. Ask what differs between your setup and the reporter's.
Timeouts as fixes
Lengthening a timeout hides latency bugs and converts errors into stalls. Sometimes it is right (a slow but legal operation); it is never right without a mechanism.
Blaming the specification boundary
"NVMe says…" when the behaviour is PCIe, or vice versa. Name the document that governs the layer.
Over-fitting to the last bug
"Last time it was the cable." Past bugs inform priors; they are not evidence about this one.

Senior engineer insight

Seniors are not faster because they know more opcodes. They are faster because they spend the first hour on the timeline and the expected model, which makes every later hour productive. They also keep a running document with the five headings, updated as they go. That document becomes the bug report (see Engineering Bug Report Training), the root-cause write-up (see Root Cause, Ambiguity and Forensic Reporting), and the input to the regression test, without rewriting.

They are also explicit about the difference between "the specification requires X" (Normative), "I reason that X follows" (Derived) and "this product happens to do X" (Implementation-dependent). Most heated debugging arguments are two people using different categories without noticing.

Challenge

The controller wrote the CQE to 0x1_0000_2000; the host's Admin CQ is at 0x1_0000_1000. Explain two different ways this could happen and how the AQA/ACQ register writes, if present earlier in a longer capture, would distinguish them.

Hint

Row 1 is at 1 000 µs analyzer time and the kernel says 5.103200 s. Then ask: who decides where a CQE goes? The controller takes it from ACQ. What did the host write to ACQ, and when?

Solution

Offset: host = analyzer + 5.1022 s (5.103200 − 0.001000). The timeout log at 65.104 s corresponds to analyzer time ≈ 59.9 s, sixty seconds after the Identify was submitted (Linux's default admin timeout is 60 s, consistent).

First abnormal event: row 24. The CQE went to 0x1_0000_2000, but the Admin CQ base the host intended is 0x1_0000_1000 (slot 0). Rows 1–23 match expected behaviour (Identify data written to the PRP address, 16 × 256 B because MPS = 256). Row 25 (MSI-X) is normal; the interrupt arrives, the host's handler checks its CQ at 0x1_0000_1000, sees no new phase bit, and treats the interrupt as spurious.

Layer: NVMe controller register programming (ACQ) or controller use of it.

Hypotheses: H1 the host wrote ACQ = 0x1_0000_2000 (driver bug or stale value from a previous allocation); H2 the host wrote ACQ correctly but only the low or high 32 bits took effect (for example, two 32-bit writes in the wrong order or one dropped), so the controller uses a mixed value; H3 the controller latched ACQ incorrectly (controller bug); H4 instrument: the analyzer's address decoding or IOMMU translation view is misleading (the device emits IOVA; check it is the same address space the host programmed).

Cheapest separator: read ACQ back (offset 30h, a 64-bit register) with nvme show-regs: if it reads 0x1_0000_2000, H1 or H2; if it reads 0x1_0000_1000 and the CQE still goes to 0x...2000, H3. A longer capture showing the MWr(s) to BAR0+0x30 and 0x34 distinguishes H1 (one clean write of the wrong value) from H2 (partial writes).

Root cause (example conclusion): medium confidence until the readback is done. Regression test: after every controller enable, read back ASQ, ACQ and AQA and compare to the values written; issue Identify and require completion within 1 s.

What a senior engineer notices

The MSI-X arrived. A junior concludes "interrupts work, so the controller completed, so the driver lost it". A senior notices that "completed" and "completed where the host is looking" are different claims, and checks the CQE address against ACQ first.

Failure variants

  • CQE written to the right address with the wrong phase tag (host never sees a new entry).
  • CQE written correctly, no MSI-X (masked vector: see worked example 4).
  • No Identify data write at all, CQE with an error status (read the status; PRP problem).

Extension

Extend first_deviation.py with a rule "every CQE MWr address must lie within the CQ range programmed for that queue", reading ranges from the CC/ACQ writes and Create I/O Completion Queue commands in the same capture.

Quiz

  1. Which of these is an observation rather than an inference?

    • The controller hung.
    • The root complex dropped the request.
    • No CplD with Tag 0x2C appears within 50 ms of the MRd with Tag 0x2C.
    • The link is unstable.
    Answer and reasoning

    Only option 3 is directly checkable in the capture without any model of internal behaviour. The others claim mechanisms nobody observed.

  2. An NVMe I/O timeout appears in the kernel log at 42.5 s. Where should you look first for the first abnormal event?

    • At 42.5 s, the timeout itself
    • About one host timeout period earlier, around when the command was submitted
    • After the controller reset
    • In the SMART log
    Answer and reasoning

    The timeout fires when the host's timer expires; the command was submitted roughly one timeout period earlier (30 s by default on Linux for I/O). The first deviation is between submission and the expected completion.

  3. AER status registers tell you:

    • that an error class occurred at least once since the bits were cleared
    • how many times each error occurred
    • the exact time of each error
    • which NVMe command caused the error
    Answer and reasoning

    Status bits are sticky flags, not counters or timestamps. The Header Log captures the header of one logged TLP for certain errors.

  4. Which experiment best disambiguates "signal integrity at 16 GT/s" from "device firmware bug" for intermittent replays?

    • Rerun the same workload overnight
    • Force the link to 8 GT/s and rerun the same workload
    • Update the kernel
    • Read the SMART log
    Answer and reasoning

    Signal integrity margins improve sharply at lower data rates; a firmware bug in TLP construction would not care about the rate. Rerunning unchanged does not separate the hypotheses.

  5. Why is a single hypothesis a warning sign?

    Model answer

    It usually means the investigator stopped generating alternatives, so every observation gets interpreted in its favour (confirmation bias). Without competitors, no experiment is designed to discriminate, and the "root cause" is only as good as the first guess.

  6. What two checks verify a fix beyond "the symptom went away"?

    Model answer

    That a fresh capture no longer contains the first abnormal event (not just faster recovery), and that reverting the fix brings the failure back. Also check no new deviation appeared elsewhere.

  7. The analyzer sits between a PCIe switch and the SSD. It shows an MRd from the SSD with no completion. Name an instrument-related hypothesis and the evidence that tests it.

    Model answer

    The completion might have been dropped by the analyzer (buffer overflow, filter misconfiguration) or the trace might have wrapped. Check the analyzer's own statistics for dropped packets, check whether the device reported a Completion Timeout (if it did, the completion really was missing on the device's side of the link), or add a second probe.

  8. Which statement correctly separates root cause from contributing factor?

    • High queue depth is the root cause because the bug only occurs at QD 128.
    • The 30-second timeout is the root cause because it caused the stall.
    • The CID-reuse bug is the root cause; high queue depth is a contributing factor that makes it likely.
    • They are the same thing.
    Answer and reasoning

    Removing the CID-reuse mechanism removes the failure at any queue depth; reducing queue depth only makes it rarer.

  9. Why does the kernel log rarely contain the first abnormal event?

    Model answer

    The kernel logs what software detects, and detection usually happens through timers or error reports that fire after the hardware event. Many first deviations are absences or link-level events invisible to software.

  10. You used a reset as an anchor and computed a clock offset. What uncertainty must you record?

    Model answer

    The delay between the hardware event and the log timestamp (software latency, possibly milliseconds), and drift over long captures; record a ± range and, for long captures, use a second anchor.

Interview questions

  1. Beginner: What is the difference between an observation and an inference? Give an NVMe example.

    Model answer

    An observation is directly recorded ("CQE for CID 7 has status Invalid Field in Command"); an inference follows from observations plus rules ("so the controller rejected a field in the SQE; the host built an invalid command"). The inference can be wrong if the rule is misapplied; the observation can be wrong only if the instrument is.

  2. Beginner: Why look for the first abnormal event rather than the most severe?

    Model answer

    Severe events are often recovery actions triggered by an earlier quiet fault. Fixing the severe event fixes nothing.

  3. Intermediate: How do you correlate an analyzer trace with dmesg?

    Model answer

    Find an anchor visible in both (CC.EN write, link-down, a marker I/O), compute the offset, apply it, record uncertainty due to log latency and clock drift, and use a second anchor for long captures.

  4. Intermediate: A colleague says "AER shows Completion Timeout, so the SSD is broken." Respond.

    Model answer

    Completion Timeout is detected by the requester. If the SSD reported it, the SSD was the requester and did not receive a completion; the fault may be upstream (IOMMU, root complex, a switch, the link). We need to know which request timed out (Header Log, analyzer) and where it was lost.

  5. Advanced: Describe a disambiguating experiment for "data corruption caused by host driver" versus "data corruption caused by the controller".

    Model answer

    Capture the SQE and PRP list at submission (driver trace) and on the wire (analyzer), and the data MWr addresses. If the list is wrong at submission, the host is implicated; if the list is right and the controller writes elsewhere or wrong data, the controller is. Alternatively, swap drives with a different controller under the same driver build.

  6. Senior: How do you decide when an investigation has enough evidence to declare a root cause?

    Model answer

    When the mechanism explains every observation including the timing, competing hypotheses have been eliminated by experiment rather than by argument, a prediction from the mechanism was confirmed, and ideally the fix/revert experiment toggles the failure. Then state the confidence explicitly and what would change it.

  7. Senior: Your fix lengthens a timeout and the failure disappears. Is it done?

    Model answer

    Not until we know why the operation took longer than the original timeout. If the duration is legal and documented (for example, a long format or sanitize), the timeout was wrong and this is a real fix. Otherwise we have converted an error into latency and hidden the mechanism.

Teach-back challenge

  • To a child: explain why the detective child looked for the first broken thing on the track instead of the engine.
  • To a junior engineer: explain the five headings, using worked example 1.
  • To a senior engineer: argue for or against declaring "High" confidence in worked example 4, naming the experiment that would raise it to "Confirmed".
Model explanations

Child: "When lots of things go wrong, they usually go wrong one after another, like dominoes. The engine stopped because no power reached it, and no power reached it because the track joint was lifted. If you fix the engine, the train still won't go."

Junior: "We observed a doorbell value of 1024 and no fetch afterwards. We inferred that 1024 is out of range for a 1024-entry queue, so the controller had nothing valid to fetch. We hypothesised a wrap bug, a memory corruption, or an analyzer decoding problem. The evidence that separated them was that every bad value was exactly 1024 after 1023, which matched only the wrap bug, and the code confirmed it."

Senior: "High is justified: the mask bit was observed, the mechanism explains the 30-second delay, and H3 is refuted. To reach Confirmed, patch the reset path to unmask all configured vectors, rerun the idle/active reset test 1 000 times with zero 30-second completions, then revert and see it return."

Summary

Debugging is a method: symptom, timeline, expected behaviour, first deviation, layer, evidence, hypotheses, elimination, root cause, fix, regression test. At every step, keep observations, inferences, hypotheses, evidence and conclusions separate, and attach a confidence level to the conclusion. The first abnormal event is usually quiet, early, and in a different layer from the loudest error. Experiments must discriminate between hypotheses; a fix is verified by the disappearance of the first abnormal event, not only of the symptom; and every root cause ends in a regression test.

Do not move on until you can:

  • recite the eleven-step chain and say what goes wrong when each step is skipped
  • classify ten mixed statements into the five categories without hesitation
  • align an analyzer trace and a kernel log using an anchor, with an uncertainty range
  • find the first abnormal event in a trace, including one that is an absence
  • assign a first deviation to a layer and name the governing specification
  • write a hypothesis table with a disambiguating experiment for each pair
  • state a root cause with a confidence level and separate contributing factors
  • write a regression test that checks the mechanism, not only the symptom
