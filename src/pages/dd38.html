Correlation is the skill of turning a pile of TLPs and log lines into a list of NVMe commands, each with its full life story. Command-to-TLP Correlation gave you the chain of keys. This module covers the hard cases: captures that start late, doorbells that publish several commands, interrupts that cover several completions, two clocks that drift apart, packets the analyzer missed, and commands whose story contradicts itself.

Orientation

In Command-to-TLP Correlation you learned the correlation chain (doorbell address → queue; fetch address → slot; Requester ID and Tag → completion; SQ Identifier (SQID) and Command Identifier (CID) → command; buffer address → data; CQ slot → completion; vector → interrupt), the scope and lifetime of each key, and an algorithm that works when you have full context. Command vs Packet vs Completion drilled the difference between NVMe objects and PCIe objects. This module assumes both and goes further in three directions: recovering context you were not given, decomposing latency per command and per stage, and judging evidence quality (dropped packets, clock alignment, ambiguous attribution).

Needed first: Command-to-TLP Correlation (the chain and its keys), Command vs Packet vs Completion (four objects, two identifier systems), Doorbells (tail arithmetic), Physical Region Pages (which addresses a command may touch), and the first-abnormal-event discipline in Error Correlation.

Where this goes next: Teledyne LeCroy Trace Analysis and Automated Forensic Analysis turn this method into tool workflows; NVMe Errors and PCIe Errors use it to place error events on a command's timeline.

Kid story

A big train station has one camera on the main platform. It records every train that passes, but trains do not carry passenger names. One evening a lost suitcase is found, and a station detective has to work out which family it belongs to.

She knows the timetable (which platform serves which town), the ticket office log (who bought which ticket, and when), and the luggage tags (a number on each bag, re-used every day). From the video she can see a family board at 18:02 with three bags, and a porter's trolley arrive at 18:05 with bag tag 41. Tag 41 was also used yesterday by someone else, so the tag alone proves nothing. But tag 41 plus "18:05" plus "platform 3, the Riverside train" plus "the ticket office sold three Riverside tickets to the Parks at 17:55" points to one family.

The camera was switched on at 18:00, so anything earlier is missing. The station clock and the camera clock also disagree by a few seconds, and they drift.

What it hides. On a link, the "passengers" (NVMe commands) are inside the cargo: you must open the payload of the fetch completion to learn the CID and opcode, and the data TLPs carry no command identity at all, only an address. Also, a single "train" can carry several families (one fetch, several SQEs) and one "announcement" can cover several arrivals (one MSI-X, several CQEs).

Where it misleads. The story suggests the camera sees everything on its platform. Analyzers can drop packets when their buffers overflow or their filters are wrong, and they cannot see what happens inside the host or the SSD. And the detective's best match is still a hypothesis: the story's confident ending is exactly the habit this module tries to correct.

Mechanism

The algorithm of Chapter 41 assumed context. Here are the steps for the realistic case where you have a capture, some host tracing, and partial context. Each step says who acts, what changes, where, and what evidence it relies on. "Who" is the analyst; "what changes" is the analyst's model.

  1. Bound the evidence. Record the capture window, the analyzer's filter and trigger, and whether DLLPs were captured. Changes: the list of things you cannot see. Evidence: capture metadata; continuity of Data Link Layer sequence numbers in each direction (a jump means lost TLPs or an analyzer filter).
  2. Align clocks. Find two marker events seen by both host and analyzer, compute offset and drift (formula below). Changes: a mapping from analyzer time to host time with an uncertainty. Evidence: trace_marker writes paired with distinctive admin commands.
  3. Establish or infer context. If the capture contains initialisation, read context directly (ASQ/ACQ writes, Create I/O SQ/CQ SQEs, MSI-X table writes). Otherwise infer: SQ base = fetch address − first fetched slot × 64, with the first fetched slot = new tail − number of fetched entries (no wrap); CQ base = CQE address − slot × 16, with slot from the head doorbell that consumed it. Changes: context table, each entry marked observed or inferred. Evidence: doorbell values, fetch addresses and lengths, CQE addresses, head doorbell values.
  4. Classify every TLP by direction and address range: doorbell, register access, MSI-X table write, SQ fetch, PRP list fetch, data, CQE, interrupt, HMB, unknown.
  5. Pair requests and completions by (Requester ID, Tag), reassembling split completions with Byte Count. Keep tag lifetimes: a tag is free after the completion whose Byte Count equals its payload.
  6. Decode SQEs from fetch completions into command records keyed by (SQID, CID): opcode, NSID, LBA range, PRP1/PRP2 or SGL, and the slot. Check for stale payloads (a CID or opcode that matches the previous pass of the slot rather than a new command).
  7. Attribute data by address against outstanding commands' PRP ranges; fetch PRP lists and add their pages. If two outstanding commands' ranges contain the address, mark attribution ambiguous.
  8. Close commands with CQEs keyed by (SQID, CID); verify the phase against the expected phase for that CQ pass; record status and SQ Head Pointer (SQHD).
  9. Attribute interrupts: each MSI-X write covers every CQE posted to CQs on that vector since the previous MSI-X on that vector.
  10. Attribute head doorbells: each covers every CQE up to the new head.
  11. Check invariants and emit anomalies: data outside any range; CQE before its data; CQE for a non-outstanding (SQID, CID); duplicate outstanding (SQID, CID); fetch beyond the published tail; CQ overrun; missing stages. Give each anomaly a confidence.
thost = th1 + k × (tan − ta1), k = (th2 − th1) / (ta2 − ta1)
thost
the event's time on the host's monotonic clock
tan
the event's time on the analyzer's clock
ta1, th1
analyzer and host timestamps of marker 1 (the same physical event)
ta2, th2
analyzer and host timestamps of marker 2, taken later
k
rate ratio between the clocks (1 + drift); for example 1.000001 for 1 part per million

With one marker you can only remove the offset, and the error grows with distance from the marker: 1 ppm of drift is 60 µs after one minute, larger than the stage latencies you are trying to order. The marker's own uncertainty (how long between the trace_marker write and the doorbell it is paired with) adds a few microseconds either way.

The central concept on the ladder

The central concept is attribution under uncertainty: assigning each observed event to one command, with a stated basis and confidence, when context may be inferred, keys are reused and evidence may be incomplete.

Tag 41 alone does not tell you whose bag it is; tag 41 plus the time, the platform and the ticket log does, and you say how sure you are.

Each TLP is linked to a command through a chain of keys; every key is unique only for a while and in one place; when a key is inferred or two commands fit, the link is weaker and must be labelled.

The controller's queue engine, DMA engines and completion engine each create one kind of key (fetch address, tag, CQ slot); the host creates CIDs, buffer addresses and vectors; the analyzer timestamps and may drop.

NVMe Base defines CID uniqueness per SQ, the SQE/CQE fields and the queue arithmetic; the PCI Express Base Specification defines tag uniqueness per requester, completion matching and splitting. Neither defines how to attribute a posted write to a command: that is derived reasoning.

Only non-posted requests carry meaningful tags. Data for READs, CQEs and MSI-X are posted and identified only by address and payload. Doorbells are posted and identified by address and value.

In a capture: filter by address ranges; pair MRd/CplD by tag; decode payloads; build per-command timelines; watch DLL sequence numbers for gaps.

Bugs live in the anomalies: an orphan, a duplicate, a stale payload, a CQE before its data, a fetch without a doorbell. The first anomaly in time is the anchor of the investigation, and its confidence decides how hard you lean on it.

Verified rules you may rely on: a host must not reuse a CID in the same SQ until the earlier command with that CID has completed; a requester must not reuse a tag until the request is complete; completions for different tags may arrive in any order; completion queue entries are posted in completion order, not submission order; the phase tag inverts on each pass through a CQ.

Derived, not specified: attribution of posted writes by address range; context inference formulas; coalesced interrupt attribution (an MSI-X covers CQEs since the previous message on that vector); clock alignment; confidence categories. Label them as derived in any report.

Implementation-dependent: how many SQEs a controller fetches per MRd; whether it fetches across the ring end in one or two requests (it must not run past the queue end); when it fetches PRP lists; interrupt coalescing policy; the Linux driver's CID encoding (a generation counter in the upper bits on current kernels); analyzer buffer sizes and drop behaviour.

Version-dependent: 10-bit tags (PCIe 4.0 and later) and 14-bit tags in FLIT mode (PCIe 6.0 and later) widen the tag space; FLIT mode also changes Data Link Layer sequence handling, so gap detection must follow the revision in use. Linux tracepoint formats change across kernel versions.

No specification defines how to correlate, what confidence is, or how an analyzer must report lost packets. NVMe does not require controllers to fetch commands in the order they were published across different SQs, nor to complete them in order. Check in the specification: the arbitration mechanisms (round robin, weighted round robin with urgent priority) and the Arbitration Burst setting, which affect how fetches interleave across SQs.

Never use a PCIe tag to match a CQE or data write to a command. Never treat inferred context as observed. Never call an event "missing" without first checking the capture window and Data Link Layer sequence continuity. Never order two events from different clocks when their difference is smaller than the alignment uncertainty.

Ordering facts that make correlation possible

  • A fetch for slot s follows a doorbell that published s. A fetch that precedes any publishing doorbell (within the capture) means either the doorbell was before the capture or the controller fetched beyond the tail (a bug).
  • For a READ, the controller writes data, then the CQE, then (possibly) the MSI-X; posted-write ordering preserves this. For a WRITE, the controller's data reads all complete before its CQE.
  • Completions for one read request arrive in address order; across tags any order is legal.
  • The head doorbell follows the CQEs it acknowledges; the host may batch.

Detecting capture loss

Each direction of the link numbers TLPs with a 12-bit sequence number (outside FLIT mode), and ACK DLLPs name the latest acknowledged sequence number. If the analyzer captured DLLPs, a jump in TLP sequence numbers that is not explained by a NAK and replay means the analyzer did not record some TLPs. If only TLPs were captured, check the analyzer's own overflow indicators. Without either, "missing" must be written as "not observed".

Without correlation, an engineer would report "the SSD's read latency is 97 µs". With it, the report says "the SSD fetched late because it was full; the media read itself took 58 µs", which points at queue depth and arbitration (host queue depth per SQ, Weighted Round Robin settings, Arbitration Burst) rather than media. Confirm with host tracepoints: nvme_setup_cmd for (3, 0x3020) should align with row 1 within the alignment uncertainty.

Row 3's CID and opcode match the previous pass of slot 0x22, not anything the host published now (host tracepoint: nvme_setup_cmd qid 1 cmdid 0x2422 opcode Write). The controller did exactly what it should: fetch after the doorbell and execute what it read. The data writes in rows 4–19 are particularly dangerous: the buffer 0x2_0002_2000 belonged to the old command and may have been unmapped (IOMMU fault, data lost) or remapped to someone else (silent corruption). Root cause: missing ordering between the SQE stores and the doorbell MMIO write on the host. Evidence against alternatives: the doorbell value is right (not a tail bug); the fetch address is right (not a base bug); the payload is internally consistent (not link corruption, which would also fail LCRC). Fix: restore the barrier (use the platform's ordered MMIO write). Regression test: run with a pattern that writes slot contents just before the doorbell under maximum load on the weakly ordered platform, and have a correlation checker compare every fetched SQE with the host's tracepoint record for that slot.

Beginner lab — Recover queue context from a late capture

Objective

Prerequisites
Doorbells; this module's Mechanism step 3.
Setup
Context known: BAR0 = 0xFE60_0000, DSTRD = 0. Nothing else. Observed: (a) MWr32 0xFE60_1018 data 0x0000_0010; (b) MRd64 Addr=0x1_2344_0380 Len=32; (c) CplD for it, BC=128, two SQEs with CIDs 0x300E and 0x300F; (d) later, MWr64 to 0x1_2345_0040 with DW2 = 0x0003_000F, DW3 = 0x0001_300E; (e) MWr64 to 0x1_2345_0050 with DW3 = 0x0001_300F; (f) MWr32 0xFE60_101C data 0x0000_0006.
Procedure
  1. Which queue does (a) address? Which does (f)?
  2. How many SQEs did (a) publish, assuming (b) fetched all of them?
  3. Infer the SQ base.
  4. Infer the CQ base from (d), (e) and (f).
Questions
What assumption makes each inference fragile?
Expected reasoning
Doorbell index → queue; fetch length → entries; new tail − entries → first slot; base = address − slot × entry size.
Solution

(a) 0x1018: index (0x18)/4 = 6 → SQ3 tail. (f) 0x101C: index 7 → CQ3 head. (b) Len 32 DW = 128 B = 2 SQEs; new tail 0x10 → slots 0x0E and 0x0F (matching CIDs 0x300E/0x300F, a coincidence of this host's numbering, not a rule). SQ base = 0x1_2344_0380 − 0x0E × 64 = 0x1_2344_0380 − 0x380 = 0x1_2344_0000. CQ: (f) sets head to 6, so the two CQEs consumed were slots 4 and 5; (d) at 0x…0040 is slot 4 → base 0x1_2345_0040 − 4 × 16 = 0x1_2345_0000; (e) at 0x…0050 is slot 5, consistent. Also, (d)'s DW2 says SQID 3, SQHD 0x0F. Fragile assumptions: that (b) fetched all published entries (a controller may fetch in pieces); that no wrap occurred; that the head doorbell consumed exactly these two CQEs (if it consumed more, the slot numbers shift). Infer the SQ size from a later fetch that wraps (two MRds, one ending at an address and one starting at the base).

def run(trace):
    pending_db = {}          # sqid -> (time, new_tail)
    fetch = {}               # tag -> (time, addr, len_dw, sqid, first_slot)
    sq_base, cmds, open_cqes = {}, {}, {}
    tag_owner = {}           # data-read tag -> command key
    for t, d, typ, addr, ln, tag, pl in sorted(trace, key=lambda r: r[0]):
        if d == "H" and typ == "MWr" and BAR0 + 0x1000 <= addr < BAR0 + 0x2000:
            idx = (addr - BAR0 - 0x1000) // STRIDE
            q, is_cq = divmod(idx, 2)
            if not is_cq:
                pending_db[q] = (t, pl[0])
            else:
                for k, c in cmds.items():
                    if k[0] == q and "cqe" in c and "head_db" not in c:
                        c["head_db"] = t
        elif d == "D" and typ == "MRd" and pending_db and addr < 0x200000000 and tag not in fetch:
            # a fetch: attribute to the queue whose un-fetched doorbell is oldest
            q, (tdb, tail) = min(pending_db.items(), key=lambda kv: kv[1][0])
            n = ln * 4 // 64
            first = tail - n
            sq_base.setdefault(q, addr - first * 64)
            fetch[tag] = (t, q, first, tdb)
            del pending_db[q]
        elif d == "D" and typ == "MRd":
            for k, c in cmds.items():
                if c["page"] <= addr < c["page"] + 0x1000:
                    tag_owner[tag] = k
                    c.setdefault("first_data", t)
        elif d == "H" and typ == "CplD" and tag in fetch and pl:
            tf, q, first, tdb = fetch.pop(tag)
            for i in range(0, len(pl), 16):
                sqe = pl[i:i + 16]
                cid, opc = sqe[0] >> 16, sqe[0] & 0xFF
                page = (sqe[7] << 32) | sqe[6]
                cmds[(q, cid)] = {"opc": opc, "page": page, "doorbell": tdb, "fetch": tf,
                                  "slot": first + i // 16}
        elif d == "H" and typ == "CplD" and tag in tag_owner:
            cmds[tag_owner[tag]]["last_data"] = t
        elif d == "D" and typ == "MWr" and addr >= 0xFEE00000 and addr < 0xFEF00000:
            for k in list(open_cqes):
                cmds[k]["msix"] = t
                del open_cqes[k]
        elif d == "D" and typ == "MWr" and ln == 4 and pl:
            sqid, cid = pl[2] >> 16, pl[3] & 0xFFFF
            c = cmds.get((sqid, cid))
            if c is None:
                print(f"ORPHAN CQE ({sqid}, 0x{cid:04X}) at {t} ns")
                continue
            c["cqe"], c["status"] = t, (pl[3] >> 17) & 0x7FFF
            open_cqes[(sqid, cid)] = True
        elif d == "D" and typ == "MWr":
            for k, c in cmds.items():
                if c["page"] <= addr < c["page"] + 0x1000:
                    c.setdefault("first_data", t)
                    c["last_data"] = t
    return sq_base, cmds

Advanced lab — Align two clocks and order events correctly

Safe lab Paper or Python.

Objective
Compute offset and drift between analyzer and host clocks from two markers, then decide the order of host and link events.
Prerequisites
The alignment formula in Mechanism; Error Correlation on timebases.
Setup
Marker 1: analyzer 0.000 000 000 s ↔ host 812.400 120 s. Marker 2: analyzer 60.000 000 000 s ↔ host 872.400 180 s. Marker uncertainty ±3 µs each. Events: (A) analyzer 44.999 998 000 s, CQE for (2, 0x2190); (B) analyzer 45.000 004 000 s, MSI-X vector 2; (C) host 857.400 172 s, nvme_complete_rq qid 2 cmdid 0x2190; (D) host 857.400 160 s, AER correctable error reported for the root port.
Procedure
  1. Compute k.
  2. Map A and B to host time.
  3. Order A, B, C, D with uncertainties.
  4. Redo with marker 1 only (offset, no drift) and compare the conclusions.
Questions
  1. How long did the host take from interrupt to completion?
  2. Could the AER event be related to this command's completion, or did it happen afterwards?
Expected reasoning
Drift is 1 ppm; over 45 s it is 45 µs, which is larger than the gaps being judged.
Solution

k = (872.400180 − 812.400120) / 60 = 60.000060 / 60 = 1.000001 (1 ppm). A: 812.400120 + 1.000001 × 44.999998 = 812.400120 + 45.000043 = 857.400163 s. B: 812.400120 + 1.000001 × 45.000004 = 857.400169 s. Combined uncertainty about ±6 µs for cross-source comparisons (A and B are on the same clock, so their 6 µs spacing is exact).

Order with drift correction: D (857.400160) and A (857.400163) are 3 µs apart, inside the uncertainty: they are simultaneous for reasoning purposes. Then B (857.400169), then C (857.400172): the host completed the command about 3 µs after the interrupt, which is normal. Conclusion: the correctable error is contemporaneous with this command's completion and may be related (for example a replayed TLP in the same burst); it is worth checking the capture for a NAK near A.

Offset only: A maps to 857.400118 and B to 857.400124. Now C appears 48 µs after the interrupt (a "slow interrupt handler" that does not exist) and D appears 42 µs after the CQE (an error "unrelated to the command" that may well be related). Both conclusions are artefacts of ignoring 45 µs of drift. Lesson: over long captures, single-marker alignment creates false stories in exactly the microsecond range that matters.

Extension
Design a marker scheme that gives ±1 µs: for example, a host MMIO read of a harmless register immediately after trace_marker, triggering the analyzer on that MRd32.

Senior forensic lab — Lost packets or late start?

Safe lab Evidence analysis.

Objective
Decide whether a correlation anomaly is real, an artefact of capture loss, or a consequence of the capture window, and write the report with confidence levels.
Prerequisites
The ambiguous trace in this module; Root Cause, Ambiguity and Forensic Reporting.
Setup
Evidence (synthetic): a 500 ms capture taken during a fio run with queue depth 128 on 8 queues. The automated checker reports 37 "CQE without fetch" orphans, all in the last 120 ms. The analyzer's status shows "buffer 100 % at 380 ms". Device-direction sequence numbers show a jump from 0x5B2 to 0x9C0 at 381.2 ms with no NAK. Host tracepoints for the same period show every one of the 37 (qid, cmdid) pairs submitted between 381 ms and 500 ms (aligned) and completed with status 0.
Procedure
Classify the 37 orphans; decide whether any is a real anomaly; state what the capture can and cannot support.
Questions
  1. How many device TLPs were lost?
  2. Can you still use the capture after 381.2 ms?
  3. What should the checker have reported?
Expected reasoning
A sequence jump without NAK and an analyzer buffer-full indicator mean capture loss.
Solution

The jump from 0x5B2 to 0x9C0 means 0x9C0 − 0x5B2 − 1 = 0x40D = 1,037 device TLPs were not recorded (modulo 4096, assuming no wrap in the gap; if more than 4096 were lost, the arithmetic is ambiguous and the gap duration says how plausible that is). The buffer-full indicator corroborates capture loss. The host tracepoints show the 37 commands were submitted and completed normally. So all 37 are artefacts: their fetches were among the lost TLPs (or the analyzer switched to a degraded mode). After 381.2 ms, the capture can still be used for events that are fully inside the post-gap region if sequence numbers stay continuous there, but any command that straddles the gap is "partially observed". The checker should have computed sequence continuity first and reported "capture loss at 381.2 ms (1,037 device TLPs); 37 commands partially observed; 0 orphans proven". Confidence that no real anomaly exists among the 37: high, because host evidence shows normal submission and completion for each. Recommend recapturing with a narrower filter (exclude data payloads, keep headers) or a deeper buffer.

Extension
Specify the checker's output format so that "unknown" is a first-class result, with the reason (pre-capture, capture loss, missing context).

Debugging

Five correlation-driven failure patterns. In each, the correlation result itself is the evidence.

Pattern 1 — Orphan CQEs

SymptomChecker or host reports completions for (SQID, CID) pairs with no outstanding command.
Possible causesCapture started late or lost packets (artefact); stale SQE fetched (host ordering bug); duplicate CID (host); controller posting a completion twice; host processing a stale CQE (phase bug).
EvidenceCapture window and sequence continuity; fetched SQE payloads vs host tracepoints; CQE phase vs expected phase.
First checkIs the orphan inside the observed window with continuous sequence numbers?
Second checkDoes the CID match an earlier pass of the same slot (stale SQE) or another outstanding command (duplicate)?
Disambiguating experimentRecapture from before queue creation with DLLPs; add host tracepoints.
Likely conclusionArtefact if window/loss explains it; otherwise host bug (stale SQE, duplicate CID) or controller double completion, distinguished by the payloads.
ConfidenceHigh once the window is excluded and payloads compared.

Pattern 2 — Data outside every command's range

SymptomDevice data TLPs to addresses no outstanding command owns.
Possible causesHost Memory Buffer traffic; PRP list pages not yet attributed; command fetched before the capture; controller address bug; stale SQE pointing at an old buffer.
EvidenceHMB descriptor ranges; PRP list fetches; SQE payloads.
First checkExclude HMB ranges and PRP list pages.
Second checkFind the most recent SQE (current or previous pass) whose PRPs include the page.
Disambiguating experimentDisable HMB; repeat with host tracing of PRP construction.
Likely conclusionHMB (common, benign); stale SQE (host ordering); controller bug (rare, proven only when the SQE is correct and the address is not).
ConfidenceDepends on context completeness; state it.

Pattern 3 — Latency blamed on the SSD that belongs elsewhere

SymptomHigh host-measured latency; device datasheet latency is low.
Possible causesHost queueing before the doorbell; controller fetch delay under load (arbitration, internal slots); interrupt coalescing; host handler delay before head doorbell.
EvidenceStage latencies per command: host submit → doorbell, doorbell → fetch, fetch → data, data → CQE, CQE → MSI-X, MSI-X → head doorbell, head doorbell → host complete.
First checkWhich stage holds the excess?
Second checkDoes the excess correlate with load on other queues (arbitration) or with interrupt settings (coalescing)?
Disambiguating experimentReduce queue depth on other SQs; disable coalescing; pin interrupts.
Likely conclusionThe stage with excess names the owner.
ConfidenceHigh when clocks are aligned within a few µs.

Pattern 4 — Impossible orderings

SymptomCQE before its last data; MSI-X before its CQE; host completion before CQE; fetch before doorbell.
Possible causesReal ordering bug (Relaxed Ordering misuse, firmware); clock misalignment (cross-source comparisons); analyzer timestamping across lanes or directions with a skew.
EvidenceWhether both events are on the same clock and link direction; attribute bits; alignment uncertainty.
First checkSame clock? If not, is the gap larger than the alignment uncertainty?
Second checkSame direction on the same link? Then the order is real (posted writes in one direction are captured in transmission order).
Disambiguating experimentRecapture with two markers; force Relaxed Ordering off.
Likely conclusionSame-direction ordering violations are real controller bugs; cross-clock "violations" smaller than ε are not.
ConfidenceHigh for same-direction; low for cross-clock below ε.

Pattern 5 — The analyzer decodes nonsense after a reset

SymptomAfter a controller reset mid-capture, the NVMe decode shows wrong commands, invalid opcodes, or nothing at all.
Possible causesQueues recreated at different addresses or sizes; BAR moved; analyzer still applying old context.
EvidenceASQ/ACQ writes and Create I/O SQ/CQ commands after the reset.
First checkFind CC.EN 1 → 0 and the re-initialisation in the capture.
Second checkRebuild context from the post-reset queue creation.
Disambiguating experimentSplit the capture at the reset and decode the halves with separate contexts.
Likely conclusionStale analysis context, not a device fault.
ConfidenceHigh once new context decodes cleanly.

Interview

Beginner

  1. Which field ties a CQE to its command?

    Model answer

    The SQ Identifier and Command Identifier in the CQE (DW2 bits 31:16 and DW3 bits 15:0), which together identify the outstanding command.

  2. Why can't you use the PCIe tag to match a data write to a command?

    Model answer

    Data writes for READs are posted; their tag has no matching meaning. Tags only pair non-posted requests with their completions. Data is attributed by address.

  3. A doorbell moves the SQ tail from 5 to 8. How many commands were published?

    Model answer

    Three: slots 5, 6 and 7.

  4. Why do CQ slots not follow submission order?

    Model answer

    The controller writes CQEs as commands finish, and commands finish in any order.

  5. What is an orphan in a trace?

    Model answer

    An event that matches no outstanding request or command: a completion without a request, data outside every range, a CQE for an unknown CID.

Intermediate

  1. How do you attribute an MSI-X write when the controller coalesces?

    Model answer

    It covers every CQE posted to CQs that use that vector since the previous message on that vector.

  2. The fetch MRd length is 48 DW. What does that tell you?

    Model answer

    Three 64-byte SQEs in one request, from consecutive slots.

  3. How do you infer an SQ base without initialisation in the capture?

    Model answer

    From a doorbell's new tail and the fetch that follows: first fetched slot = new tail − number of entries fetched; base = fetch address − slot × 64. Mark it inferred.

  4. What does a head doorbell that jumps by 4 mean?

    Model answer

    The host consumed four CQEs before writing the doorbell; the doorbell acknowledges all four.

  5. How do you detect that the analyzer missed TLPs?

    Model answer

    Data Link Layer sequence numbers jump without a NAK and replay; the analyzer's own overflow indicators; host events with no link counterpart.

Advanced

  1. Why do you need two markers for clock alignment over a long capture?

    Model answer

    One marker removes only the offset. Clocks drift (parts per million), and over tens of seconds the error exceeds the microsecond gaps you need to order. Two markers give the rate ratio.

  2. How would you detect a stale SQE fetch?

    Model answer

    Compare each fetched SQE with what the host published for that slot (tracepoints) or check whether its CID and opcode equal the previous pass of the slot. A match with the previous pass means the controller read before the host's store was visible.

  3. What breaks correlation behind a PCIe switch?

    Model answer

    Upstream of the switch, several devices share the link, so Requester IDs must be filtered; timing includes switch queuing; and the capture may not see peer-to-peer traffic that stays below the switch.

  4. When is attribution of a data TLP ambiguous?

    Model answer

    When two outstanding commands' described ranges contain its address (buffer reuse, overlapping PRPs) or when the owning command's SQE was not observed. Report it as ambiguous.

  5. How do 10-bit tags change correlation?

    Model answer

    Up to 768 or 1,024 distinct tags (depending on rules) instead of 256, so tags recycle less often; the analyzer must decode the extra tag bits, otherwise pairings will be wrong.

Senior

  1. How do you write an automated correlation checker that is a trustworthy test oracle?

    Model answer

    It must verify capture integrity first (sequence continuity, window), separate observed from inferred context, report three outcomes (pass, fail, unknown) with reasons, and be validated by seeded faults (duplicate CID, stale SQE, CQE-before-data, orphans, loss).

  2. A customer sends a capture and says "your SSD completed a command we never sent". How do you respond?

    Model answer

    Ask for the capture window, DLL data and host tracepoints. Check whether the command was submitted before the window, whether capture loss occurred, and whether the fetched SQE was stale (host ordering) or duplicated. Only after those does "controller fabricated a completion" become a hypothesis.

  3. How do you report confidence in a correlation-based root cause?

    Model answer

    Per key: proven (unique key match), probable, consistent. Then state evidence for and against, what could precede the first abnormal event unobserved, and the experiment that would raise confidence.

  4. Why does stage latency matter more than end-to-end latency in vendor disputes?

    Model answer

    Because each stage has a different owner: host queuing, controller fetch policy, media, interrupt delivery, host handling. End-to-end numbers cannot assign responsibility; stage numbers can.

  5. Design a capture plan for a 1-in-a-million latency spike.

    Model answer

    Host-side detection triggers the analyzer (for example a tracepoint-based latency threshold that performs a distinctive MMIO access the analyzer triggers on), with a deep pre-trigger buffer, header-only capture to avoid overflow, DLLPs for loss detection, two-marker alignment at start and end, and full context from the last initialisation.

Quiz

  1. Which key matches a CplD to its MRd?

    • (SQID, CID)
    • (Requester ID, Tag)
    • Address
    • CQ slot
    Answer and reasoning

    Completions are routed and matched by Requester ID and Tag.

  2. A fetch MRd at 0x1_2342_0480 follows an SQ2 doorbell with new tail 0x13. Assuming one SQE was fetched, the SQ2 base is:

    • 0x1_2342_0480
    • 0x1_2342_04C0
    • 0x1_2342_0000
    • 0x1_2341_0000
    Answer and reasoning

    Slot 0x12; 0x12 × 64 = 0x480; 0x1_2342_0480 − 0x480 = 0x1_2342_0000.

  3. Two CQEs on CQ1 and one MSI-X on vector 1 follow. What is the right conclusion?

    • One interrupt covers both CQEs; not an error by itself
    • One interrupt is missing
    • The controller violated the specification
    • The second CQE is stale
    Answer and reasoning

    An MSI-X can cover several CQEs; coalescing or timing explains it.

  4. Device TLP sequence numbers jump from 0x120 to 0x150 without a NAK. What happened?

    • The SSD skipped sequence numbers
    • The link replayed
    • The root port rejected 47 TLPs
    • The analyzer did not record 47 TLPs
    Answer and reasoning

    0x150 − 0x120 − 1 = 47 missing in the capture; transmitters do not skip numbers, and replays reuse numbers.

  5. Clock drift is 2 ppm. After 30 s from a single marker, the alignment error from drift alone is about:

    • 0.6 µs
    • 6 µs
    • 60 µs
    • 600 µs
    Answer and reasoning

    30 s × 2 × 10⁻⁶ = 60 µs.

  6. Which observation most strongly indicates a stale SQE fetch?

    • The fetch came 0.7 µs after the doorbell
    • The fetched CID and opcode equal those of the previous pass of the same slot, not the host's new command
    • The fetch used tag 0x2E
    • The fetch length was 16 DW
    Answer and reasoning

    Only the payload comparison reveals staleness.

  7. Explain why a CQE for a command with no observed fetch is not automatically an orphan.

    Model answer

    The fetch may precede the capture window or have been lost by the analyzer. Check window timing against expected command duration and sequence continuity before calling it an orphan.

  8. Give the stages of a READ's latency decomposition and the owner of each.

    Model answer

    Host submit → doorbell (host software); doorbell → fetch (controller fetch policy, arbitration); fetch → first data (media and controller); first → last data (link and controller DMA); last data → CQE (controller); CQE → MSI-X (controller, coalescing); MSI-X → head doorbell (host interrupt path and handler); head doorbell → completion to application (host).

  9. What three outcomes should a correlation checker report, and why is the third essential?

    Model answer

    Pass, fail and unknown. Unknown is essential because missing context, capture loss and window edges are common; forcing them into pass or fail creates false confidence or false alarms.

Teach-back

  1. To a child. How does the station detective find the right family for the bag?

    Model explanation

    She does not trust the bag number alone, because numbers are reused. She puts together several clues: the time, the platform, the train, the ticket list. When they all point at one family, she is fairly sure. If the camera was off for part of the evening, she says "I don't know" for that part instead of guessing.

  2. To a junior engineer. How do you turn a capture into a command list?

    Model explanation

    Get or infer the context: BAR, doorbell stride, queue bases, vectors. Classify TLPs by address. Use doorbells to know how many commands were published, fetches to know which slots, tags to pair fetches with their completions, and the SQE payloads to get each (SQID, CID), opcode and PRPs. Attribute data by address range, close each command with its CQE's (SQID, CID), and attach interrupts and head doorbells. Then list the stage times and anything that does not fit.

  3. To a senior engineer. What makes a correlation result trustworthy?

    Model explanation

    Integrity of evidence (window, sequence continuity, buffer state), provenance of context (observed vs inferred), alignment with quantified uncertainty when sources are merged, keys used only within scope and lifetime, and explicit confidence per attribution. The anomalies are the product: orphans, duplicates, stale payloads and ordering violations, each with the reason it cannot be an artefact.
