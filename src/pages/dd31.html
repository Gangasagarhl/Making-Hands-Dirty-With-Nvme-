The WRITE chapters taught one write at a time. Real systems run dozens at once, on hosts with busy memory and an Input/Output Memory Management Unit (IOMMU) in the way, and they lose power at the worst moment. This module goes inside the data pull: how a controller keeps enough reads in flight, how it reassembles split completions, what it must do when a completion arrives poisoned, late or never, and what "atomic" and "ordered" really mean for overlapping writes.

Orientation

This module deepens the Non-Volatile Memory Express (NVMe) Write command from the point where Chapter 33 (WRITE) and Chapter 40 (End-to-End Write) stopped. Those chapters gave you the command fields, the single-command Peripheral Component Interconnect Express (PCIe) footprint (doorbell, SQE fetch, Memory Read requests, Completions with Data, Completion Queue Entry, interrupt) and the durability contract of the volatile write cache, Force Unit Access (FUA) and Flush. Here we assume all of that and push into four harder areas: the pull pipeline under concurrency, completion reassembly and its failure modes, atomicity and ordering between writes, and validation of the whole write path. The traces are new (concurrent writes, a host-latency-limited pull, a poisoned completion, overlapping writes) and the labs are harder (you will build a completion checker and a pipeline model, then work a forensic case).

Read first: WRITE (command fields, FUA, the durability table), End-to-End Write (the seventeen stages, Byte Count and Lower Address), PCIe Tags and Outstanding Requests, Completion Rules and Physical Region Pages. The companion READ module is Deep dive: READ.

Where it goes next: Deep dive: FLUSH takes the durability half further; Deep dive: DMA with NVMe generalises the pull and push engines; Deep dive: PCIe Completions and Deep dive: PCIe Tags give the PCIe-side rules in full.

Kid story

A mosaic artist is building a picture on a museum wall. Your class has made the coloured tiles, and they are lying on your desks. The artist cannot walk into the classroom; she sends a robot with request cards. Each card has a number and says "bring me the tiles from desk 3, row 2". She can send out sixteen cards at once, so sixteen robots are rolling around at the same time.

The classroom helpers fill each robot's tray. Sometimes a tray is too small, so a robot comes back in two or four trips, and each trip it says "this is card 7, and there are still 6 tiles to come for card 7". The artist must never mix up card 7's tiles with card 8's. Robots for different cards come back in any order, but the trips for one card always come back in order.

When every tile for a picture is in the artist's glass box, she rings a little bell: "Got it!" She glues the tiles on the wall later. Two rules: do not touch the tiles on your desk until you hear "Got it!", and if you ask for two pictures in the same spot at the same time, nobody promises which one ends up on the wall.

Analogy boundary

What it represents. Request cards are MRd Transaction Layer Packets (TLPs); card numbers are PCIe tags; robot trips are split CplDs; "still 6 tiles to come" is the Byte Count field; the glass box is the controller's write buffer or volatile cache; gluing to the wall is the NAND program; "Got it!" is the Completion Queue Entry (CQE) plus interrupt; the "same spot" rule is the absence of ordering between NVMe commands.

What it hides. Card numbers are reused within microseconds, so a tag identifies a request only while it is outstanding. The helpers are not free: every card passes through an address translator (the IOMMU) whose cache can miss, and a slow helper slows the artist even though the artist did nothing wrong. A tray can come back marked "poisoned" (the data was damaged in host memory), or not at all. And the artist's glass box can be full, in which case she stops sending cards altogether.

Where it becomes misleading. "Got it" sounds like a promise the picture is on the wall. It is not, unless FUA was set, no volatile cache exists, or a later Flush completed (Chapter 33). Also, real tiles cannot be changed on the desk while the robot is away; real memory can, and the controller may read the same byte twice. Finally, the story suggests the artist checks every tile; a controller checks packet framing and Byte Count, not whether your data is what you meant.

Architecture

The write path is a pipeline with four buffers that can each run out: host submission slots, PCIe tags, device receive (reassembly) buffer, and device write cache. Back-pressure travels from the last one to the first.

Mechanism

We follow two concurrent 4 KiB writes, CID 0x0210 and CID 0x0211, submitted with a single doorbell to SQ1 slots 0x20 and 0x21. This is the normal trace in the Trace view, step by step.

  1. Driver builds two SQEs. Who: NVMe driver on CPU 3. What changes: 128 bytes at 0x1_2340_0800–0x1_2340_087F; CID table marks 0x0210/0x0211 busy; the two 4 KiB buffers (IOVA 0x2_0010_0000 and 0x2_0010_3000) are mapped. Where: host DRAM and IOMMU page tables. Evidence: nvme_setup_cmd tracepoints with both CIDs.
  2. One doorbell. Who: CPU. What: SQ1 tail 0x20 → 0x22. Where: BAR0 + 0x1008 (MWr32). Evidence: analyzer row with Data=0x00000022. One doorbell for two commands is normal batching.
  3. SQE fetch, possibly batched. Who: queue manager. What: it may fetch both entries in one 128-byte MRd (Len=32 DW) or two 64-byte reads Implementation-dependent. Where: SQ1 ring. Evidence: MRd at 0x1_2340_0800, CplD with 128 bytes; Byte Count tells you which.
  4. Command contexts and buffer reservation. Who: controller firmware. What: allocates 4 KiB of reassembly/cache space per command; if unavailable, nothing further happens yet (back-pressure). Where: controller SRAM/DRAM. Evidence: only the gap between SQE CplD and first data MRd.
  5. Pull issue. Who: pull engine. What: 8 MRds per command (512 B each, MRRS limit), tags allocated from the pool; the two commands' requests may interleave. Where: upstream link. Evidence: 16 MRd64 rows with distinct tags and addresses inside each PRP page.
  6. Host serves reads. Who: root complex, IOMMU, memory controller. What: translate IOVA (IOTLB hit or walk), read DRAM coherently, form completions of at most MPS and split on RCB-aligned boundaries. Where: host. Evidence: MRd-to-first-CplD latency per tag.
  7. Reassembly. Who: pull engine. What: for each CplD, check tag outstanding, Byte Count equals remaining, Lower Address matches, payload ≤ MPS, EP clear; place payload; retire tag when Byte Count equals payload. Where: reassembly buffer. Evidence: CplD sequence per tag; any violation becomes an AER-logged error at the device.
  8. Commit decision. Who: cache manager. What: with cache enabled and FUA clear, data is "accepted"; with FUA or no cache, it must reach non-volatile media first. Where: device DRAM/NAND. Evidence: last-CplD-to-CQE gap (sub-microsecond to a few microseconds cached; hundreds of microseconds with FUA).
  9. CQEs, in completion order, not submission order. Who: completion engine. What: 16-byte MWrs into CQ1 slots 0x20 and 0x21 — CID 0x0211 may complete first. Where: CQ1 ring. Evidence: CQE MWr rows; slot order equals completion order, CID order need not.
  10. Interrupt and release. Who: controller then driver. What: MSI-X MWr32 to 0xFEE0_1000 data 0x41; driver matches CIDs, unmaps buffers, writes CQ1 head doorbell 0x22. Where: LAPIC window, CQ1, BAR0 + 0x100C. Evidence: MSI-X row, nvme_complete_rq, head doorbell row.
  11. Destage (invisible). Who: FTL and NAND back end. What: the 8 KiB join other dirty data in a program unit. Where: NAND. Evidence: none on the link; only later latency effects and Self-Monitoring, Analysis and Reporting Technology (SMART) counters.

The central concept on the ladder: the pull pipeline

The artist sends many numbered request cards at once, gets tiles back in trips, never mixes cards, and says "Got it" only when every tile of a picture is in her box.

For each Write the controller issues several read requests to host memory, keeps many outstanding across commands, rebuilds each request's data from one or more completions, and posts the CQE only after all data needed is inside the drive.

A tag allocator, a request table indexed by tag, a reassembly buffer sized for the worst-case outstanding bytes (the device advertises infinite completion credits, so it must reserve space before issuing), a cache allocator that can stall the pull, and a completion engine.

NVM Command Set: Write semantics, FUA, atomicity fields in Identify. NVMe Base: CQE, status codes such as Data Transfer Error, buffer rules for PRP/SGL. PCI Express Base Specification: MRd/CplD, MRRS, MPS, RCB, Byte Count, Lower Address, completion ordering within a request, EP, Unsupported Request, Completer Abort, Completion Timeout.

Upstream: header-only MRds, CQE MWrs, MSI-X. Downstream: CplDs carrying all the data, doorbell MWrs. Completions for one request arrive in increasing address order; completions for different requests may interleave in any order.

Look for: bursts of MRd with rising tags; CplDs whose Byte Count steps down (512, 384, 256, 128) per tag; interleaved tags; the gap from MRd to first CplD (host latency); the gap from last CplD to CQE (device commit).

Long MRd→CplD gaps convict the host; long SQE→first-MRd gaps convict device back-pressure; error completions or EP point at addresses or host memory; CQEs out of submission order are normal; overlapping writes with surprising results convict the host's ordering.

The sixteen questions for the Write command are answered in Chapter 33 and for the pulled write in Chapter 40; this module adds the pipeline and failure answers above and in the Debugging section.

The specifications do not say: how many reads a controller should keep outstanding; whether a controller must re-read the buffer or may read it twice; how long it may wait before pulling; what NVMe status reports a poisoned or timed-out pull; how a controller orders the commit of two overlapping writes; or whether a write larger than AWUPF tears at LBA granularity or at some larger internal unit. Check in the specification: the Status Field layout (SCT, SC, More, Command Retry Delay (CRD), Do Not Retry (DNR)); the value of Write Fault in the Media and Data Integrity Errors table; and the exact atomic-boundary rules (NABSN, NABO, NABSPF).

Never infer commit order from CQE order. Never treat a successful CQE as proof that the bytes the device read are the bytes you intended (the buffer could have changed). Never assume a controller that tolerated a malformed completion stream on one platform will tolerate it on another: tolerance is not a requirement.

An endpoint advertises infinite completion credits, so it may not refuse a completion. It therefore issues an MRd only when it has reserved space for the whole requested length. A controller with 64 KiB of reassembly space can never have more than 128 outstanding 512-byte reads, whatever its tag count. That number, not the 256 tags, is often the real pipeline limit Derived.

Completion: two different words

A PCIe completion answers one MRd; an NVMe completion (CQE) answers one command. A 128 KiB write produces 256 data MRds and, on a root complex that returns 128-byte completions, 1,024 PCIe completions — and exactly one CQE. When colleagues say "the completion was late", ask which kind.

Errors on the pull, and what the controller must do

  • UR or CA completion. The request ends with no data. The controller cannot complete the write successfully; it posts an error CQE (commonly Data Transfer Error) and must not commit partial data as if it were the full write. Whether it signals ERR_NONFATAL depends on whether it treats the event as an Advisory Non-Fatal error and on AER mask/severity settings Implementation-dependent.
  • Poisoned CplD. The payload is known bad. The controller must not program it as good data; it fails the command and logs "Poisoned TLP Received" in its AER status if AER is implemented.
  • Completion Timeout. Some bytes never arrive. The controller's timer (range in Device Control 2; the PCIe default range is 50 µs to 50 ms) expires; it logs the error, fails the command, and must ignore any late completion for that tag (which would be an Unexpected Completion).
  • Malformed completion (payload larger than MPS, Byte Count inconsistent). A malformed TLP is a receiver error the device reports; the command fails.

The controller may post the CQE only when it no longer needs the host buffer. Since the host may unmap and reuse the buffer the instant it sees the CQE, any data CplD for that command arriving after the CQE is a controller bug — and if the CplD arrives, the host has already lost the right to assume its buffer is unchanged Derived.

Analysis. The SSD did not damage anything: the root complex marked the data poisoned because the memory controller hit an uncorrectable error reading 0x2_0051_0300–0x2_0051_03FF (after IOMMU translation). The kernel log around the same instant should hold two records: a machine-check or memory-error report naming a physical address and DIMM, and an AER report from the root port for 0000:01:00.0 with "Poisoned TLP" in the device's uncorrectable status. The NVMe driver logs a write error for the request, the block layer may retry (DNR is clear), and a retry will poison again if the same page is reused. The correct conclusion is "host DRAM fault surfaced through a storage write"; the fix is memory replacement and page retirement, not an SSD return. Note the device's correct behaviour: it refused to commit and did not post success.

Analysis. Is the device wrong? The trace cannot tell you in which order the controller placed the two versions in its cache or on NAND; CQE order does not even imply that. What the trace can prove is decisive: both commands were outstanding at the same time (the second was submitted before the first completed). For that situation NVMe defines no order, so either final content is legal and the device is not at fault. The row marked abnormal is not a protocol violation but the first event that contradicts the host's (wrong) assumption. The root cause is in the host: a missing dependency between two writes to the same LBA (a cache-flush daemon racing an application write, or two threads of a user-space I/O library). Remaining ambiguity: had the trace shown the second write submitted after the first's CQE, the AAh result would be a genuine device bug (stale cache entry). That single fact — submission time of B versus completion time of A — decides the case. Confidence: high that the host is at fault given row 1 and the note under it.

Questions
(1) Why is PRP2 used although the write is only 4 KiB? (2) How many MRds and CplDs? (3) What gap would you expect between the last CplD and the CQE on a cached drive with no power-loss protection?
Expected reasoning
Decode CDW0 bit by bit before anything else: opcode (bits 7:0), FUSE (9:8), PSDT (15:14), CID (31:16). Only the PSDT value tells you how to read CDW6–9.
Solution

CDW0 = 0x02A7_4001: opcode 01h (Write); bits 9:8 FUSE = 00b; bits 15:14 of 0x4001 are 01 — so PSDT = 01b, which would select an SGL. That is the trap in this lab: the data-pointer Dwords look like two PRPs, but the command says SGL. On a controller with SGLS bits 1:0 = 00b this is rejected (Invalid Field in Command is the usual outcome; confirm the expected status). If you corrected the dump to the intended CDW0 = 0x02A7_0001, the rest decodes as: CID 0x02A7; NSID 1; PRP1 0x2_0071_0A00 (offset 0xA00); PRP2 0x2_0071_1000; SLBA 0x0012_3450; CDW12 = 0x4000_0007 → NLB 7 (8 blocks, 4 KiB) with FUA set. Pages touched: offset 0xA00 + 4096 → 2 pages (1,536 B in the first, 2,560 B in the second), so PRP2 is the second data page. MRds: first page 1,536 B = 3 × 512; second page 2,560 B = 5 × 512 → 8 MRds (0xA00 is a multiple of 512, so no extra partial request). CplDs at 256 B: 16. With FUA on a drive with a volatile cache and no power-loss protection, the gap from last CplD to CQE grows to a NAND program time (hundreds of µs in our synthetic drive). The meta-lesson: decode CDW0 bit-exactly before believing what DPTR "looks like".

Extension. Change PRP1 to 0x2_0071_0A40 and recount, assuming the controller cuts requests only at MRRS and 4 KiB boundaries: the page split becomes 1,472 + 2,624 B, giving 2 full requests and one of 448 B in the first page, and 5 full requests and one of 64 B in the second — 9 MRds instead of 8. A controller that re-aligns its requests to 512-byte addresses would issue a different, equally legal pattern Implementation-dependent.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""cpl_reassembly.py - check Completions with Data against the Memory Read requests they answer.

Synthetic teaching model (standard library only). Lab-system limits: MPS 256 B, RCB 64 B.
Each completion is (tag, byte_count, lower_addr, payload_bytes, status, ep).
"""
MPS, RCB = 256, 64

def check(requests, completions):
    """requests: {tag: (address, length)}. Returns a list of findings, first abnormal first."""
    done = {tag: 0 for tag in requests}
    live = set(requests)
    findings = []
    for i, (tag, bc, la, n, status, ep) in enumerate(completions, 1):
        where = f"cpl #{i} tag 0x{tag:02X}"
        if tag not in live:
            findings.append(f"{where}: unexpected completion (tag not outstanding)")
            continue
        addr, length = requests[tag]
        if status != "SC":
            findings.append(f"{where}: error completion {status}; request fails, no data")
            live.discard(tag)
            continue
        remaining = length - done[tag]
        if bc != remaining:
            findings.append(f"{where}: Byte Count {bc} but {remaining} bytes remain")
        if la != (addr + done[tag]) & 0x7F:
            findings.append(f"{where}: Lower Address 0x{la:02X}, expected 0x{(addr + done[tag]) & 0x7F:02X}")
        if n > MPS:
            findings.append(f"{where}: payload {n} B exceeds MPS {MPS} B (malformed)")
        if ep:
            findings.append(f"{where}: poisoned (EP=1); data must not be committed as good")
        done[tag] += n
        if n < bc and (addr + done[tag]) % RCB:
            findings.append(f"{where}: partial completion ends at 0x{addr + done[tag]:X}, not RCB-aligned")
        if done[tag] >= length:
            live.discard(tag)
    for tag in sorted(live):
        findings.append(f"tag 0x{tag:02X}: {requests[tag][1] - done[tag]} bytes never arrived -> Completion Timeout")
    return findings or ["all requests satisfied; no findings"]

if __name__ == "__main__":
    reqs = {0x40: (0x2_0010_0000, 512), 0x41: (0x2_0010_0200, 512)}
    good = [(0x40, 512, 0x00, 128, "SC", 0), (0x41, 512, 0x00, 64, "SC", 0),
            (0x41, 448, 0x40, 64, "SC", 0), (0x40, 384, 0x00, 128, "SC", 0),
            (0x41, 384, 0x00, 128, "SC", 0), (0x40, 256, 0x00, 256, "SC", 0),
            (0x41, 256, 0x00, 256, "SC", 0)]
    bad = [(0x40, 512, 0x00, 256, "SC", 0), (0x41, 512, 0x00, 200, "SC", 0),
           (0x40, 256, 0x00, 256, "SC", 1), (0x42, 64, 0x00, 64, "SC", 0)]
    for name, stream in (("good", good), ("bad", bad)):

            print("  " + f)

Observations

Lower Address is 7 bits. A non-final split must end on an RCB boundary; the final one ends wherever the request ends. A completion for a non-outstanding tag is an Unexpected Completion: discard and log, never place the data.

Advanced lab — how deep must the pull pipeline be?

Safe lab Python 3, standard library.

Objective
Quantify how outstanding-read depth and host round-trip time limit write throughput, and connect the numbers to Trace M31-S.
Prerequisites
Little's Law (calculators), the formula in Chapter 33.
Setup
Save pull_pipeline.py.
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""pull_pipeline.py - how many outstanding Memory Reads does a WRITE data pull need?

Synthetic teaching model (standard library only). The controller pulls TOTAL bytes in
requests of REQ bytes, keeping at most k requests outstanding. Each request's data comes
back RTT ns after issue, but the downstream link can only deliver LINK bytes per ns, so
completions queue behind each other. A tag is reused as soon as its last byte arrives.
"""
import heapq

def pull_time_ns(total, req, k, rtt, link):
    n = total // req
    ser = req / link                 # ns the link is busy delivering one request's data
    link_free = 0.0
    slots = [0.0] * k                # times at which each tag becomes free
    heapq.heapify(slots)
    finish = 0.0
    for _ in range(n):
        issue = heapq.heappop(slots)                 # wait for a free tag
        start = max(issue + rtt - ser, link_free)    # data streams in at the end of the round trip
        done = start + ser
        link_free = done
        finish = max(finish, done)
        heapq.heappush(slots, done)
    return finish

if __name__ == "__main__":
    TOTAL, REQ, LINK = 128 * 1024, 512, 7.0   # 128 KiB write, MRRS 512 B, ~7 GB/s = 7 B/ns
    print("k   " + "".join(f"RTT={r:>5} ns   " for r in (600, 1000, 3000)))
    for k in (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64):
        row = []
        for rtt in (600, 1000, 3000):
            t = pull_time_ns(TOTAL, REQ, k, rtt, LINK)
            row.append(f"{t / 1000:6.1f} us {TOTAL / t:4.2f}GB/s")
        print(f"{k:<4}" + "  ".join(row))
Procedure
(1) Run it. For RTT 600 ns, find the smallest k that reaches ≈6.8 GB/s. (2) Repeat for 3,000 ns. (3) Change REQ to 256 (an MRRS of 256) and repeat.
Observations
Record the knee of each curve: the k beyond which throughput stops rising.
Questions
(a) Derive the knee from k ≥ RTT × link ÷ REQ. (b) Why does halving MRRS double the needed k? (c) A controller has 256 tags but a 32 KiB reassembly buffer; what is its effective k at MRRS 512?
Expected reasoning
Little's Law: bytes in flight = rate × round trip.
Solution

The script prints (values rounded): at RTT 600 ns, k = 8 gives 6.65 GB/s and k = 16 saturates at 6.81 GB/s; at RTT 1,000 ns, k = 16 reaches 6.67 GB/s; at RTT 3,000 ns, k = 16 gives only 2.67 GB/s (49.1 µs) and even k = 64 reaches 6.05 GB/s. (a) Knee ≈ 600 × 7 ÷ 512 ≈ 8.2 and 3,000 × 7 ÷ 512 ≈ 41. (b) Each request carries half the bytes, so twice as many must be in flight for the same bytes in flight. (c) 32 KiB ÷ 512 B = 64 requests; the buffer, not the tag pool, is the limit.

Extension. Give RTT a distribution (for example 90% at 600 ns and 10% at 5 µs for IOTLB misses) using random with a fixed seed, and show that tail round trips cost far more than their share.

Senior forensic lab — "the SSD corrupts our log segments"

Safe lab Evidence analysis only.

Objective
Work an incident from mixed evidence to a defensible root cause and a fix owner.
Prerequisites
This module; PRP/SGL Debugging Lab; Debugging Methodology.
Setup (the evidence pack, synthetic)
  • A logging service built on a user-space NVMe driver writes 64 KiB segments with a per-512-byte checksum. About 1 in 2 million segments fails verification on read-back: the bad region is always a whole 4 KiB page, and its content is a valid page from a later segment.

  • An analyzer capture of one failing segment shows the 128 pulls for the segment's 16 pages; one page (the 9th) is pulled 31 µs after the doorbell, while the others are pulled within 6 µs. The CplD payload for that page already contains the later segment's data.
  • The failure rate rises sharply when the drive is near full and sustained writes run.

Observations
Separate "what the device did" from "what the host allowed".
Questions
(1) Is a 31 µs delay before pulling one page legal? (2) Why does it correlate with full-drive sustained writes? (3) Why are there no errors anywhere? (4) What test would have caught this before release?
Expected reasoning
Buffer ownership lasts until the CQE; the device chose to pull late; the host changed the buffer in between.

Timeline: doorbell → SQE fetch → most pages pulled within 6 µs → driver recycles the buffer (the recent change) and fills it with the next segment → controller pulls page 9 at 31 µs and receives the new content → success CQE. Debugging

Six write failure patterns. Each is written as symptom → possible causes → evidence → first check → second check → disambiguating experiment → likely conclusion → confidence. Patterns covered in Chapter 33 (simple Data Transfer Error from an unmapped IOVA, durability loss from missing Flush) are only cross-referenced here.

Write throughput plateaus well below link rate

Symptom
Sequential 128 KiB writes stall at 2–4 GB/s on a Gen4 x4 link; reads reach ≈7 GB/s.
Possible causes
Host read latency (IOMMU, memory congestion, remote NUMA node); device outstanding-read limit; small MRRS; device back-end limit; cache full (steady state).
Evidence
Analyzer: MRd→first CplD latency; number of outstanding MRds; link utilisation per direction.
First check
lspci -vvv MaxReadReq: 128 B instead of 512 B quadruples the needed depth.
Second check
Is throughput low from the first second (pipeline) or only after minutes (cache/garbage collection)?
Disambiguating experiment
Repeat with buffers on the SSD's local NUMA node and huge pages; if throughput rises with no device change, the host round trip was the limit.
Likely conclusion
Round-trip × depth limit (Trace M31-S) when latency is high and outstanding count is flat at a fixed value.
Confidence
High once both MRd latency and outstanding count are measured; medium from host-side measurements only.

Write fails with a poisoned completion

Symptom
Occasional write errors (Data Transfer Error or Internal Error) with AER "Poisoned TLP" on the SSD.
Possible causes
Host DRAM uncorrectable error; poisoned data forwarded from another device (peer-to-peer); a switch or root complex fault.
Evidence
Kernel MCE/EDAC lines; AER status of device and root port; analyzer EP bit.
First check
Memory-error log at the same timestamp, with a physical address.
Second check
Does the same physical page fail repeatedly?
Disambiguating experiment
Offline the suspect page or move the workload to another socket; errors follow the memory, not the SSD.
Likely conclusion
Host memory fault (Trace M31-F).
Confidence
High with a matching memory-error record; medium without.

Write hangs, then a Completion Timeout on the device

Symptom
A write takes the driver's I/O timeout (30 s default on Linux), Abort fails, controller reset follows; AER shows Completion Timeout on 0000:01:00.0.
Possible causes
Root complex or switch dropped a completion; a completion arrived but was malformed and discarded; device lost track of a tag (firmware); link recovery that lost a TLP (should be impossible with a healthy Data Link Layer).
Evidence
Analyzer: an MRd with no CplD for its tag; any Replay or NAK DLLPs nearby; device AER status.
First check
Find the MRd without completions and the time it was sent.
Second check
Was a completion sent but rejected (malformed, wrong Requester ID or tag)? Check the root port side of the capture too.
Disambiguating experiment
Capture both sides of a switch; a completion seen upstream but not downstream convicts the switch.
Likely conclusion
Usually platform (switch/RC) or device tag bookkeeping; rarely the link.
Confidence
Medium until the missing completion is localised to a hop.

Corrupt data with success status, whole pages from other writes

Symptom
Read-back verification fails on whole 4 KiB pages containing other valid data; no errors anywhere.
Possible causes
Host buffer reuse before CQE; PRP list slot reuse (Chapter 38, Case 2); overlapping writes in flight; device mapping bug.
Evidence
Content of the bad page (which later write?), CplD payloads in a capture, driver buffer lifecycle.
First check
Is the bad content from a write submitted later than the victim (reuse) or to the same LBA (overlap)?
Second check
Does the failure rate rise with device pull latency (full drive, sustained writes)?
Disambiguating experiment
Poison buffers right after CQE and delay recycling; failures disappear if reuse was the cause.
Likely conclusion
Host buffer-ownership bug (senior forensic lab).
Confidence
High when the CplD payload already contains the wrong data.

Torn data after power loss

Symptom
After a power cut, a 16 KiB database page reads back with the first 8 KiB new and the last 8 KiB old.
Possible causes
Write larger than AWUPF/NAWUPF or crossing an atomic boundary; application relying on atomicity the device never promised; device not honouring its stated unit.
Evidence
Identify values (AWUPF, NAWUPF, NABSPF, NABO); the write's SLBA and size; whether the write was in flight at the cut.
First check
Compute whether the write fit within the power-fail atomic unit and did not cross a boundary.
Second check
Was the write completed (and flushed) before the cut? A completed and flushed write must not tear at all.
Disambiguating experiment
Power-cut test with writes exactly at, and one block above, the atomic unit, aligned and misaligned; count torn outcomes per class.
Likely conclusion
Usually the application assumption (16 KiB pages on a device with AWUPF of one block); a device bug only if tears occur within the unit.
Confidence
High for the arithmetic; medium for device behaviour until power-cut statistics exist.

CQE arrives before the last data completion

Symptom
Rare corruption of the tail of large writes; analyzer shows a data CplD for a command's address range after that command's CQE MWr.
Possible causes
Controller firmware posting completion on "last MRd issued" instead of "last CplD received"; analyzer timestamping across two capture ports (false alarm).
Evidence
Single-port capture with both directions on one time base.
First check
Confirm the timestamps come from one time base.
Second check
Is the late CplD's tag one of this command's requests?
Disambiguating experiment
Slow the host's read latency deliberately (busy memory); a real bug becomes more frequent.
Likely conclusion
Controller bug violating the CQE-after-data rule.
Confidence
High once the time base is trusted.

Interview

Beginner

  1. In an NVMe Write over PCIe, which packets carry the user data, and in which direction?

    Model answer

    Completions with Data travelling host-to-device, answering Memory Read requests the controller sends upstream. Host MWrs carry only doorbells.

  2. What is NLB = 15 in bytes on a 512-byte-LBA namespace?

    Model answer

    NLB is 0's based: 16 blocks × 512 B = 8 KiB.

  3. Why can the host not reuse a write buffer right after ringing the doorbell?

    Model answer

    The controller may read the buffer any time until it posts the CQE, possibly late or more than once; ownership returns only at completion.

  4. What does FUA guarantee and what does it not?

    Model answer

    This command's data and metadata are on non-volatile media before completion. It does not flush other cached data and does not order commands.

  5. Two writes are in flight; the second completes first. Is that a bug?

    Model answer

    No. NVMe defines no completion order between commands.

Intermediate

  1. A 4 KiB write is pulled with 8 MRds of 512 B; the root complex returns 128-byte completions. Give the Byte Count sequence for one request.

    Model answer

    512, 384, 256, 128 — each value is bytes remaining including the current completion. 32 CplDs for the 4 KiB.

  2. Why is the PCIe tag limit often not the limit on outstanding reads?

    Model answer

    The device must reserve receive space for every outstanding request because it advertises infinite completion credits; its reassembly buffer size divided by request size is usually smaller than 256.

  3. How do you attribute interleaved MRds to commands in a trace without an NVMe-aware decoder?

    Model answer

    Decode each SQE's PRP/SGL from the SQE fetch CplD, then map each MRd address into those pages or descriptors; tags are only valid while outstanding.

  4. What is in the gap between the SQE completion and the first data MRd, and when is it long?

    Model answer

    Command decoding, buffer/cache reservation, PRP list fetch. It grows under cache back-pressure in steady-state writes.

  5. What does AWUPF = 7 mean on a 512-byte-LBA namespace with no namespace-specific value?

    Model answer

    0's based: 8 blocks = 4 KiB. A write of up to 4 KiB that does not cross an atomic boundary is all-old or all-new after power failure.

Advanced

  1. Derive the outstanding-read depth needed for 7 GB/s at a 1.5 µs host round trip with MRRS 512.

    Model answer

    Bytes in flight = 7×10⁹ × 1.5×10⁻⁶ = 10,500 B; ÷ 512 ≈ 21 requests.

  2. A CplD arrives with EP=1 during a write pull. What must the controller do, and what does the host see?

    Model answer

    Not commit the data as good, drain remaining completions, fail the command with an error status (implementation's choice, often Data Transfer Error), log Poisoned TLP Received and signal per AER configuration. The host sees an error CQE, AER logs, and likely a memory-error record.

  3. Why does an IOMMU hurt write throughput more than read throughput?

    Model answer

    Translation latency adds directly to the non-posted MRd round trip the device waits on; posted read-data writes are buffered by the root complex and only consume credits.

  4. How would you tell from a trace whether a slow write is device- or host-limited?

    Model answer

    Split time into SQE fetch, SQE→first MRd (device), MRd→CplD (host), last CplD→CQE (device). Long MRd→CplD with a flat outstanding count convicts the host; long device gaps convict the device.

  5. What evidence decides whether overlapping-write results are a device bug?

    Model answer

    Whether the second write was submitted after the first completed. If both were in flight, any result is legal; if strictly serialised, stale data is a device bug.

Senior

  1. Design a validation plan for the write pull of a new controller.

    Model answer

    Exerciser-driven completions at every legal split (RCB multiples, 4…256 B), interleaving across tags, maximum outstanding, injected UR/CA/EP/no-completion per request position (first, middle, last), malformed completions, late unexpected completions, MRRS 128–4096, 10-bit tags, plus host-level PRP/SGL alignment matrix, MDTS-size writes, overlapping writes and power-cut atomicity. Pass criteria: no success CQE with bad data, correct error status, no buffer access after CQE, no hang.

  2. A customer says "your SSD corrupted data" with no errors logged. How do you run the investigation?

    Model answer

    Characterise the corruption (granularity, content provenance, timing), collect driver tracepoints and, if possible, a capture; test the host invariants (buffer ownership, ordering, flush) before device hypotheses; reproduce with signature-writing tools; only then examine firmware.

  3. What is the controller's correct behaviour if power fails mid-pull?

    Model answer

    The command has not completed, so the host has no durability claim; the LBAs must read back as old or new within the atomic unit and boundary rules, and may be mixed above them. The controller must never expose a partially pulled LBA (a single LBA cannot be half written).

  4. Should a driver choose FUA or a following Flush for a commit record?

    Model answer

    Flush (or preflush) to make prior data durable, then FUA on the commit record so it alone pays the program cost — the Linux REQ_PREFLUSH|REQ_FUA pattern. On a device without volatile cache both are no-ops.

  5. How do reassembly buffer size, MRRS and host latency interact in a product spec review?

    Model answer

    Required bytes in flight = target rate × worst realistic round trip (including IOMMU misses and switches); the buffer must exceed it with margin; smaller MRRS raises request count and header overhead but not bytes in flight; validate on the slowest supported platform, not the reference board.

Quiz

  1. A non-final split completion for a 512-byte request ends at address 0x…02C8 on a platform with RCB 64 B. This is:

    • Legal, because it is smaller than MPS
    • A violation: non-final splits must end on an RCB-aligned address
    • Legal only with Relaxed Ordering
    • Legal if the Byte Count is correct
    Answer and reasoning

    0x2C8 is not a multiple of 64; only the final completion of a request may end off an RCB boundary.

  2. Which agent sets EP=1 on a CplD during a write pull?

    • The SSD controller
    • The NVMe driver
    • The completer (root complex), typically after an uncorrectable host memory error
    • The IOMMU on a translation fault
    Answer and reasoning

    The completer marks known-bad data. An IOMMU fault produces UR/CA, not poisoned data.

  3. CQEs for CIDs 0x0211 then 0x0210 appear, though 0x0210 was submitted first. What can you conclude about media content if both wrote different LBAs?

    • 0x0211's data is durable, 0x0210's is not
    • The controller is non-compliant
    • 0x0210 was retried
    • Nothing about durability or commit order; completion order is not defined
    Answer and reasoning

    Completion order carries no ordering or durability meaning.

  4. Host round trip 2 µs, MRRS 512 B, target 7 GB/s. Minimum outstanding reads?

    • 14
    • 28
    • 56
    • 256
    Answer and reasoning

    7×10⁹ × 2×10⁻⁶ = 14,000 B; ÷ 512 ≈ 27.3 → 28.

  5. Which status best signals a failed data pull (UR on a data MRd) on most controllers?

    • Generic status 04h, Data Transfer Error
    • LBA Out of Range
    • Invalid Namespace or Format
    • Write Fault
    Answer and reasoning

    Data Transfer Error describes a transfer failure; Write Fault describes failure to commit to media. The exact choice is implementation-dependent, which is why the trace matters.

  6. A device reports NAWUPF = 3 (512-byte LBAs). Which write is guaranteed not to tear on power loss, assuming no boundary is crossed?

    • A 4 KiB write
    • A 3 KiB write
    • A 2 KiB write
    • Any FUA write
    Answer and reasoning

    0's based: 4 blocks = 2 KiB. FUA governs durability on completion, not atomicity of an in-flight write.

  7. Why does a READ trace almost never show Completion Timeouts for user data, while a WRITE trace can?

    Model answer

    Read data travels as posted MWrs, which have no completions; write data is fetched with non-posted MRds, each of which can time out.

  8. In Trace M31-S, what single measurement best separates "device slow" from "host slow"?

    Model answer

    The MRd→first-CplD latency per tag (host) compared with SQE→first-MRd and last-CplD→CQE (device).

  9. Give two reasons a controller might read part of a write buffer twice.

    Model answer

    Internal retry after a reassembly or internal ECC error; firmware that pulls once to compute protection information and again to store; a reset/abort path re-executing. Hence buffers must stay stable until the CQE.

  10. What is the difference between Data Transfer Error and Write Fault in diagnosis?

    Model answer

    Data Transfer Error points to the host transfer (addresses, IOMMU, poisoned data); Write Fault points inside the device (media commit failure). Look for PCIe/IOMMU evidence for the former, SMART/media logs for the latter.

Teach-back

  1. To a child: explain why the mosaic artist must not have you change the tiles on your desk until she says "Got it".

    Model explanation

    The artist's robots might still be on their way to your desk. If you swap a tile, the robot brings the new one, and the picture on the wall ends up with the wrong tile even though the artist did everything right.

  2. To a junior engineer: explain how a write's data reaches the drive and why the trace shows four completions per request.

    Model explanation

    The controller asks for the data with Memory Read requests of up to MRRS, here 512 bytes, each with a tag. The root complex answers with Completions with Data no larger than MPS and may split them at RCB multiples; this platform splits at 128 bytes, so four completions with Byte Counts 512, 384, 256 and 128 answer each request. The controller rebuilds the data by tag, and posts the CQE only after the last byte has arrived.

  3. To a senior engineer: argue whether a given write-throughput regression belongs to the SSD or the platform.

    Model explanation

    Write throughput is bytes in flight divided by round trip. The capture shows the controller holding a constant 16 requests outstanding and reissuing tags within 100 ns of retirement, with commit gaps under a microsecond, so its behaviour is unchanged. The MRd→CplD round trip rose from 0.6 to 3.1 µs, consistent with IOTLB pressure since list reads stayed fast. That predicts 2.6 GB/s, which is what we measured. Platform owns it; the SSD's only lever would be a deeper pipeline, which we can request as an enhancement but not as a defect fix.
