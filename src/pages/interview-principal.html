Who this bank is for and how to use it

This is the last of five interview banks. It is written for engineers who are already comfortable reading a Transaction Layer Packet (TLP) trace, decoding a Submission Queue Entry (SQE) and following a Linux error log, and who now get asked a different kind of question: not "what is this field?" but "what should we build, buy, measure or change, and how would you know you were wrong?" Principal engineers in storage and interconnect teams own those questions for a platform, a product line or a fleet of hundreds of thousands of drives.

A principal answer has a recognisable shape. It states the decision or diagnosis in one sentence; names the options and what each one costs; picks one and gives the reason; names the evidence that would reverse the decision; and is honest about what the specification says, what is implementation-dependent and what the candidate would confirm before relying on it. Interviewers at this level care much more about judgement and evidence than about recall. Getting a bit position wrong costs little if you said you would confirm it; inventing one with confidence costs a lot.

A principal engineer is like the person who designs the whole railway network, not the driver of one train. When a train is late they do not just ask "which train?" PCIe link, physical layer and roadmap

Questions P1–P16 cover channel design, link health at fleet scale, PCIe generation choices and the qualification work that keeps physical-layer problems out of production. Expect to be asked to choose, not just to explain.

  1. [Architecture] Your server places a Gen5 x4 U.2 bay at the end of a long backplane path with two connectors. The signal-integrity team offers a redriver or a retimer. Which do you choose, and what does each choice do to debugging?

    Model answer

    A redriver is an analog amplifier with equalization: it boosts the signal but also amplifies noise and does not reset accumulated jitter, and it is invisible to the protocol. It cannot be guaranteed to work at 32 GT/s over a loss budget that already exceeds what the host and device equalizers can recover, and its tuning is static. A retimer is protocol-aware at the physical layer: it recovers the clock and data, retransmits a clean signal, participates in link training and equalization on both sides, and splits the channel into two separately equalized segments. The PCI Express Base Specification defines retimer behaviour and limits how many may sit on one link (two in recent revisions; confirm for your revision). For Gen5 with this loss I would choose the retimer, because it turns one marginal channel into two healthy ones.

    Costs: added latency (small, implementation-dependent, on the order of tens of nanoseconds), power, board area, another firmware image, and a new failure mode during equalization. For debugging, a retimer means an analyzer sees only one segment; errors in the other segment show up as retraining rather than visible bad symbols on your probe. Lane margining measures the receiver at each segment end separately, so plan where to margin.

    A strong answer includes: the loss-budget reason, the "two segments" consequence for analyzers and margining, the retimer's role in equalization, latency and power as real but secondary costs, and a qualification plan (margin both segments across temperature).

    A common weak answer: "Retimers are better, always use them," with no mention of what changes for debugging or that a redriver may be fine on a short channel.

    Learn it in: Chapter 5, Chapter 54, Module 07.

  2. [Trace] A fleet dashboard alarms on Link Training and Status State Machine (LTSSM) Recovery entries. One healthy-looking Lab SSD shows the following (synthetic) root port log while almost idle. Is anything wrong?

    #TimeFromToContext
    100:00:00.000L0L1No traffic, Active State Power Management (ASPM) L1 entry
    200:00:01.912L1RecoveryDoorbell write pending at root port
    300:00:01.912RecoveryL0Speed Gen4, width x4 unchanged
    400:00:01.950L0L1Idle again
    Pattern repeats about 40 times per minute. Correctable error counters are zero; link speed and width never change.
    Model answer

    Nothing here is abnormal. Exit from L1 goes through Recovery by design: the link must re-establish bit and symbol lock before returning to L0. So with ASPM L1 enabled and bursty traffic, every wake from L1 produces a Recovery entry. The evidence that this is benign is in the log itself: each Recovery is immediately preceded by L1, returns to L0 at the same speed and width, and the correctable error counters (Receiver Error, Bad TLP, Bad Data Link Layer Packet (DLLP), Replay Timer Timeout, REPLAY_NUM Rollover) stay at zero.

    The real problem is the dashboard. A Recovery counter alone mixes power-management exits with error-driven retraining, so it will page people for healthy drives and hide unhealthy ones in the noise. I would change the signal to classify Recovery entries by predecessor state (from L1 versus from L0) and to alarm on error-driven Recovery, changes in negotiated speed or width, and correctable error rates. Exit latency from L1 still matters for tail latency, but that is a power-policy question, not a link-health one.

    A strong answer includes: L1 exit via Recovery as normal behaviour, the zero error counters as the discriminating evidence, unchanged speed and width, and a concrete fix to the alarm logic.

    A common weak answer: "Recovery means the link is unstable; replace the riser."

    Learn it in: Chapter 8, Chapter 50, Module 08.

  3. [Architecture] You must qualify a new riser card for 20,000 servers. Design a qualification method based on Lane Margining at the Receiver (lane margining). What can it tell you, and what can it not?

    Model answer

    Lane margining, introduced for 16 GT/s and above, lets software ask a receiver to move its sampling point in time and (optionally) voltage on a live link and report errors, giving a per-lane estimate of eye margin without external equipment. A qualification method: margin every lane, both directions where the far-end receiver supports it, on a statistically meaningful sample of servers across riser lots, slot positions, cold and hot chassis temperature and loaded versus idle power rails. Record margin per lane, compare the distribution with a golden baseline (a known-good riser), and set acceptance on the low tail (for example the minimum lane per system), not the mean. Repeat after thermal cycling and after field return.

    Limits: margining measures the receiver's view at one moment; it is not a compliance eye measurement, step sizes and ranges are reported by each implementation, and results from different vendors' receivers are not directly comparable. It disturbs the receiver, so it is a caution-level operation on production traffic and can itself cause correctable errors. It does not see transmitter-side defects that only appear under specific data patterns. It cannot replace a lab with a scope for root cause; it is a screening and trending tool.

    A strong answer includes: per-lane, tail-focused acceptance against a golden baseline, temperature and lot coverage, the "not comparable across receivers" caveat and the production-safety caveat.

    A common weak answer: "Run margining once and pass if no errors," treating a single measurement as an eye diagram.

    Learn it in: Chapter 54, Chapter 9, Validation Engineering.

  4. [Performance] A storage sled has a fixed budget of 32 lanes from the CPU. One proposal is eight Gen5 x4 SSDs; another is four Gen4 x8 SSDs of the same capacity class. Compare them.

    Model answer

    Per-device raw bandwidth is the same: Gen5 x4 is 32 GT/s × 4 lanes and Gen4 x8 is 16 GT/s × 8 lanes, both 128 Gb/s per direction, about 15.75 GB/s after 128b/130b encoding and before TLP and DLLP overhead. So the comparison is about everything else. Eight x4 devices give twice the device count, twice the failure-domain granularity (losing one drive loses 1/8 of capacity, not 1/4), more independent controllers and NAND back ends, and they match the dominant x4 form factors. Four x8 devices need fewer retimers per lane only if the channel is shorter at Gen4; Gen4 is more forgiving electrically, uses less physical-layer power per bit at the receiver in many designs and works in older hosts at full speed.

    Total sled bandwidth is equal (32 lanes either way). The decisive questions are: can each SSD's back end actually sustain 15 GB/s (often the NAND and controller, not the link, are the limit, which favours more devices); what is the channel length (Gen5 may need retimers); does the platform need per-drive hot-plug and isolation (favours more devices); and will these drives run in Gen4-only hosts later (a Gen5 x4 drive drops to half bandwidth there, an x8 drive in an x4 slot also halves). I would pick eight Gen5 x4 for a scale-out storage service and verify channel margin.

    A strong answer includes: the equal-bandwidth arithmetic, back-end limits, failure-domain size, channel and retimer cost, and backward compatibility.

    A common weak answer: "Gen5 is newer so it is faster," ignoring that the raw bandwidth is identical.

    Learn it in: Chapter 6, Performance Engineering, Mathematics for Storage and Links.

  5. [Architecture] Your SSD controller team is starting a PCIe 6.x design. What does FLIT mode change inside the controller compared with a Gen5 design, and what stays the same for NVMe?

    Model answer

    At 64 GT/s the physical layer uses PAM4 signalling, which has a much higher raw bit error rate than the two-level Non-Return-to-Zero (NRZ) signalling of earlier generations. To cope, PCIe 6.x introduces FLIT mode: traffic is carried in fixed 256-byte flits that hold TLP bytes, Data Link Layer bytes, a cyclic redundancy check (CRC) and lightweight forward error correction (FEC). Consequences inside the controller: the Data Link Layer acknowledges and replays flits rather than individual TLPs, so the replay buffer and ACK logic are redesigned; FLIT mode uses a different TLP header format, so the transaction layer's header encode and decode logic changes and must also support the older format when the link trains in non-FLIT mode at lower speeds; per-TLP framing and LCRC disappear in FLIT mode; and the L0p state lets the link reduce active width while staying up, which the power manager can use.

    What stays the same: NVMe sits above PCIe and does not change. Queues, doorbells, SQEs, Completion Queue Entries (CQEs), Physical Region Page (PRP) and Scatter Gather List (SGL) processing, MSI-X, ordering semantics at the transaction level, and the NVMe over PCIe Transport binding are unaffected. The controller's DMA engine still issues memory reads and writes; it simply needs more outstanding requests and buffering to fill a faster link. Field-level details must be read in the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x.

    A strong answer includes: PAM4 → FEC → fixed flits as a causal chain, flit-level replay, the new header format plus backward compatibility, L0p, and an explicit "NVMe does not change."

    A common weak answer: "It is just Gen5 at double speed," or claiming NVMe needs a new command format for Gen6.

    Learn it in: Chapter 6, Chapter 7, Module 07.

  6. [Architecture] Product management asks whether the SSD controller taping out in two years should target PCIe 6.x or wait for 7.0. How do you frame the decision?

    Model answer

    Frame it around where the bottleneck will be, not the headline rate. PCIe 7.0 doubles the per-lane rate again to 128 GT/s, keeping PAM4 and FLIT mode; an x4 link at 7.0 carries roughly what an x16 link carried at Gen5. Questions to answer with data: (1) Host availability: will the platforms that buy this drive ship root ports at that rate within the drive's sales window? A drive faster than every host runs at a lower speed and the extra PHY area and power are wasted. (2) Back-end balance: can the NAND channel count, controller cores, DRAM and firmware actually sustain the link rate for realistic workloads, or will the drive be media-bound well below it? (3) Power and thermal envelope: higher-rate physical layers cost power, and SSD form factors have hard thermal limits. (4) Signal integrity: higher rates shorten channels and require more retimers in servers. (5) Risk: a new PHY generation brings interoperability bugs; the first silicon of a new rate needs more validation time.

    A common outcome is to target the earlier rate with a design that can scale, and to compete on efficiency and quality of service at that rate. I would present the choice as a table of host-availability dates, sustainable back-end bandwidth and power, and name the evidence (host roadmaps from platform vendors, NAND interface rates) that would flip it.

    A strong answer includes: host availability, back-end balance, power, signal integrity and validation risk; a clear recommendation with a reversal condition.

    A common weak answer: "Always target the newest generation for marketing."

    Learn it in: Chapter 6, Module 59, Senior Engineer Thinking.

  7. [Troubleshoot] Across one server model, about 2% of SSDs log a steady trickle of correctable Receiver Errors. Per-lane data shows almost all of them on lane 2 of the x4 link. What does that pattern suggest, and how do you prove it?

    Model answer

    A lane-specific pattern that repeats across many drives in one server model points away from the SSDs (different units, possibly different lots) and towards something the drives share in that position: a trace, via, connector pin, cable conductor, retimer lane or equalization preset on that platform. A random silicon defect would not repeat on the same lane number across 2% of a fleet.

    Evidence plan: confirm the per-lane attribution source. The Secondary PCI Express Extended Capability includes a Lane Error Status register, and lane margining gives per-lane margin; both are read at the receiver, so check which end logged the errors (upstream receiver at the root port, or the SSD's receiver). Then do the cheap discriminating experiments: swap the SSD to another slot (does the error follow the slot or the drive?), swap a known-good SSD into the affected slot, check whether lane reversal is in use (logical lane 2 may be a different physical lane), and margin lane 2 versus the other lanes on affected and unaffected servers. Cohort by board revision, cable lot and slot. If confirmed, the fix is a platform change (preset tuning, board rework or cable), and the operational decision is whether the error rate threatens link stability (watch for Recovery or replay rollover) or is merely noise.

    A strong answer includes: the "shared position" inference, which receiver logged the errors, swap tests, lane reversal, margining comparison and cohort analysis.

    A common weak answer: RMA the affected SSDs.

    Learn it in: Chapter 45, Chapter 9, Evidence-Based Debugging.

  8. [Architecture] A just-a-bunch-of-flash (JBOF) enclosure connects to hosts by PCIe cables, and the enclosure has its own reference clock. Which clocking architectures are available, and what does each require from the SSDs and switches?

    Model answer

    Three options. Common reference clock: both ends use the same reference clock (distributed over the cable or regenerated). It is the simplest for receivers and allows spread-spectrum clocking (SSC) on both ends because they spread together, but distributing a clock across a cable is hard and is a shared failure point. Separate Reference clocks with No SSC (SRNS): each end has its own clock, spread spectrum disabled; receivers handle only a small frequency offset through SKP ordered set insertion and removal in the elastic buffer. Separate Reference clocks with Independent SSC (SRIS): each end has its own clock and its own spread spectrum, so the instantaneous frequency difference is much larger; transmitters must send SKP ordered sets more frequently and receivers need deeper elastic buffers.

    Design consequences: SRIS must be supported and configured at both ends of each link (the SSD and the switch downstream port), and not every device supports it, so it becomes a vendor qualification item. Mismatched configuration typically shows up as receiver errors or elastic-buffer problems rather than a clean failure. Electromagnetic compliance often wants SSC enabled, which pushes cabled designs towards SRIS. I would choose SRIS for a cabled JBOF if every component supports it, otherwise SRNS with a check on emissions, and keep the requirement in the vendor checklist.

    A strong answer includes: all three architectures, SKP frequency and elastic buffer as the mechanism, both-ends configuration, and vendor support as a qualification gate.

    A common weak answer: "PCIe recovers the clock from the data so the reference clock does not matter."

    Learn it in: Chapter 5, Module 07, Module 03.

  9. [Coding] Write a small function a capacity-planning tool can use to estimate usable one-direction data throughput of a PCIe link for large memory writes, given the generation, width and Max Payload Size (MPS). State your assumptions.

    Model answer

    The estimate multiplies the line rate by encoding efficiency and by the fraction of each TLP that is payload, then subtracts a small allowance for DLLPs (acknowledgements and flow-control updates). For 128b/130b generations (Gen3–Gen5) a 4-doubleword (DW) memory write TLP costs about 4 bytes of framing and sequence number, 16 bytes of header and 4 bytes of Link CRC (LCRC), so about 24 bytes per TLP. These are approximations: FLIT mode (Gen6 and later) has a different overhead model and is excluded.

    RATES_GT = {3: 8.0, 4: 16.0, 5: 32.0}   # GT/s per lane, 128b/130b generations only

def usable_gbytes_per_s(gen, width, mps, tlp_overhead=24, dllp_fraction=0.03):
    """Rough usable payload bandwidth, GB/s, one direction, large writes.
    tlp_overhead: framing + sequence + 4DW header + LCRC, in bytes (approximate).
    dllp_fraction: share of link time spent on ACK and UpdateFC DLLPs (assumed)."""
    if gen not in RATES_GT:
        raise ValueError("only 128b/130b generations modelled")
    raw = RATES_GT[gen] * 1e9 * width * 128 / 130 / 8      # bytes per second
    payload_eff = mps / (mps + tlp_overhead)
    return raw * payload_eff * (1 - dllp_fraction) / 1e9

for gen, mps in [(4, 256), (5, 256), (5, 512)]:
    print(f"Gen{gen} x4 MPS {mps}: {usable_gbytes_per_s(gen, 4, mps):.2f} GB/s")

    For the Lab SSD (Gen4 x4, MPS 256) this prints about 6.99 GB/s against a raw 7.88 GB/s. A principal reviewer would add: reads cost extra request TLPs in the opposite direction; small transfers are dominated by per-command overhead (doorbells, SQE fetch, CQE, MSI-X), not the payload formula; and real platforms may lose more to root-complex limits than to framing.

    A strong answer includes: explicit assumptions, the encoding and per-TLP terms, a DLLP allowance, and a statement of where the model is wrong.

    A common weak answer: GT/s × lanes ÷ 8 with no overhead, or a confident number with no assumptions.

    Learn it in: Mathematics for Storage and Links, Bandwidth, Latency and Little's Law Calculators, Chapter 10.

  10. [Architecture] A 24-drive storage server can attach its SSDs either by bifurcating CPU root ports into x4 links or through two PCIe switches. Compare the two for performance, error containment and operations.

    Model answer

    Direct attach with bifurcation (an x16 root port split into four x4 links): every drive has its own root port, the lowest latency, no oversubscription, and each link's errors, Downstream Port Containment (DPC) and hot-plug events are handled at a root port, which the operating system and firmware handle most uniformly. It needs 96 CPU lanes for 24 drives and depends on the platform's bifurcation support and on root-port hot-plug features.

    Switch attach: fewer CPU lanes (for example two x16 uplinks), so bandwidth is oversubscribed if all drives stream at once; added latency per switch hop (implementation-dependent); peer-to-peer traffic between drives or to a network card can stay below the root complex if Access Control Services (ACS) allow; and the switch becomes a shared failure domain, with its own firmware, its own error behaviour and its own containment configuration on each downstream port. A switch reset or firmware bug can take out twelve drives at once, and errors can be reported by the switch rather than the drive, which complicates attribution.

    I would pick direct attach where lanes allow it, for blast radius and simplicity; pick switches where lane count or peer-to-peer needs dominate, and then require DPC on every downstream port, switch firmware under change control, and switch telemetry in the fleet pipeline.

    A strong answer includes: oversubscription arithmetic, blast radius, where DPC and hot-plug live, attribution complexity, and peer-to-peer as the main reason to accept a switch.

    A common weak answer: "Switches add latency, so never use them," with no mention of lanes, failure domains or peer-to-peer.

    Learn it in: Module 03, Chapter 5, Chapter 55.

  11. [Troubleshoot] After a chassis redesign, 0.3% of SSDs in the fleet are running at x2 instead of x4, and nobody noticed for three months because performance dashboards averaged across drives. What do you do about the drives and about the monitoring?

    Model answer

    A link that trains with fewer lanes is legal and silent: the LTSSM negotiates the widest width all lanes can support, and NVMe works normally at half the bandwidth. The evidence is in the Link Status register (negotiated width) compared with the Link Capabilities register (maximum width) on both ends; Linux exposes both through lspci -vvv and sysfs attributes such as current_link_width and max_link_width.

    For the drives: cohort by chassis revision, slot position and cable or connector lot; check whether the same slots fail repeatedly; swap tests to see if width follows the slot or the drive; inspect for lanes that fail receiver detection or polling (a bent pin, a missing cable conductor, a lane-reversal mismatch). Fix the platform cause, and decide per drive whether to retrain or service it.

    For the monitoring, the real defect: averages hide per-device degradation. Add an explicit invariant per drive: negotiated speed and width must equal the expected values for that platform and slot, checked at boot and periodically, alarmed individually. Track it per chassis revision so a new design is caught in its first week. Add per-device throughput percentiles rather than fleet averages. Then ask what else is silently "legal but degraded" (speed downgrades, disabled features such as 10-bit tags or ASPM settings, MPS mismatches) and cover those with the same mechanism.

    A strong answer includes: link width as legal and silent, the LnkSta/LnkCap comparison, slot-versus-drive swap test, and an invariant-based monitoring fix generalised to other silent degradations.

    A common weak answer: "Reseat the drives" with no change to monitoring.

    Learn it in: Chapter 9, Chapter 69, Chapter 57.

  12. [Spec] A vendor says their Gen5 SSD "passed PCI-SIG compliance." What does that tell you, and what does it not tell you, about deploying it in your platform?

    Model answer

    Compliance testing at a PCI Special Interest Group (PCI-SIG) compliance workshop checks that a product meets selected requirements of the specifications in a standardised test setup: electrical tests of transmitter and receiver against reference channels, link and transaction protocol tests, configuration space checks, and interoperability sessions with other attendees' products. Passing typically earns a place on the Integrators List. That is valuable evidence that the device's basics are sound and that it interoperated with some peers.

    It does not tell you: that the device works with your root complex, retimers, switch and channel at your temperatures; anything about NVMe behaviour (that is a separate compliance programme, and even then optional features vary); error-handling behaviour under real faults (surprise down, Completion Timeout recovery, Function Level Reset (FLR) under load); performance or quality of service; firmware maturity across updates; or behaviour of optional features you rely on (10-bit tags, ASPM substates, ACS, lane margining support). Compliance is a necessary floor, not a qualification.

    So I would accept it as entry criteria and then run our own platform qualification: link training loops across resets and temperature, margining, error injection, reset and power sequences, and NVMe functional and performance suites. I would also ask which test revision and which specification revision the device was tested against, since requirements evolve.

    A strong answer includes: what compliance covers, what it cannot cover, PCIe versus NVMe compliance as separate, and compliance as entry criteria for in-house qualification.

    A common weak answer: "It passed compliance, so it is interoperable."

    Learn it in: Validation Engineering, Module 54, Specification Reading School.

  13. [Architecture] Your lab must capture Gen5 x4 traffic between a host and an SSD on a backplane that includes a retimer. Where do you place the protocol analyzer interposer, and what will you be unable to see?

    Model answer

    An interposer is a new piece of channel: it adds loss and reflections, and at Gen5 it can push a marginal channel over the edge, so first check that the link still trains at full speed and width with the interposer present, and that error counters do not change. That is the probe-effect check.

    With a retimer, the link is two electrical segments that share one logical link. Placing the interposer between the retimer and the SSD (closest to the device) usually gives the cleanest view of what the device sends and receives, and it is the right place when the device is the suspect. Placing it on the host side of the retimer shows what the root port sends and receives. In either place you see all TLPs and DLLPs, because the retimer forwards them, but you only see the physical-layer behaviour of your segment: symbol errors, equalization and ordered sets on the other segment are invisible, and an error there appears to you only as its consequence (a NAK, a replay or a Recovery). Equalization phases can also differ per segment.

    So the capture plan should name which segment is observed, collect lane margining and error counters from both ends to cover the unobserved segment, and, for physical-layer questions, consider moving the interposer or using a second capture point.

    A strong answer includes: probe effect, segment-limited physical visibility, protocol traffic still visible, and complementary in-band evidence for the hidden segment.

    A common weak answer: "Put the analyzer anywhere; it sees the whole link."

    Learn it in: Teledyne LeCroy Analyzer Training, Module 55, Trace Analysis Laboratory.

  14. [Performance] To save power, a team proposes lowering SSD link speed from Gen5 to Gen3 at night by writing the Target Link Speed field and retraining the link. Review the idea.

    Model answer

    The mechanism exists: software can set Target Link Speed in Link Control 2 at the downstream port and request a retrain, and the LTSSM changes speed through Recovery. But the review needs four questions answered. (1) How much power does it save? The physical layer is only part of an SSD's power; the controller, DRAM and NAND usually dominate. Measure at the wall, per drive, rather than assuming. (2) What does the transition cost? Each speed change stalls traffic for the retrain (implementation-dependent duration), may fail and fall back, and exercises equalization paths that are less tested than the boot path, which is a reliability risk at fleet scale. (3) What is the latency cost if load arrives unexpectedly? Gen3 x4 is about a quarter of Gen5 x4 bandwidth; a nightly batch job could miss its window. (4) Are there better mechanisms? ASPM L1 and L1 substates save power when idle without changing speed; NVMe power states and Autonomous Power State Transitions (APST) target the device; PCIe 6.x L0p reduces width without leaving L0.

    I would ask for a measured power saving and a reliability test of thousands of speed changes on every qualified platform before approving. Without them, I would prefer idle-state power management, which is designed for this and does not change bandwidth.

    A strong answer includes: measure before deciding, transition stall and failure risk, bandwidth headroom, and the existing idle-power mechanisms as alternatives.

    A common weak answer: approving because "lower speed must be lower power."

    Learn it in: Chapter 50, Module 42, Chapter 9.

  15. [Architecture] You are writing the PCIe section of a qualification plan for three candidate SSD vendors. List the tests you would require and explain how you would weight the results.

    Model answer

    Group tests by what they protect. Link bring-up: thousands of cold boots, warm reboots, hot resets and link disable/enable cycles per platform, checking speed, width and time to link up; temperature corners; lane margining distributions against a baseline. Reset behaviour: FLR under I/O load, secondary bus reset, surprise removal and re-insertion where the platform supports hot-plug, and DPC-triggered recovery. Error handling: injected errors (with an exerciser or jammer) covering bad TLPs and DLLPs, poisoned completions, Completion Timeouts and Unsupported Requests, verifying both AER logging and that the device recovers. Configuration: MPS 128/256/512, Max Read Request Size (MRRS) settings, extended and 10-bit tags, Relaxed Ordering, No Snoop and ACS combinations the platform uses. Power: ASPM L1 and substates entry and exit, Latency Tolerance Reporting (LTR) values, D3hot and D3cold cycles. Security and manageability if required: Data Object Exchange (DOE) and Security Protocol and Data Model (SPDM) support.

  1. [Performance] You are sizing the DMA engine of a Gen5 x4 SSD controller. Host-memory read round-trip time (request out to last completion in) is about 1.5 µs on a direct-attached root port but up to 4 µs behind a switch with an Input-Output Memory Management Unit (IOMMU) translation miss. MRRS is 512 bytes. How many outstanding reads and how much completion buffering do you need to keep the link full for writes (the SSD pulling data from host memory)?

    Model answer

    Use the bandwidth-delay product. Gen5 x4 is about 15.75 GB/s raw per direction; call it about 14 GB/s of usable completion payload. In-flight bytes needed = bandwidth × round-trip time: 14 GB/s × 1.5 µs ≈ 21 KB; 14 GB/s × 4 µs ≈ 56 KB. Divided by 512-byte requests that is about 41 and about 110 outstanding read requests. Eight-bit tags give 256 identifiers, so tags are not the limit here; the limits are the completion buffer and the internal request tracking.

    Completion buffering matters because an Endpoint advertises infinite completion credits: it promises to accept every completion it asked for without flow-control back-pressure, so it must reserve buffer space before issuing each read. To sustain the worst case, the controller needs at least about 56 KB of completion buffer plus margin for jitter, or it must throttle its own reads and accept lower throughput in the high-latency topology. I would size for the realistic worst topology the product supports (behind a switch with IOMMU misses), add margin of perhaps 1.5×, and expose internal counters (outstanding reads, buffer occupancy) for debugging. If MRRS were raised, fewer, larger requests would be needed, but the buffer requirement in bytes would not change.

    A strong answer includes: the bandwidth-delay arithmetic for both latencies, tags versus buffer as separate limits, infinite completion credits as the reason buffering must be pre-reserved, and design margin for the worst supported topology.

  3. [Spec] A multi-function SSD exposes many SR-IOV Virtual Functions behind a PCIe switch. What does ID-Based Ordering (IDO) allow, and why might you want it enabled for this device? Does it endanger NVMe's "data before CQE" guarantee?

    Model answer

    The default PCIe ordering rules make a posted write wait behind earlier posted writes in the same traffic class, regardless of which function sent them. IDO, when enabled at the requester and supported along the path, allows TLPs from different Requester IDs to pass each other, because ordering between unrelated requesters is not needed for correctness. In a Single Root I/O Virtualization (SR-IOV) device behind a switch, that means a stalled stream from one Virtual Function (VF) (for example, its target memory controller is congested) does not hold up writes from another VF in shared buffers. It is a head-of-line blocking and isolation improvement for multi-tenant devices.

    It does not endanger NVMe's guarantee inside one function. Each VF's data writes, CQE writes and MSI-X writes carry the same Requester ID, so IDO does not let them pass each other; only cross-requester reordering is permitted. The risk to check is different: whether any component (an older switch or root complex) mishandles IDO, and whether software has assumptions about ordering between functions (rare in NVMe). As with Relaxed Ordering, enablement is a host decision through the Device Control 2 register, and the path must support it.

    A strong answer includes: the cross-requester scope, the multi-tenant head-of-line benefit, and the reasoning that within-function ordering is untouched.

    A common weak answer: "IDO is like Relaxed Ordering, so it could let the CQE pass the data."

    def check_completions(addr, length, cpls, rcb=64):
    """addr, length: the request in bytes. cpls: list of (byte_count, lower_addr, payload_len)."""
    problems, cur, remaining = [], addr, length
    for i, (bc, la, plen) in enumerate(cpls):
        if bc != remaining:
            problems.append(f"cpl {i}: Byte Count {bc}, expected {remaining}")

  5. [Architecture] A controller design review shows that the SSD's ingress logic stops accepting posted writes (including doorbell writes) whenever its internal read-completion buffer is full, waiting for its own outstanding reads to complete. Why is this dangerous?

    Model answer

    It creates a dependency that the PCIe ordering and flow-control rules are designed to forbid, and it can deadlock. PCIe requires that posted requests be able to make progress past non-posted requests and completions in the cases the ordering table defines; the reason is deadlock avoidance in a system where every component has finite buffers. Here the SSD makes acceptance of posted traffic (by withholding posted credits) depend on completions arriving for its reads. Those completions come from the root complex, whose egress path towards the SSD is now blocked by posted writes that cannot drain because the SSD returns no posted credits. If the completions are queued behind those posted writes in any shared buffer (for example in a switch, or in the root complex's own queue towards this port), nothing moves: a deadlock, which in the field looks like a Completion Timeout on one side and a hung link on the other.

    Even if a particular platform happens not to deadlock, the design couples host-to-device latency (doorbells) to device-to-host read latency, adding jitter. The fix is to keep posted ingress independent: reserve buffer for posted requests, process doorbells in a path that never waits for DMA completions, and rely on the infinite-completion-credit rule the device already promised. The review should also ask for a stress test with a deliberately slow root complex.

    A strong answer includes: the deadlock cycle described concretely, ordering rules as deadlock avoidance, symptoms in the field and a design fix.

    A common weak answer: "It is just back-pressure, which flow control allows."

  7. [Troubleshoot] Fleet telemetry shows bursts of correctable "Replay Timer Timeout" errors on one platform, but almost no "Bad TLP" or "Bad DLLP" errors at either end. What does that combination suggest?

    Model answer

    Replay Timer Timeout is logged by the transmitter when it has not received an acknowledgement for a transmitted TLP within the replay timer; it then replays. Bad TLP and Bad DLLP are logged by a receiver that sees a corrupted packet (for example an LCRC failure). If TLPs were being corrupted, the receiving end would log Bad TLP and send NAKs, and the transmitter would replay promptly on the NAK rather than waiting for a timeout. Timeouts without receive-side errors suggest that acknowledgements are late or lost rather than TLPs being damaged: the far end may be slow to send ACKs (busy receive pipeline, power-management exit, an implementation that batches ACKs too aggressively), ACK DLLPs may be lost in a way the counters do not attribute, or the replay timer may be set too short for the actual round trip (for example with a retimer or long channel adding latency, or a timer value not adjusted for the link width and payload size).

    Evidence: correlate timeouts with link state transitions (L1 exits, speed changes); check whether REPLAY_NUM Rollover also appears (four replays without progress trigger link retraining, which is when it becomes service-affecting); capture with an analyzer to measure actual ACK latency against the timer; compare with the same SSD on another platform. If timeouts cluster at L1 exit, the issue is likely power-management interplay rather than signal integrity.

  8. [Trace] The root port sends TLPs with sequence numbers 100–105 to the SSD. The SSD returns one DLLP: NAK with AckNak_Seq_Num = 102. Which TLPs are acknowledged, which are replayed, and what happens to the replay buffer? What if a second NAK for 102 arrives after the replay?

    Model answer

    A NAK carries the sequence number of the last TLP the receiver accepted correctly. So NAK 102 acknowledges 100, 101 and 102: the transmitter purges them from its replay buffer. It then replays every TLP after 102 that is still in the buffer, in order: 103, 104 and 105. The receiver discards anything it receives out of sequence until 103 arrives correctly; that is why a single corrupted TLP causes everything after it to be resent.

    If a second NAK 102 arrives after the replay, the receiver is telling the transmitter that 103 was corrupted again. The transmitter replays 103–105 again and its REPLAY_NUM counter increments each time a replay is triggered without forward progress. If REPLAY_NUM rolls over (after four replays), the transmitter's Data Link Layer directs the Physical Layer to retrain the link through Recovery and logs a correctable REPLAY_NUM Rollover error. If replays continue to fail after retraining, the link may eventually go down, which escalates to a Surprise Down or DPC event depending on configuration.

    For a principal, the interesting part is the operational reading: one NAK is routine; repeated NAKs for the same sequence number point to a persistent, pattern-dependent corruption (a specific TLP's bit pattern hitting a marginal lane), which is worth capturing and correlating with lane margin.

    A strong answer includes: the "last good" meaning of the NAK sequence number, purge and replay sets, REPLAY_NUM and rollover to Recovery, and the operational meaning of repeated NAKs.

  9. [Architecture] Should you enable End-to-end CRC (ECRC) across your fleet of NVMe servers? Argue both sides and decide.

    Model answer

    The case for: LCRC protects each link hop, but each switch and the root complex check and then regenerate LCRC, so corruption that happens inside a component (a buffer bit flip, a firmware or logic bug in a switch) is not detected by LCRC. ECRC is computed once by the originator and checked by the final receiver, covering those internal paths. For a storage fleet where silent data corruption is the worst outcome, closing that gap has real value.

    The case against or for caution: ECRC generation and checking are optional capabilities, controlled through the AER capability, and support is uneven; some root complexes do not check ECRC on traffic they receive, which removes most of the benefit for device-to-host data. Switches must forward the TLP Digest correctly. Enabling a rarely exercised feature across a fleet risks interoperability bugs and noisy errors (ECRC Check errors are uncorrectable non-fatal by default), and the operational response to an ECRC error must be defined. Data integrity at the storage level is also covered by other layers: NVMe Protection Information (PI), application checksums and file-system checksums.

    Decision: enable it on platforms where every component in the path has been qualified for ECRC generation and checking, starting with a canary population and with a defined response (log, fail the I/O path, investigate the component). Where any component lacks it, rely on end-to-end NVMe PI and application checksums instead. Linux exposes an ECRC policy when built with support; the setting must match platform firmware ownership of AER.

    A strong answer includes: the LCRC regeneration gap, optional support and root-complex checking as the main practical limit, canary rollout and defined response, and alternatives at higher layers.

    A common weak answer: "LCRC already protects everything" or "turn it on everywhere."

  10. [Troubleshoot] During an NVMe Write, the SSD receives a CplD for its data read with the Poisoned (EP) bit set. What should each side do, and where is the root cause most likely?

    Model answer

    A poisoned completion means the completer (here the root complex) knew the data was bad when it sent it: most often an uncorrectable error in host DRAM, or poisoned data propagated from elsewhere in the memory system. The SSD, as the receiver, logs Poisoned TLP Received (uncorrectable, non-fatal by default; it may be handled as an Advisory Non-Fatal error depending on the role and configuration) and must not commit the poisoned data as if it were good.   11. [Architecture] Set a fleet policy for the PCIe Completion Timeout value on root ports and NVMe SSDs, relative to the NVMe driver's I/O timeout. What constraints drive the choice?

    Model answer

    The Completion Timeout is configured per function in Device Control 2 from the ranges that function advertises in Device Capabilities 2. The default range is 50 µs to 50 ms; optional ranges extend from tens of microseconds up to many seconds (Range D reaches 64 s). It can also be disabled where supported. It applies to the requester: a root port timing out reads it sent to the SSD (configuration and MMIO reads), and the SSD timing out its DMA reads to host memory.

    Constraints: (1) It must comfortably exceed the worst legitimate completion latency, including switch hops, IOMMU misses, memory contention and, for the SSD's reads, root-complex behaviour under heavy load; too short produces false timeouts and Unexpected Completions later. (3) An MMIO read from the CPU that times out may stall a CPU core until the timeout fires, so very long root-port values can make a dead device hang a core for seconds.

    Policy: keep the default range unless measurement shows legitimate latencies near it; never disable it on production SSDs; make sure the platform reports Completion Timeout through AER or firmware-first records into the fleet pipeline; and test the value with a stress topology. Document the rationale so later tuning does not silently break the "PCIe fails first" property.

  13. [Performance] A platform team proposes raising MRRS on all SSDs from 512 bytes to 4096 bytes "for performance." What changes, what does not, and what is the risk?

    Model answer

    What changes: the SSD can request up to 4 KiB per memory read, so it sends one eighth as many read request TLPs for large transfers, uses fewer tags and less request-tracking state, and the upstream direction carries less request overhead. On platforms where the device was tag- or request-limited, Write throughput (the SSD reading host memory) may improve.

    What does not change: completions are still limited by MPS (256 bytes on the Lab SSD) and may be split at the RCB, so the downstream completion stream carries the same number of data TLPs and the same overhead. The completion buffer the device needs, in bytes, is the same for the same in-flight data.

With large read requests, one device can occupy a shared completer (root port or switch upstream link) with a large completion burst, so other devices sharing that path see longer latency spikes. That is why some platforms and operating systems cap MRRS; for example Linux's pcie_bus_perf setting limits MRRS to no more than MPS. A large MRRS can also expose root complexes that handle large reads less efficiently. The right answer is to measure: run the mixed-tenant latency tests, not just one device's bandwidth, with each setting, and pick the value that meets both the throughput target and the latency target.

    The write starts at 0x2_0000_0F80 and carries 256 bytes, so it ends at 0x2_0000_107F: it crosses the 4 KiB boundary at 0x2_0000_1000. A memory request must not cross a 4 KiB address boundary; a receiver that detects one treats it as a Malformed TLP, which is fatal by default. The Requester ID 0x0100 is the Lab SSD, and nothing on the path modifies addresses and lengths, so the SSD's DMA engine broke the rule. It should have split the transfer into a 128-byte write ending at 0x2_0000_0FFF and a write starting at 0x2_0000_1000.

Most tests use page-aligned buffers, so the path is rarely exercised; the default Linux block path also tends to use aligned buffers. That is how it escaped.

  16. [Coding] Write a function for your trace-analysis pipeline that finds leaked tags: non-posted requests from the device whose completions never fully arrive within a window, and tags reused while still outstanding.

            report.append((req["t"], f"no final completion, {req['owed']} bytes owed", key))
    return report

  17. [Troubleshoot] A hot-plugged SSD behind a switch works until heavy writes start, then the switch logs Malformed TLPs from the SSD. The SSD's Device Control shows MPS 512; the switch port above it is set to MPS 256. How did this happen, and what is the policy fix?

    Model answer

    MPS must be set consistently along a path: a transmitter must not send TLPs with payloads larger than the MPS programmed at the receiver, and a receiver treats an oversized payload as a Malformed TLP. Here the SSD was configured for 512 bytes, so its device-to-host data writes (which grow under heavy traffic towards the largest allowed size) exceeded what the switch port accepts. Small transfers worked because their TLPs were smaller than 256 bytes. "Works until heavy writes" here really means "works until large payload TLPs appear," which is typical of this misconfiguration.

    How it happens: MPS is chosen by system firmware at boot and by the operating system at enumeration; a hot-plugged device is configured after the hierarchy's values are already fixed. Linux has several policies for this (pcie_bus_safe, pcie_bus_perf, pcie_bus_peer2peer, pcie_bus_tune_off), and if the policy leaves the device at a value the hierarchy does not support, or firmware and the OS disagree, the mismatch appears. Peer-to-peer traffic adds a further constraint: every device on a peer path must agree.

    Fix: choose one owner and policy for MPS across the fleet (usually the OS, with a documented setting per platform), verify after every hot-add that each device's MPS is no greater than every port on its path, and add that invariant to fleet monitoring. For platforms with peer-to-peer use, the conservative common value is the price of safety.

    A strong answer includes: receiver-side MPS rule, why only large writes fail, hot-plug timing, Linux MPS policies, and a fleet invariant check.

    A common weak answer: "Set everything to 512 for performance."

  1. [Architecture] A vendor offers a variant of its SSD with a large Controller Memory Buffer (CMB) exposed through a 64-bit prefetchable BAR of several gigabytes. What could you use it for, and what platform and operational risks does it bring?

    Model answer

    Uses: placing Submission Queues in the CMB, so the host writes SQEs directly into device memory over MMIO and the controller does not fetch them across the link (saving a round trip per command, useful for latency-sensitive paths); staging data for peer-to-peer DMA, for example a network card writing received data directly into the SSD's CMB in an NVMe over Fabrics target, avoiding host DRAM. Resizable BAR can help negotiate size. (2) Performance asymmetry: host CPU writes to the CMB are posted and can be fast with write-combining, but host reads from device memory are slow non-posted round trips, so any code path that reads the CMB is a trap. (3) Peer-to-peer depends on root-complex and switch support and ACS settings; it may silently route through the root complex or not work. (4) Security: CMB is device memory reachable by other devices if peer-to-peer is allowed; isolation must be designed. (5) Data in the CMB is not persistent (that is the Persistent Memory Region's role), so no one may treat it as storage.

    I would take it only for a defined use (peer-to-peer target offload or a latency tier), on platforms qualified for large BARs and peer-to-peer.

    A strong answer includes: concrete uses, BAR allocation and firmware constraints, MMIO read cost, peer-to-peer dependencies, isolation and non-persistence.

  2. [Troubleshoot] A technician hot-adds a U.2 SSD into an empty bay. The kernel log reports (paraphrased) that BAR 0 of 0000:05:00.0 could not be assigned because there is no space in the bridge window, and no NVMe device appears. What happened, and what is the platform-level fix?

    Model answer

    At boot, platform firmware and the operating system sized each bridge's memory window from the devices present. An empty hot-plug bay had little or no memory window reserved, so when a device appears later, its 16 KiB (or larger) BAR has nowhere to go inside the downstream port's window, and the parent windows cannot grow without moving resources of devices already in use. The device is detected (configuration space works) but cannot be enabled because its BAR is unassigned, so the NVMe driver never binds.

    Short-term options: Linux kernel parameters that reserve space for hot-plug bridges (pci=hpmemsize=, and on newer kernels separate hpmmiosize and hpmmioprefsize) or pci=realloc to let the kernel reassign windows at boot; rebooting with the drive present also works but defeats hot-plug. The platform-level fix is a firmware policy that reserves a window for every hot-plug-capable port (sized for the largest device the bay supports, including any large CMB or Persistent Memory Region (PMR) BARs) and a qualification test that hot-adds the largest supported device into every bay.

    The principal angle is that hot-plug is a contract among firmware, OS, and devices; the failure appears only with real hot-adds, so it must be in the qualification plan, and the reservation size must be part of the platform's hardware requirements when new device types are allowed.

    A strong answer includes: boot-time window sizing, why a hot-added BAR cannot fit, kernel and firmware options, and a qualification test with the largest device.

  4. [Architecture] Should your SSDs use Address Translation Services (ATS) so that they cache IOMMU translations and send already-translated requests? Discuss performance, security and failure modes.

    Model answer

    With ATS the device asks the IOMMU (the translation agent) for translations, caches them in its Address Translation Cache (ATC), and marks later requests as translated, so the root complex skips the lookup. Benefit: fewer IOTLB misses at the root complex for workloads with many scattered buffers, which can reduce latency and improve throughput when the IOMMU is in translated mode.

    Costs and risks: (1) Security: a translated request bypasses the IOMMU's check, so the host must trust that the device only uses translations it was given; a buggy or malicious device can reach any memory. Platforms often disable ATS for untrusted or externally attached devices for this reason. (2) Invalidation: when the host unmaps a buffer, it must send an invalidation to the device and wait for its completion before reusing the memory. In strict IOMMU mode, where each I/O's buffer is unmapped on completion, that becomes an invalidation per I/O and can erase the benefit. (3) Correctness: an ATC that returns stale translations after an invalidation is a silent memory-corruption bug; validation must test invalidation under load. (4) Complexity: Page Request Interface (PRI) for on-demand paging is not needed for NVMe, whose buffers are pinned, so ATS without PRI is the realistic configuration.

    Decision: enable ATS only where measurements show IOTLB misses limit performance, the device is trusted (attested, first-party), the IOMMU uses a mapping strategy that does not invalidate per I/O, and the device passed invalidation stress tests. Otherwise, IOMMU passthrough or large-page mappings get most of the benefit with less risk.

    A strong answer includes: ATC mechanism, trust model, invalidation cost under strict mode, stale-translation risk and the alternatives.

    A common weak answer: "ATS makes DMA faster, enable it."

  7. [Spec] A virtualization team cannot pass one SSD's Virtual Function to a VM because the IOMMU group also contains other SSDs behind the same switch. Explain the cause and the options.

    Model answer

    IOMMU groups are the smallest sets of devices the kernel can isolate from each other. Two devices end up in the same group when the platform cannot guarantee that one cannot reach the other without passing through the IOMMU, for example peer-to-peer traffic between two switch downstream ports that turns inside the switch. ACS is the PCIe mechanism that provides that guarantee: on switch downstream ports and root ports, ACS controls such as Source Validation, P2P Request Redirect, P2P Completion Redirect and Upstream Forwarding force peer traffic up to the root complex where the IOMMU sees it. If the switch's downstream ports lack ACS or it is disabled, the kernel must put everything below that switch into one group, and VFIO requires the whole group to be assigned together.

    Options: (1) Use switches whose downstream ports implement ACS and enable it (firmware or OS). This is the real fix and should be a procurement requirement for multi-tenant platforms. (2) Attach SSDs to root ports with ACS support. (3) Kernel overrides that pretend isolation exists exist in some downstream kernels; they defeat the security guarantee and are unacceptable for multi-tenant use. (4) For SR-IOV inside one SSD, the VFs are separated by the device's own design; the device must report this correctly (multi-function devices may need ACS capability or platform quirks to be split into separate groups).

    A strong answer includes: what a group means, how ACS redirect creates isolation, why VFIO needs whole groups, the procurement fix, and why override hacks are unsafe.

    A common weak answer: "Turn off the IOMMU" or "use the ACS override patch."

  11. [Architecture] Design the control-plane flow that provisions NVMe secondary controllers on an SR-IOV SSD for a cloud host, and say what must happen when provisioning fails halfway.

  12. [Spec] A hypervisor issues a Function Level Reset (FLR) to one VF of an SSD while that VF has writes in flight. What must the device guarantee, what happens to the data, and what does the hypervisor have to do?

    Model answer

    FLR resets only the target function: its configuration-visible state returns to defaults (except what the specification says is preserved), it must stop issuing requests, and it must complete the reset within 100 ms. Completions that arrive afterwards for requests the function issued before the reset are discarded. Other functions, including the physical function and other VFs, must not be disturbed: their links, queues and I/O continue. For NVMe, the VF's secondary controller is reset as part of the function reset, so its queues disappear and in-flight commands are not completed to the host.

    Data: namespace contents persist (FLR is not a format). But writes in flight at the moment of reset are indeterminate: each may have been fully written, partially written at block granularity (subject to the namespace's atomicity guarantees), or not at all. The guest never received completions, so it must treat them as failed and retry or recover according to its own consistency rules.

    Hypervisor duties: quiesce or account for the VM's I/O first if possible; wait the required time before accessing the VF's configuration space (and handle not-ready responses); restore configuration the guest expects (BARs, MSI-X) or let the guest re-enumerate; and make sure no DMA mapping for the VF's guest memory was torn down while the VF could still write (the FLR stops new requests, but mappings should be removed only after it completes). Validation should prove the other functions are unaffected under load.

    A strong answer includes: function scope, 100 ms completion, discarding stale completions, NVMe controller reset as a consequence, indeterminate in-flight writes and atomicity, and hypervisor sequencing.

    A common weak answer: "FLR resets the whole SSD."

  13. [Spec] During a kexec into a new kernel, an NVMe controller still has commands outstanding from the old kernel. The new kernel's early boot clears and then re-enables Bus Master Enable (BME) before the NVMe driver loads. Why is this dangerous, and what is the correct order?

    Model answer

    When BME is clear, the function must not issue memory or I/O requests; MSI and MSI-X messages are memory writes, so they stop too. That makes clearing BME a strong containment step. But it does not cancel commands: the controller still holds its queue state and in-flight commands pointing at buffers and queue memory owned by the old kernel. As soon as BME is set again, the controller can resume DMA into those addresses, which the new kernel may have reused for its own data structures. The result is memory corruption that depends on timing, the hardest kind to reproduce.

    The correct order is: stop the controller before handing memory to anyone else. The old kernel's shutdown path should disable the controller (clear CC.EN and wait for CSTS.RDY to read 0, or do a normal shutdown with CC.SHN and wait for CSTS.SHST), and the new kernel's driver should reset the controller before enabling bus mastering for normal operation. Where the platform has an IOMMU, the new kernel can also keep DMA blocked by default until the driver has reset the device. Validation should include kexec and crash-kernel paths with I/O in flight, because these paths are rarely exercised and produce delayed, unrelated-looking corruption.

    A strong answer includes: BME semantics including MSI-X, BME clear as a pause not a cancel, the reuse hazard, controller disable before re-enabling, IOMMU default-deny, and testing the rare paths.

    A common weak answer: "Clearing BME resets the device."

  14. [Coding] Write the completion-queue consumer loop of a polled or interrupt-driven NVMe driver: phase handling, barriers, batching and the head doorbell. Then explain what goes wrong if the head doorbell is written too rarely.

    Model answer 
    struct nvme_cqe {               /* 16 bytes, little-endian */
    uint32_t dw0, dw1;
    uint16_t sqhd, sqid;        /* DW2 */
    uint16_t cid, status;       /* DW3: status bit 0 is the Phase Tag */
};

int cq_consume(struct cq *cq)
{
    int n = 0;
    for (;;) {
        volatile struct nvme_cqe *e = &cq->ring[cq->head];
        uint16_t st = READ_ONCE(e->status);
        if ((st & 1) != cq->phase)
            break;                          /* entry not yet written this pass */
        dma_rmb();                          /* read the rest of the CQE after the phase */
        sq_update_head(e->sqid, e->sqhd);   /* frees SQ slots */
        complete_cmd(e->sqid, e->cid, st >> 1, e->dw0);
        if (++cq->head == cq->depth) {
            cq->head = 0;
            cq->phase ^= 1;                 /* expected phase flips on wrap */
        }
        n++;
    }
    if (n)
        writel(cq->head, cq->head_db);      /* one doorbell per batch */
    return n;
}

    Key points: the expected phase starts at 1 (the queue memory is zeroed) and flips on every wrap; the phase must be read before the rest of the entry with a read barrier in between; the CQE's SQ Head Pointer (SQHD) is how the host learns which SQ slots are free; status is the upper 15 bits of the status word; and the head doorbell is written once per batch to save MMIO writes.

    If the head doorbell is written too rarely: the controller sees the CQ as full when its tail is one behind the last head value the host reported. It must not overwrite unconsumed entries, so it stops posting completions to that CQ, and every SQ feeding it stalls even though the host has processed everything. In a trace this looks like a device that "stops completing" with no error; the evidence is a CQ head doorbell that has not changed for a whole queue's worth of entries. Batching is fine; deferring the doorbell across polling loops or holding it until a timer fires is not.

    A strong answer includes: phase initialisation and flip, the read barrier, SQHD handling, batched doorbell, and the CQ-full stall with its trace signature.

    A common weak answer: code that checks a "valid" bit and clears it, or writes the doorbell for every entry with no reason.

    Learn it in: Chapter 25, Chapter 28, Module 27.

  15. [Architecture] In a virtualized host, a VM owns an SSD Virtual Function directly. Trace the path of one completion interrupt from the device to the guest's NVMe driver, and say where latency and jitter come from.

    Model answer

    The VF writes its CQE into guest memory (through the IOMMU's DMA translation for that VM), then sends its MSI-X message: a memory write to an address in the interrupt window. With interrupt remapping enabled, the IOMMU treats the message as an interrupt request, looks it up in the interrupt remapping table, and decides where to deliver it. With posted-interrupt support (available on common server platforms; behaviour is implementation-dependent), the interrupt can be recorded for the target virtual CPU and delivered directly if that vCPU is running, without a VM exit; if the vCPU is not running, the hypervisor is notified to wake or schedule it. The guest's interrupt handler then reads the CQE and rings the head doorbell, which, for a VF, is a direct MMIO write to the device with no exit.

    Latency and jitter sources: whether the vCPU is running at that instant (scheduling delay dominates tail latency in oversubscribed hosts); interrupt remapping and posted-interrupt processing; the guest's own interrupt affinity; host power states of the physical CPU; and IOMMU translation of the CQE write. Mitigations: pin vCPUs that run storage-intensive work, size guest queues to vCPUs, consider guest-side polling for latency-critical tenants, and monitor host scheduling delay as a storage latency signal.

    The diagnostic lesson: a guest-observed latency spike may have nothing to do with the SSD; correlate guest latency with host scheduler and interrupt statistics before blaming the device.

    A strong answer includes: MSI-X as a memory write, interrupt remapping, posted interrupts and the running-versus-not-running cases, doorbells without exits, and scheduler-driven jitter.

    A common weak answer: "Every interrupt causes a VM exit" or "the SSD interrupts the guest directly."

  16. [Architecture] The security team wants every SSD in the fleet to prove its identity and firmware integrity before it is trusted with data. Design the attestation flow and say what it does and does not protect.

    Model answer

    The standard building blocks are the Security Protocol and Data Model (SPDM) from the Distributed Management Task Force (DMTF), carried in-band over PCIe Data Object Exchange (DOE) mailboxes in configuration space, or out-of-band over the Management Component Transport Protocol (MCTP) through a baseboard management controller (BMC). The flow: negotiate version, capabilities and algorithms; retrieve the device's certificate chain and check it against the vendor's root of trust and your allow list; challenge the device to sign a fresh nonce with its private key (proving possession, so the certificate was not copied); request signed measurements of firmware components and compare them with a database of known-good values for the approved firmware versions; then admit the device, or quarantine it if anything fails. Record results in the fleet inventory.

    What it protects: substituted or counterfeit devices, unapproved firmware, and drift after unauthorised updates. What it does not protect: data on the wire (that requires Integrity and Data Encryption (IDE) on the link, or encryption at rest); bugs in approved firmware; physical attacks below the root of trust; and devices whose measurements are honest but whose firmware is vulnerable. It also depends on the vendor publishing reference measurements, which becomes a procurement requirement. Note that NVMe 2.4 updates asymmetric key-exchange algorithms in preparation for post-quantum cryptography; the attestation design should plan for algorithm agility rather than hard-coding today's algorithms.

    A strong answer includes: SPDM transport options, certificate plus challenge plus measurements, reference measurement database, explicit limits, and algorithm agility.

    A common weak answer: "Check the serial number and firmware revision in Identify Controller" (both are self-reported and can be forged).

  17. [Architecture] An NVMe over Fabrics target team wants the network card to write received data directly into SSD memory (peer-to-peer DMA into the SSD's CMB) instead of host DRAM. When is that worthwhile, and what can go wrong?

    Model answer

    Worthwhile when host memory bandwidth or CPU cache pollution is the bottleneck at very high throughput: data arrives from the network, goes into the SSD's CMB, and the NVMe Write command points at CMB addresses, so the data never crosses the host memory system. Linux supports this through its peer-to-peer DMA framework, which can expose a CMB as peer-to-peer memory, and the kernel NVMe target can be configured to use it.

    What can go wrong: (1) Topology: peer-to-peer works well when both devices sit under the same PCIe switch with ACS allowing direct routing; through a root complex, support and performance vary widely by CPU, and Linux only allows it for host bridges known to handle it. Traffic may silently go the slow way. (2) Isolation: allowing direct peer traffic conflicts with ACS redirect, which is what gives IOMMU isolation; multi-tenant platforms may not accept the trade. (3) Capacity: CMB size limits in-flight data, so queue depth may be capped by CMB space rather than SSD capability. (4) Error handling: a fault in the network card's write to the CMB appears at the SSD or switch, not where the network stack looks. (5) Operational complexity and fewer people who can debug it.

    I would approve it for a dedicated appliance with a fixed, qualified topology and measured gains (host memory bandwidth saved, CPU cycles, latency), and keep the conventional path as a fallback that can be switched at run time.

    A strong answer includes: the memory-bandwidth motivation, topology dependence, the ACS-isolation conflict, CMB capacity as a queue-depth limit, and a measured, reversible deployment.

    A common weak answer: "Zero-copy is always better."

NVMe architecture, queues, commands and data movement

Questions P51–P68 move above PCIe into NVMe itself: how many queues and how deep, what the doorbells and completions prove, how commands describe memory, and how a fleet tool should talk to a production controller without hurting it.

  1. [Spec] The Linux driver on a 64-CPU host asks the Lab SSD for queues with Set Features, Number of Queues (Feature Identifier 07h), using the value 0x003F_003F. The completion's Dword 0 returns 0x001F_001F. What was asked, what was granted, and how should the driver map 64 CPUs onto the result?

    Model answer

    Both halves of the request and of the result are zero-based counts: the upper 16 bits are completion queues, the lower 16 bits submission queues. 0x003F_003F asks for 64 I/O SQs and 64 I/O CQs; 0x001F_001F grants 32 of each. The admin queue pair is not counted. The controller may allocate fewer than requested, and the host must not create more queues than allocated. The granted values do not change until the next controller reset, and the feature is meant to be set during initialisation, before any I/O queues are created.

    Mapping: 32 queue pairs for 64 CPUs means two CPUs share each queue. Prefer CPUs that share a core or at least a NUMA node per queue, so submissions from both stay in the same cache domain and the queue's interrupt targets a CPU near both. The Lab SSD has 33 MSI-X vectors, so admin gets vector 0 and each I/O CQ gets its own vector 1–32. Two CPUs sharing an SQ need a lock (or per-CPU submission batching), so lock contention becomes a measurable cost at high IOPS. If the driver also wants dedicated poll queues, they come out of the same 32, leaving fewer interrupt-driven queues.

    The principal angle: queue count is a negotiated resource, and the host design must degrade gracefully when a device grants less, including on virtual functions that may grant very few. Test with a device that grants 1.

    A strong answer includes: zero-based decoding of both halves, admin queues excluded, "may grant fewer," set-before-create timing, a NUMA-aware mapping and the shared-SQ locking cost.

    A common weak answer: reading 0x3F as 63 queues, or assuming the controller always grants the request.

  2. [Architecture] The Lab SSD reports CAP.MQES = 0xFFF and CAP.CQR = 1. A team proposes creating 32 queue pairs of 4096 entries each "to never run out of queue space." Review the proposal.

    Model answer

    MQES is zero-based, so the maximum is 4096 entries per queue. CQR = 1 means queues must be physically contiguous (from the device's point of view: contiguous in I/O virtual address space, which on most hosts means contiguous allocations). Memory: 4096 × 64 bytes = 256 KiB per SQ plus 4096 × 16 bytes = 64 KiB per CQ, so 320 KiB × 32 = 10 MiB per drive; on a host with 24 drives that is 240 MiB of pinned, contiguous memory. That is affordable on a server but not free, and contiguous allocations of this size can fail on fragmented systems.

    The bigger issue is that queue depth beyond what the device can work on in parallel buys nothing but queueing delay. By Little's law, outstanding commands = throughput × latency: a drive doing 1.5 million IOPS at 100 µs device latency has about 150 commands in service. Commands beyond the device's internal parallelism just wait inside the SSD, adding latency while hiding congestion from the host's scheduler, which can then no longer reorder or prioritise them. Very deep device queues also make fairness between tenants harder, since the device, not the host, decides order. Linux defaults to 1024 entries per I/O queue (the nvme module parameter io_queue_depth), which is generous already.

    Recommendation: size queue depth to cover the device's parallelism with headroom (measure the knee of the throughput-versus-depth curve), keep the queueing in the host where it can be controlled, and revisit only with evidence of queue-full stalls.

    A strong answer includes: zero-based MQES, CQR meaning, the memory arithmetic, Little's law for useful depth, and the control and fairness argument for host-side queueing.

    A common weak answer: "Deeper queues always mean more performance."

    Learn it in: Chapter 25, Chapter 24, Mathematics for Storage and Links.

  3. [Trace] I/O SQ1 on the Lab SSD has 1024 entries. Read the capture and decide whether the last doorbell write is legal.

    #DirTime (µs)TypeFieldsMeaning

    3Host→Dev15.0MWr32Addr=0xFE60_1008 Len=1 Data=0x0000_000CSQ1 tail = 12
    Model answer

    It is not legal. Before row 3 the controller's head is 12 (from SQHD) and the tail is 16, so entries 12–15 are outstanding. Writing tail = 12 moves the tail forward modulo 1024 by (12 − 16) mod 1024 = 1020 entries. That would make 4 + 1020 = 1024 entries in a 1024-entry queue, but a queue can hold at most size − 1 = 1023 entries, because tail = head must mean "empty." From the controller's point of view, tail 12 equals head 12, so the queue suddenly looks empty while the host believes it has 1024 commands queued. The host has either overflowed the queue or, more likely, written a stale or corrupted tail value.

    What the controller does: the NVMe Base Specification defines an asynchronous event for an invalid doorbell write value, reported if the host has an Asynchronous Event Request outstanding; beyond that, how a controller reacts is implementation-dependent, so do not assume it will ignore the write. Likely host causes: two CPUs submitting to the same SQ without proper locking (one writes an older tail after the other wrote a newer one), a tail counter not wrapped modulo the queue size, or a driver that tracks free slots from the wrong CQE field.

    The fix is in the host. The lesson for tooling: a trace checker should compute queue occupancy from doorbells and SQHD values and flag any doorbell that implies more than size − 1 entries or a tail outside the queue.

    A strong answer includes: modular arithmetic, the size − 1 rule, why tail = head reads as empty, the asynchronous event, plausible host races and an automated check.

  4. [Architecture] A cloud provider emulates NVMe controllers in its hypervisor for general-purpose VMs. Why are doorbell writes expensive in that design, and how do shadow doorbells with event indexes help? What do they cost?

    Model answer

    In an emulated controller there is no hardware behind BAR0: every guest MMIO write to a doorbell traps into the hypervisor (a VM exit), which is far more expensive than a posted write to real hardware. With one doorbell per submission and one per completion batch, exits can dominate CPU cost at high IOPS.

    The Doorbell Buffer Config admin command lets the host give the controller two memory buffers: shadow doorbells, where the guest driver writes its latest tail and head values in ordinary memory, and event-index (EventIdx) buffers, where the emulated controller writes the value at which it next wants a real doorbell write. The guest updates the shadow value on every submission and performs the real MMIO write only when the new value has passed the controller's event index. While the emulation thread is busy polling the queues, it sets the index so that it is not notified, and exits mostly disappear; when it goes idle, it asks to be woken.

    Costs and risks: memory-ordering subtleties (the shadow value, the SQE and the event-index check need correct barriers on both sides, or commands are missed until the next doorbell); an extra memory read per submission; a polling thread in the hypervisor that consumes CPU; and a feature mostly useful to emulated controllers. Real hardware gains little because posted MMIO writes are cheap. A principal would require a stress test that targets the race between the guest's index check and the emulator going idle, the classic lost-wakeup bug.

    A strong answer includes: VM exit as the cost, the shadow plus event-index mechanism, the polling interplay, ordering races and lost wakeups, and why hardware controllers rarely benefit.

    A common weak answer: "Shadow doorbells make NVMe faster on all SSDs."

    Learn it in: Chapter 28, Module 27, Chapter 56.

  5. [Architecture] Design the host retry policy for failed NVMe commands across a fleet with SSDs from several vendors. How do the Do Not Retry (DNR) bit and Command Retry Delay fit in, and which commands must never be blindly retried?

    Model answer

    The CQE status field tells the host whether retrying the same command could succeed: DNR = 1 means the controller says the identical command will fail again, so do not retry it on this path. DNR = 0 means a retry may succeed. The Command Retry Delay (CRD) field, when the host has enabled Advanced Command Retry through the Host Behavior Support feature, selects one of three delay values the controller reports in Identify Controller (in units of 100 ms), so the controller can say "retry, but not before this long." Without that, hosts retry immediately, which can turn a transient internal condition into a retry storm.

    Policy: retry only DNR = 0 errors, honour CRD delays when enabled, cap retries per command (Linux's default limit is a handful, set by the nvme_core max_retries parameter), add a per-device retry budget so one sick drive does not consume the host, and on multipath systems prefer failing over to another path for path-related errors. Record every retry in telemetry, because a rising retry rate is an early warning that normal error counters miss.

    Never retry blindly: commands whose effect is not idempotent. A Zone Append that may have succeeded can append the data twice at a different location; a Compare and Write or other fused pair can see changed data; reservation commands and namespace management change state; and Format, Sanitize or firmware commands must never be retried by generic code. Ordinary Read, Write, Flush and Write Zeroes to fixed LBAs are idempotent at the block level.

    A strong answer includes: DNR semantics, CRD with Host Behavior Support and its units, retry caps and budgets, multipath failover, and the non-idempotent command list with Zone Append as the sharpest example.

    A common weak answer: "Retry everything three times."

  6. [Coding] Write C that builds the SQE for an 8 KiB Read from LBA 0x1000 of namespace 1 on the Lab SSD (512-byte LBAs) into the page-aligned buffer at IOVA 0x2_0000_0000, using Command Identifier 0x0042. Show the resulting dwords and the checks your code should do.

    Model answer 
    struct nvme_sqe {                    /* 64 bytes, little-endian */
    uint32_t cdw0;                   /* opcode 7:0, FUSE 9:8, PSDT 15:14, CID 31:16 */
    uint32_t nsid;
    uint32_t cdw2, cdw3;
    uint64_t mptr;
    uint64_t prp1, prp2;             /* DPTR */
    uint32_t cdw10, cdw11, cdw12, cdw13, cdw14, cdw15;
};

int build_read(struct nvme_sqe *s, uint64_t slba, uint32_t bytes, uint64_t buf, uint16_t cid)
{
    const uint32_t lba = 512, page = 4096, mdts_bytes = 128 * 1024;
    if (bytes == 0 || bytes % lba || bytes > mdts_bytes) return -EINVAL;
    if (buf % page || bytes > 2 * page) return -EINVAL;  /* this helper: PRP1+PRP2 only */
    uint32_t nlb = bytes / lba - 1;                      /* zero-based */
    if (slba + nlb + 1 > NSZE_NS1) return -ERANGE;

    memset(s, 0, sizeof *s);
    s->cdw0  = 0x02 | ((uint32_t)cid << 16);   /* Read, PRPs, not fused */
    s->nsid  = 1;
    s->prp1  = buf;
    s->prp2  = bytes > page ? buf + page : 0;
    s->cdw10 = (uint32_t)slba;
    s->cdw11 = (uint32_t)(slba >> 32);
    s->cdw12 = nlb;                           /* FUA, PRINFO, LR left 0 */
    return 0;
}

    Result: CDW0 = 0x0042_0002, NSID = 1, PRP1 = 0x0000_0002_0000_0000, PRP2 = 0x0000_0002_0000_1000 (the second 4 KiB page, not a list pointer, because exactly two pages are needed), CDW10 = 0x0000_1000, CDW11 = 0, CDW12 = 0x0000_000F (16 blocks minus one). MPTR and the remaining dwords are zero.

    Checks a principal reviewer looks for: NLB is zero-based (a classic off-by-one that reads one block too many or too few); the transfer respects MDTS (5 on the Lab SSD, so 25 × 4 KiB = 128 KiB); the LBA range fits inside NSZE; the buffer and PRP rules (PRP2 is a data pointer for two pages and a list pointer for more); structure packing and little-endian layout; the CID is unique among this SQ's outstanding commands; and that the SQE is fully written before the doorbell (write barrier). Using the namespace's actual LBA format (from Identify Namespace) instead of a hard-coded 512 is the production version.

    A strong answer includes: the correct dwords, zero-based NLB, PRP2 semantics, MDTS and NSZE checks, CID uniqueness and the barrier before the doorbell.

    A common weak answer: NLB = 16, or putting a PRP list pointer in PRP2 for a two-page transfer.

  8. [Architecture] Your fleet mixes SSDs that support SGLs for I/O commands and SSDs that do not. Define the host's policy for when to use SGLs instead of PRPs, and the validation it needs.

    Model answer

    For NVMe over PCIe, SGL support for I/O commands is optional and reported in Identify Controller (the SGL Support field); NVMe over Fabrics always uses SGLs. PRPs describe memory as a first pointer with an offset plus page-sized entries, which is efficient for page-aligned buffers but needs one entry per page and a list for anything over two pages. SGLs describe arbitrary byte-granular segments and can describe large contiguous regions with one descriptor, which suits large physically contiguous buffers, buffers with unaligned middle segments, and features such as bit-bucket descriptors (discarding parts of a read) and SGL-described metadata.

    Policy: use SGLs only where the controller reports support for the needed descriptor types, and where they are expected to win: large average segment sizes. Linux uses exactly this heuristic: the nvme module parameter sgl_threshold (default 32 KiB average segment size) chooses SGLs above it when the controller supports them. Keep PRPs as the universal fallback. For special cases that only SGLs can express (byte-granular unaligned buffers), require SGL support or fall back to a bounce buffer.

    Validation: SGL paths are less exercised in the field than PRP paths, so require tests across segment counts, segment boundaries, the last-segment and segment-chaining descriptors, and lengths that do not end on page boundaries; compare data and capture the descriptor fetch traffic. Treat an SGL support bit as "present," not "proven," until those tests pass for each firmware version. Make SGL use switchable per drive model so a field bug can be avoided without a kernel change.

    A strong answer includes: optional on PCIe and mandatory on fabrics, what SGLs express that PRPs cannot, the segment-size heuristic with Linux's parameter, per-model switchability and descriptor-level validation.

    A common weak answer: "SGLs are newer, always use them when supported."

  9. [Performance] Vendor A reports MDTS = 5 and vendor B reports MDTS = 3, both with CAP.MPSMIN of 4 KiB. What does that mean for a 1 MiB sequential read stream, and when does it matter?

    Model answer

    MDTS is a power of two in units of the minimum memory page size: vendor A allows 25 × 4 KiB = 128 KiB per command (the Lab SSD's value), vendor B 23 × 4 KiB = 32 KiB. The block layer splits larger requests; on Linux, max_hw_sectors_kb in sysfs shows the result. A 1 MiB read becomes 8 commands on A and 32 on B.

    What that costs: per-command overhead on both host and device. Each command means an SQE, a doorbell (possibly batched), an SQE fetch, a CQE, often an interrupt, and block-layer work. At 1 MiB requests and a few GB/s, that is tens of thousands versus hundreds of thousands of commands per second; at high throughput, B uses noticeably more host CPU and more of the device's command-processing capacity. On the link, data TLP counts are the same, because MPS limits payloads either way; only command-related TLPs grow.

    When it matters: hosts that are CPU-bound at high sequential throughput; devices whose command-processing rate, not NAND, is the limit; and workloads where large commands map well to the device's internal striping. When it does not matter: random small I/O, which never reaches either limit. Ask vendor B why MDTS is small (internal buffer sizing is a common reason), and measure throughput and CPU per GB at 1 MiB with both devices rather than ranking them on the field value.

    A strong answer includes: correct MDTS arithmetic, splitting and the sysfs view, per-command overhead versus unchanged data TLPs, the CPU-bound case, and measurement over specification.

    A common weak answer: "Vendor B has a quarter of the bandwidth."

    Learn it in: Chapter 31, Chapter 24, Module 53.

  10. [Spec] A database team writes 16 KiB pages to a namespace with 512-byte LBAs and asks: "Is a 16 KiB write atomic if power fails?" How do you answer from the specification and the device?

    Model answer

    NVMe defines atomicity per controller and per namespace. Identify Controller reports Atomic Write Unit Normal (AWUN), the size guaranteed atomic with respect to other commands, and Atomic Write Unit Power Fail (AWUPF), the size guaranteed atomic across a power failure. Both are zero-based counts of logical blocks. A namespace can report its own values (NAWUN, NAWUPF), which take precedence when the namespace says they are valid, and an atomic boundary (size and offset fields) so that writes crossing that boundary lose the guarantee. For 16 KiB on 512-byte LBAs, the database needs a power-fail atomic unit of at least 32 blocks, meaning AWUPF or NAWUPF ≥ 31, and the page must not cross an atomic boundary.

    If the device reports AWUPF = 0 (one block), the honest answer is no: after a power failure, the 16 KiB may be partly old and partly new at 512-byte granularity (a torn write), and the database must keep its own protection (a doublewrite buffer or full-page logging). Also confirm that the write path does not split the I/O (MDTS, the block layer and the file system can split a 16 KiB write into pieces, each atomic on its own but not together). Linux, from version 6.11, adds an explicit atomic-write interface for block devices (the RWF_ATOMIC flag, with limits reported through statx), which promises not to split; that is version-dependent and requires device support.

    Finally, ask for evidence: the vendor's power-fail test results at that size, and an in-house power-cut test with a checker for torn pages.

    A strong answer includes: AWUN versus AWUPF, zero-based units, namespace overrides and boundaries, splitting along the I/O path, the Linux interface as version-dependent, and power-cut testing.

  11. [Performance] After a file-system cleanup job deletes 20 TB across a cluster, read latency p99.9 spikes for an hour on some drive models. The file systems are mounted with online discard. What is happening, and what policy do you set?

    Model answer

    Online discard turns every file deletion into Dataset Management commands with the Deallocate attribute (or Write Zeroes with deallocate, depending on configuration) sent close to when the deletion happens. A mass deletion produces a storm of deallocations. Depending on the firmware, deallocation can be cheap (a mapping-table update) or expensive (mapping updates that must be persisted, background work triggered, sometimes processed in the same pipeline as reads). Some models handle the storm gracefully; others stall reads behind it, which matches the per-model pattern.

    Policy options: (1) Prefer batched discard (periodic fstrim) over online discard, scheduled off-peak and staggered across the fleet so not every drive trims at once. (2) Cap the size of individual discard commands (Linux's writable discard_max_bytes queue attribute) so each command is short and reads can interleave. (3) Rate-limit trim per drive. (4) For models that handle deallocation well, online discard may be acceptable; make the policy per drive model, based on measurement. Keep in mind why trim matters at all: it gives the SSD free space knowledge that reduces write amplification and garbage-collection latency later, so turning it off entirely trades a visible spike for a slower, invisible degradation.

    Qualification should include a deallocate-storm test with concurrent latency measurement, and vendor evaluation should treat "read p99.9 during deallocation" as a scored criterion.

    A strong answer includes: the deletion-to-deallocate path, firmware-dependent cost, batched and capped trim, per-model policy and the cost of disabling trim entirely.

    A common weak answer: "Turn off discard."

  12. [Architecture] Should the storage platform format its namespaces with end-to-end Protection Information (PI)? Describe what it protects, what it costs, and how you would roll it out.

    Model answer

    PI stores a small tuple with each logical block in its metadata: a guard (a CRC of the block's data), an application tag and a reference tag (typically derived from the LBA for Type 1). The host can generate PI and send it with the data, and the controller checks it on write and stores it; on read, the controller and host can check it again. The PRACT bit lets the controller insert or strip PI itself, and the PRCHK bits choose which fields are checked. Newer NVMe revisions add formats with larger guards (32- and 64-bit) for large blocks.

    What it protects: data corrupted anywhere between the host's generation point and the media and back, including inside the controller and DRAM, and misdirected writes or reads (the reference tag catches "right data, wrong LBA"). That covers failure modes no link-level CRC sees.

    Costs: the namespace must be formatted with an LBA format that includes metadata, which is destructive and must be planned; capacity for metadata; host CPU to compute guards (hardware-accelerated CRC helps); and the software stack must support it end to end (Linux has block-integrity support, but some stacks, file systems or virtualization layers drop it). Interoperability matters: check that every drive model implements the chosen PI type and guard size correctly, including at LBA boundaries and under Write Zeroes and deallocate.

    Rollout: start with controller-generated PI (PRACT) for media-to-controller protection, then host-generated PI where the stack supports it, on new deployments only, with corruption counters exported to telemetry. Any PI error in production is a sev-1 investigation, not a retry.

    A strong answer includes: the three fields and what each catches, PRACT and PRCHK, destructive format, stack support, and a staged rollout with telemetry.

    A common weak answer: "The SSD has ECC, so PI is redundant."

  14. [Spec] The Lab SSD reports CAP.TO = 0x14. On some cold boots after an unsafe shutdown, CSTS.RDY takes 12 seconds to become 1 and the host gives up. Who is wrong, and what did NVMe 2.0 add for this situation?

    Model answer

    CAP.TO is in units of 500 ms, so 0x14 = 20 means 10 seconds: the worst-case time the host should wait for CSTS.RDY to change after CC.EN changes. A controller that takes 12 seconds is exceeding the timeout it advertised, so the device is out of contract, and a host that gives up after 10 seconds is behaving correctly. The likely cause is recovery work after an unsafe shutdown (rebuilding mapping tables from media) that the firmware performs before reporting ready.

    NVMe 2.0 added Controller Ready Modes: a controller can report (in CAP) that it supports becoming ready independently of media, and a Controller Ready Timeouts register reports separate worst-case times for "ready with media" and "ready independent of media." A host that enables the independent mode can start talking to the controller quickly and learn per namespace when media is available, rather than waiting for everything. Recent Linux kernels read the new timeouts when present; behaviour is version-dependent.

    Actions: report the violation to the vendor with timestamps and the unsafe-shutdown history; ask whether they support the newer ready modes; test time-to-ready across the full matrix (cold, warm, after unsafe shutdown, after a firmware update, near end of life, at temperature corners) and add it to qualification. As a temporary mitigation, a host could wait longer for that model, but document it as a quirk with a removal condition, not a silent change.

    A strong answer includes: the 500 ms unit and 10 s result, the device as out of contract, unsafe-shutdown recovery as cause, NVMe 2.0 ready modes and timeouts named without invented offsets, and a test matrix.

  15. [Coding] Write a queue-ledger checker for an analysis pipeline. Given time-ordered SQ tail doorbells, CQEs (with SQHD and Phase Tag) and CQ head doorbells for one SQ/CQ pair, flag every protocol violation you can detect.

    Model answer

    The checker keeps the four pointers (SQ head and tail, CQ head and tail) and the expected phase, and applies the invariants: a queue holds at most size − 1 entries; SQHD can never advance past the SQ tail; the controller must not post into a full CQ; the Phase Tag must equal the expected phase, which flips each time the CQ tail wraps; the host must not move the CQ head past posted entries.

    def check_ledger(events, sq_size, cq_size):
    """One SQ feeding one CQ. events are time-ordered tuples:
    ('sqdb', tail)          host writes the SQ tail doorbell
    ('cqe', sqhd, phase)    controller posts a CQE at the CQ tail
    ('cqdb', head)          host writes the CQ head doorbell
    Returns (event index, problem) pairs."""
    out = []
    sq_tail = sq_head = cq_tail = cq_head = 0
    expect_phase = 1
    for i, ev in enumerate(events):
        if ev[0] == "sqdb":
            new = ev[1]
            if new >= sq_size:
                out.append((i, f"SQ tail {new} outside a {sq_size}-entry queue"))
                continue
            used = (sq_tail - sq_head) % sq_size
            added = (new - sq_tail) % sq_size
            if used + added > sq_size - 1:
                out.append((i, f"SQ overflow: {used} in use + {added} new > {sq_size - 1}"))
            sq_tail = new
        elif ev[0] == "cqe":
            sqhd, phase = ev[1], ev[2]
            if (cq_tail + 1) % cq_size == cq_head:
                out.append((i, "CQE posted into a full CQ (host head doorbell stale?)"))
            if phase != expect_phase:
                out.append((i, f"phase {phase}, expected {expect_phase}"))
            if (sqhd - sq_head) % sq_size > (sq_tail - sq_head) % sq_size:
                out.append((i, f"SQHD {sqhd} beyond SQ tail {sq_tail}"))
            sq_head = sqhd
            cq_tail = (cq_tail + 1) % cq_size
            if cq_tail == 0:
                expect_phase ^= 1
        elif ev[0] == "cqdb":
            new = ev[1]
            if new >= cq_size or (new - cq_head) % cq_size > (cq_tail - cq_head) % cq_size:
                out.append((i, f"CQ head {new} beyond entries posted"))
            cq_head = new
    return out

trace = [("sqdb", 7), ("sqdb", 0),                 # 8 entries in an 8-entry SQ
         ("cqe", 1, 1), ("cqe", 2, 1), ("cqe", 3, 1),
         ("cqe", 4, 1)]                            # 4th CQE into a 4-entry CQ, head never moved
for row in check_ledger(trace, sq_size=8, cq_size=4):
    print(row)

The second is a consequence of the first (after the overflow, tail equals head, so the ledger believes the SQ is empty), which is exactly why reports should lead with the first abnormal event and mark later findings as possibly derived. Production extensions: one ledger per queue built from Create I/O SQ/CQ commands, CID tracking from fetched SQEs, capture-gap awareness, and row references for every finding.

    A strong answer includes: modular occupancy arithmetic, the five invariants, phase flip on wrap, and the cascade caveat in reporting.

    A common weak answer: comparing raw pointer values without modular arithmetic, so every wrap is flagged.

  16. [Architecture] A fleet health agent will run on every storage host and periodically read SMART/Health, error logs and telemetry from every SSD. What rules must it follow so it never harms production I/O or hides events from the driver?

    Model answer

    (1) Respect asynchronous events. Some log pages, when read, clear the asynchronous event condition that caused the driver's Asynchronous Event Request to complete. Get Log Page has a Retain Asynchronous Event (RAE) bit; the agent should set it so that its reads never acknowledge events the driver or another tool still needs to see. (2) Keep admin traffic light. SMART/Health and error log reads are cheap; host-initiated telemetry capture can be large and may make the firmware gather internal state, which can cause latency spikes. Collect heavy logs on triggers, not on a timer. (3) Avoid fleet-wide synchronisation: add jitter so 100,000 drives are not polled in the same second, and stagger heavy collection per rack. (4) Bound everything: per-command timeouts, chunked reads within MDTS, and a circuit breaker that stops polling a drive that is already timing out, so the agent does not pile admin commands onto a sick controller. (5) Never issue state-changing commands (Set Features, Format, Sanitize, firmware) from the monitoring path. (6) Identify drives by stable identifiers (serial number and namespace identifiers), not by /dev/nvmeN names, which can change at reboot.

    Validate the agent like any production component: measure I/O latency percentiles with the agent on and off under load on each drive model, and include the heavy-collection path in the test.

    A strong answer includes: RAE, admin-versus-I/O interference, triggered heavy collection, jitter, circuit breaker, read-only discipline, stable identity and an on/off latency test.

    Model answer 
    # Get Log Page fields used (Command Dword 10 to 13):
#   LID   - Log Page Identifier for the host-initiated telemetry log (confirm in spec)

    return admin_get_log_page(lid=lid, lsp=CREATE if create else 0, rae=1,

  18. [Architecture] A storage appliance uses dual-port NVMe SSDs, each port connected to a different controller node, for high availability. How do multiple controllers, Asymmetric Namespace Access (ANA) and reservations combine to make failover safe?

    Model answer

    A dual-port SSD is one NVM subsystem with (at least) two controllers, one per port; a shared namespace is attached to both, so both nodes see the same data through different controllers. That raises three problems. Path selection: ANA lets each controller report, per ANA group of namespaces, whether access through it is Optimized, Non-Optimized, Inaccessible, in Persistent Loss, or in Change (transitioning). Hosts read the ANA log page and get asynchronous notifications when states change, so multipath software (Linux native NVMe multipath, with its I/O policies) chooses the right path and waits correctly during transitions rather than failing I/O. Write safety: if both nodes can write, they must coordinate. NVMe reservations (Register, Acquire, Release, Report) give fencing: the active node holds a reservation such as Write Exclusive, and on failover the survivor preempts the failed node's registration, so a half-dead node that wakes up cannot corrupt data. Identity: hosts must be identified by stable Host Identifiers so reservation ownership survives reconnections.

    Failover sequence: detect failure; the survivor preempts the old owner's reservation; it updates ANA states if the appliance controls them; I/O continues on the surviving path. Test it with the ugly cases: a node that pauses for a minute and resumes, a port that flaps, and failover in the middle of a large write stream. Verify no write from the fenced node lands after preemption.

    A strong answer includes: one subsystem and two controllers, ANA states and change notification, multipath behaviour during Change, reservations as fencing with preemption, host identity and the "zombie node" test.

    A common weak answer: "Multipath handles it," with no fencing.

  2. [Architecture] Your platform team wants to change Advanced Error Reporting (AER) severity settings on SSD root ports: make Completion Timeout fatal and mask Unsupported Request entirely, "to reduce noise." Review it.

    Model answer

    The Uncorrectable Error Severity register lets software choose, per error type, whether an uncorrectable error is reported as fatal or non-fatal; the Uncorrectable Error Mask register suppresses reporting (and logging, for most purposes) of a type. Defaults reflect the specification's judgement: for example, Completion Timeout and Unsupported Request are non-fatal by default; Surprise Down, Malformed TLP and Data Link Protocol Error are fatal by default.

    Making Completion Timeout fatal: fatal errors typically lead the OS (or DPC, if configured to trigger on fatal) to reset the link, which turns every slow completion into a link reset that affects all I/O to the device. The likely result is more outages, not fewer. Completion Timeout is often a symptom on the requester side whose correct response is at the transaction or driver level. I would reject that change unless evidence shows that continuing after a timeout risks data integrity on this platform.

    Masking Unsupported Request: URs often come from benign probing (configuration reads of non-existent functions, vendor tools). That is noise. But a UR can also reveal a real bug, such as an SSD DMA to an address that no longer decodes. Masking everything removes that evidence permanently. Better: keep reporting enabled, filter known-benign patterns in the fleet pipeline using the header log (who sent what), and alert on unexpected sources.

    General principle: severity and masking are policy with fleet-wide consequences, so each change needs a written rationale, a canary, and a measurement of outage and error-attribution effects.

    A strong answer includes: what severity and masking do, default severities, the outage amplification of a fatal Completion Timeout, the evidence loss from masking, and filtering in the pipeline instead.

    A common weak answer: accepting both changes because "fewer alerts is better."

  3. [Troubleshoot] On a new server platform the Linux kernel never logs AER errors for SSDs, even when you know errors occur. Where did the errors go, and how do you get the evidence into your fleet pipeline?

    Model answer

    The platform probably uses firmware-first error handling: system firmware keeps ownership of the AER registers and handles PCIe errors itself, then reports them to the OS through the Advanced Configuration and Power Interface (ACPI) Platform Error Interface, as Common Platform Error Record (CPER) entries delivered through the Generic Hardware Error Source (GHES) mechanism, or only to the baseboard management controller (BMC). The OS learns which features it controls through the ACPI _OSC negotiation at boot; the kernel log shows which capabilities (AER, DPC, hot-plug and so on) the OS was granted. If AER is not in that list, the Linux AER driver is not in charge and will not log in its usual format.

(2) A RAS daemon (for example rasdaemon) that stores these records in a queryable form. (3) The BMC's system event log, retrieved through its management interface. (4) Firmware settings: some platforms allow switching to OS-first handling, or forwarding all records to the OS.

    The fleet pipeline must ingest all three sources and normalise them into one event schema (device, error type, severity, timestamp, header if available), or the platform becomes a blind spot. Validate it by injecting a known correctable error on one server and confirming it arrives in the pipeline with the right attribution. Also note that firmware-first handling may itself take time and may reset devices in its own way, which changes recovery behaviour compared with OS-first.

  4. [Architecture] Design the automated recovery policy a storage service applies when an SSD stops responding: which actions, in what order, with what limits, and when to stop.

    Model answer

    Order the actions from least to most disruptive, and capture evidence before each step destroys it. (0) Before acting: snapshot what is cheap and still readable (kernel logs, AER and DPC status of the port, link status, the driver's queue state). (1) Command-level recovery: abort the timed-out command if appropriate. (2) Controller reset (CC.EN to 0 and back), which the Linux driver does on timeout; first try to read the controller's error and telemetry logs if the admin queue still works. (3) Function-level reset or a secondary bus reset of the slot if the controller reset fails, which also resets the PCIe state. (4) Slot power cycle, if the platform supports slot power control. (5) Quarantine: take the drive out of service, mark it for replacement and keep it powered for remote diagnosis if possible.

    Limits: a per-drive budget (for example, at most a few controller resets per hour; a drive that exceeds it goes straight to quarantine); a per-host and per-rack budget, so a platform-wide fault does not trigger hundreds of simultaneous resets and turn a partial outage into a total one; and a fleet-level brake that halts automation and pages a human when the rate of recoveries crosses a threshold, because that pattern means a common cause such as a firmware rollout, power event or switch firmware bug.

    Stop conditions: data integrity at risk (PI errors, critical warnings indicating read-only mode or volatile memory backup failure) goes straight to quarantine without reset loops. Every action is logged with its evidence, so the next engineer can see what was tried.

    A strong answer includes: escalating ladder, evidence capture before each step, per-drive and per-population budgets, a fleet-wide brake, and integrity-based immediate quarantine.

  5. [Spec] The Lab SSD sets CAP.NSSRS = 1. A tooling team wants to use NVM Subsystem Reset (NSSR) as the "big hammer" in automated recovery. What does it do, and what are the risks on single-port and dual-port drives?

    Model answer

    NSSRS = 1 means the controller supports NVM Subsystem Reset. The host triggers it by writing the value 0x4E56_4D65 (the ASCII characters "NVMe") to the NSSR register at offset 20h. It resets the entire NVM subsystem: every controller in it, including those reached through other ports, and on PCIe it typically causes the link to go down and retrain, so the host sees what looks like a surprise removal and re-insertion. After the reset, CSTS.NSSRO (NSSR Occurred) is set, so software can tell this reset from others.

    Risks: on a single-port drive the host must handle a link-down event (with DPC configured, it can trigger containment; without it, errors and possibly a hot-plug removal and re-add of the device) and must be prepared to re-enumerate. On a dual-port drive, NSSR on one port resets the controller used by the other host or node as well, so a "local" recovery action becomes an outage for the partner; in an HA appliance that can turn a single-node problem into a full outage. It also discards controller state that might have explained the original fault.

    Policy: allow NSSR only after controller reset and function-level options have failed, only when the tool knows the subsystem's topology (number of controllers and who else uses them), coordinated with the partner node for multi-port drives, and only when the platform's hot-plug and DPC handling have been tested with NSSR. Linux's nvme-cli provides a subsystem-reset command; it should not be the agent's default.

    A strong answer includes: the trigger value and register, subsystem-wide scope, link-down consequence, NSSRO, the multi-port blast radius and a gated policy.

    A common weak answer: "It is just a stronger controller reset."

  6. [Troubleshoot] After a planned datacentre maintenance window, the Unsafe Shutdowns counter in SMART/Health incremented on 30% of drives in one hall, although every server was "shut down cleanly." What happened, and why does it matter?

    Model answer

    The Unsafe Shutdowns counter increments when a controller loses power without having completed a normal shutdown: the host sets CC.SHN to request a shutdown and the controller reports completion through CSTS.SHST. If power is removed before that handshake finishes, or the host never performs it, the count goes up. "Shut down cleanly" at the OS level does not guarantee the NVMe handshake completed.

    Likely causes: the OS shutdown path did not wait long enough (Linux waits based on the drive's reported RTD3 Entry Latency (RTD3E) or its own shutdown_timeout module parameter, default 5 seconds, and some drives need longer); platform firmware removed power soon after the OS halted; a power-distribution sequence in that hall cut power before servers finished; or a kernel or driver version in that hall skipped the shutdown (for example during a kexec-based reboot path). The 30% concentrated in one hall suggests the facility or a server-image cohort rather than drive defects.

    Why it matters: on drives with a volatile write cache and no power-loss protection, unsafe shutdowns can lose acknowledged-but-unflushed data; on all drives they trigger longer recovery at next boot (which can exceed ready timeouts) and add wear. Evidence: compare the counter before and after per drive, cohort by hall, server image and drive model, and read the OS shutdown logs for the NVMe shutdown completion message. Fix the sequence, then add the counter's delta to post-maintenance checks.

    A strong answer includes: CC.SHN/CSTS.SHST handshake, RTD3E and shutdown_timeout, the cohort reasoning, data-loss and recovery consequences, and a post-maintenance check.

    A common weak answer: "The counter is unreliable; ignore it."

    Learn it in: Chapter 50, Chapter 57, Module 42.

  7. [Architecture] Two vendors both claim "full power-loss protection" (PLP). Design the evaluation that would let you trust the claim.

    Model answer

    Start from what PLP should guarantee: every write the controller completed (CQE posted) is durable after a sudden power loss, and metadata is consistent, so the drive comes back with correct data and no unexpected read errors. Drives with full PLP usually report no volatile write cache, so Flush is cheap; check what Identify Controller says (the Volatile Write Cache field) and whether the vendor's claim matches.

    Test design: a power-cut rig that removes power from the drive (not just the host) at random points, thousands of times per drive, across many drives; a workload that writes verifiable data (each block carries its LBA, a sequence number and a checksum) with a mix of sizes, queue depths, Flush and FUA, including writes in flight at the cut; an oracle that records which writes were acknowledged before the cut; after each power-up, verify that every acknowledged write reads back correctly, that unacknowledged writes are either old or new data (never garbage), and that atomicity guarantees (AWUPF) hold. Also run cuts during firmware activation, during format and sanitize, during garbage collection-heavy periods, at hot and cold temperatures, and late in the drive's life when capacitors have aged (accelerated aging if possible, or ask for the vendor's capacitor health monitoring and how it is reported).

    Score: any acknowledged-data loss is disqualifying; compare time-to-ready after cuts; check that the Unsafe Shutdowns counter increments; check that health reporting flags degraded capacitors before they fail.

    A strong answer includes: the durability definition tied to the CQE, a verifiable workload with an oracle, cut timing variety, aging and temperature, and capacitor health reporting.

  8. [Troubleshoot] In a fleet of 50,000 laptops, one SSD model occasionally fails to resume from suspend, leaving the system with a read-only root file system. How do you investigate, and how do you choose between a kernel quirk and waiting for a firmware fix?

    Model answer

    Suspend and resume exercise the least-tested paths: deep NVMe power states (non-operational states entered through Autonomous Power State Transitions (APST)), PCIe ASPM and L1 substates, D3hot or D3cold, and platform-specific power sequencing. Investigation: collect kernel logs from failures (typical signatures are a controller that does not become ready after resume, or I/O timeouts followed by reset failure); correlate failures with firmware revision, BIOS version, kernel version and whether the system used suspend-to-idle or deeper sleep; reproduce on a few units with thousands of suspend cycles while varying one factor at a time: disable APST, limit the deepest allowed state (Linux's nvme_core.default_ps_max_latency_us), disable ASPM substates, change the platform's sleep mode.

    Linux has quirk flags for exactly this class of problem (for example, quirks that disable the deepest power state or APST for specific models). Choosing: a quirk is fast and safe for users but costs battery life for every user of that model and tends to stay forever; a firmware fix is the right long-term answer but takes months to reach the fleet. A principal answer usually does both: apply a narrowly scoped mitigation (by model and firmware revision, through fleet configuration if possible rather than a kernel change), measure the battery cost, require the vendor fix with a reproduction case, and set a removal condition for the mitigation once fixed firmware reaches a defined share of the fleet.

    A strong answer includes: the power-state paths involved, single-variable experiments with specific knobs, quirks as scoped mitigations, the battery trade-off and a removal condition.

    A common weak answer: "Disable power management on all laptops."

  10. [Architecture] Define the automated action for each bit of the SMART/Health Critical Warning field in a large storage fleet.

    Model answer

    The Critical Warning field has bits for: available spare below its threshold; temperature above an over-temperature threshold or below an under-temperature threshold; NVM subsystem reliability degraded (media errors or internal errors); media placed in read-only mode; volatile memory backup device failed; and Persistent Memory Region read-only or unreliable (in newer revisions). A controller can also report these through asynchronous events, so the fleet should listen for events and not only poll.

    Actions: Spare below threshold: the drive is wearing out or remapping heavily; schedule replacement within a planning window, drain data proactively, and check the cohort for early wear (a workload or firmware problem). Temperature: check the platform first (fans, airflow, neighbouring drives), throttle or move load; repeated events on one slot are a facility issue. Reliability degraded: treat as a data-risk event; mark the drive suspect, stop placing new data, re-replicate data from it, and collect telemetry for the vendor. Read-only: the drive has protected itself; drain and replace immediately; the remaining data is readable but treat writes as failed. Volatile memory backup failed: the drive can no longer guarantee power-loss safety; immediately disable reliance on its cache (or remove it from service), because a power event would lose data. PMR read-only: applications using the PMR must stop writing there.

    For all: record the transition with timestamps and firmware revision, alert on rate changes by cohort, and verify that the action happened (a closed loop).

  11. [Troubleshoot] A replicated storage service sees a few unrecovered read errors per week per thousand drives. Each CQE reports a media error status and the Error Information log entry names an LBA. Design the host-side handling and the fleet analysis.

    Model answer

    Host-side handling for one error: the CQE status says the media could not return the data; the Error Information log entry gives the namespace, LBA and a vendor-specific detail. The service should (1) return the error to the replication layer, which reads the data from another replica; (2) rewrite the block on the failing drive with the good data, which lets the SSD place it on healthy media; (3) record the event. Do not retry the read endlessly; a small bounded retry is reasonable because some read errors are transient, but the drive has already performed its own internal recovery before reporting failure.

    Proactive measures: background scrubbing (periodically reading all data, or using the Verify command where supported to check readability without transferring data) finds latent errors before a second failure makes them unrecoverable; scrub rate is a trade between wear, performance impact and risk window.

    Fleet analysis: compute the error rate per drive model, firmware revision, age (power-on hours and percentage used), workload and temperature history. Look for clustering: errors concentrated on drives with high read-disturb workloads, on old data never rewritten (retention), on a specific firmware, or on a manufacturing lot. Compare with the vendor's specified uncorrectable bit error rate. Track whether drives with one error go on to have more (a predictor for proactive replacement). The goal is a decision rule: rewrite and continue, replace, or escalate to the vendor for a cohort.

    A strong answer includes: read from replica and rewrite, bounded retries, scrubbing with its trade-off, cohort analysis by model, firmware, age and workload, and a replacement rule.

    A common weak answer: "Replace any drive with a read error."

  12. [Spec] You need to line up events from an SSD's Persistent Event Log with host kernel logs and analyzer captures after an incident. What makes that hard, and how do you design for it?

    Model answer

    Three clocks: the host's wall clock (with its own drift and time-zone and synchronisation issues), the analyzer's capture clock (nanosecond resolution but an arbitrary origin), and the SSD's internal time. The Persistent Event Log records significant controller events (such as resets, firmware commits, thermal excursions, power-on and power-off, and changes reported elsewhere) with timestamps from the controller's notion of time, which may be time since power-on, or real time if the host has set it.

    NVMe's Timestamp feature lets the host set the controller's time (Set Features) and read it back (Get Features), including whether it was set by the host and whether the controller's clock stopped across a power cycle. Design: the fleet agent sets the timestamp at driver load and periodically re-syncs it; it records host-versus-device time offsets in telemetry, so later analysis can correct drift; it reads the Persistent Event Log with a known offset and records the log's generation information so overlapping reads are merged correctly.

    For analyzer captures, insert a marker the analyzer can see and the host can log at a known time (for example a recognisable MMIO write or a vendor-specific command at a recorded host time) so the analyzer timeline can be anchored.

    Even with all this, event order across clocks has an uncertainty window; correlation reports should state it ("within about 10 ms") rather than claim exact order when events are close. The Persistent Event Log is optional, so the design must degrade gracefully on drives without it.

    A strong answer includes: three clocks, the Timestamp feature and its status bits, periodic sync with recorded offsets, analyzer anchoring and explicit uncertainty windows.

    A common weak answer: "Sort everything by timestamp."

  13. [Troubleshoot] A latency-sensitive service wants to activate new SSD firmware without a reset (Firmware Commit with "activate immediately"). During a canary, p99.99 latency spikes to several hundred milliseconds at activation. What governs that pause, and how do you decide whether the rollout can continue?

    Model answer

    Activating new firmware without a reset requires the controller to pause command processing, switch images and resume. Identify Controller reports the Maximum Time for Firmware Activation (MTFA), in 100 ms units, as the worst-case time the controller will pause processing for an activation without reset; if an activation would exceed it, the controller rejects the immediate activation with an error status saying so, and the host has to use an activation that includes a reset instead. During the pause, commands queue up and their latency grows by up to that time, which is what the canary saw.

    Decision: compare the observed pause with MTFA (if the pause exceeds MTFA, that is a firmware defect to report); compare the pause with the service's latency budget and with the alternative (an activation with a controller reset, which also pauses I/O and adds reset risk, or draining the drive's traffic first). For a replicated service, the usual answer is to drain traffic from the drive (or the node) before activation, so the pause hits no user requests, then validate (firmware revision in Identify Controller, health, error counters, latency) before undraining. Roll out in waves with automated halt conditions (any drive not returning, error rates above the cohort baseline).

    Also watch for the asynchronous event the controller sends on activation and for any change in reported capabilities after activation (new firmware can change Identify data), which the driver may need to re-read.

    A strong answer includes: MTFA and its units, the pause mechanism, defect versus expected behaviour, drain-then-activate for latency-sensitive services, staged rollout with halt conditions and Identify re-read.

    A common weak answer: "Activation without reset has no impact."

  14. [Architecture] Design an error-injection strategy that proves your NVMe and PCIe error handling works end to end before a new platform ships. Which tools inject which faults, and what does each prove?

    Model answer

    Inject at every layer, because each layer's handling is separate code. Physical and data link: a protocol jammer or exerciser on the link can corrupt LCRC, drop or delay ACKs, inject NAKs and force link-down; this proves replay, Recovery, Surprise Down handling and DPC behaviour. Transaction layer: the jammer or a host-side tool returns poisoned completions, Unsupported Request or Completer Abort statuses, or withholds completions (Completion Timeout); this proves AER logging, driver error handlers and the attribution in fleet telemetry. Linux also has a software AER injector (built with the AER inject option) that simulates AER events in the kernel without a real fault; it tests the OS path only, not the hardware. NVMe level: Linux's NVMe fault injection (through debugfs, when built with fault injection support) can make commands complete with chosen status codes, testing retry and DNR handling; vendor test firmware can inject media errors, controller fatal status (CSTS.CFS) and slow commands. Platform: slot power removal, hot-plug, thermal stress, and power cuts to the whole server.

    For each injection, define the expected chain of evidence: what is logged where, what the driver does, what the application sees, what the fleet pipeline records, and the recovery time. A test passes only if all of these match; many bugs are in the evidence chain (an error that is handled but never reported).

    Run the suite on every new platform, firmware and kernel combination, keep the results as baselines, and treat any change in the evidence chain as a regression.

    A strong answer includes: layered injection with named tool types, what software injection cannot prove, expected evidence chains, reporting bugs as first-class failures, and baselines.

  15. [Troubleshoot] Forty SSDs across the fleet see a link-down and recovery about once a day each. All forty sit behind the same PCIe switch model; other drives of the same SSD model attached directly to root ports never do. The switch vendor says "the SSD dropped the link"; the SSD vendor says "the switch did." How do you settle it?

    Model answer

    The cohort already says something: the same SSD model is clean on root ports, so the SSD alone is not sufficient to cause it; the switch is necessary, at least as part of the combination. But "necessary" is not "at fault": the combination could expose a marginal SSD behaviour that root ports tolerate (for example, a timing edge in L1 exit or equalization) or a switch bug.

    Evidence to collect: the switch's own logs and LTSSM state history for the affected downstream ports (many switches keep per-port event logs and error counters), the root port's view of the upstream link (to rule out a switch-wide event), the SSD's persistent event log and telemetry after each event, and the time pattern (exactly every N hours suggests a timer or a periodic management action; temperature correlation suggests margin). Then a lab capture with an analyzer on one affected downstream link, triggered on the LTSSM leaving L0 unexpectedly, which shows who sent electrical idle or training sets first and whether any error preceded it.

    Discriminating experiments: swap in a different SSD model behind the same switch; move affected SSDs to root ports; update or roll back switch firmware on half of a cohort; disable ASPM on the switch ports for half a cohort. Each experiment changes one variable. With the analyzer showing the first party to leave L0 and the experiments separating firmware and power-state factors, the attribution becomes evidence-based, and both vendors can be given the same capture.

    A strong answer includes: necessary-versus-sufficient reasoning, switch per-port logs, periodicity, a triggered capture that shows who left L0 first, single-variable experiments and shared evidence for both vendors.

  16. [Architecture] Design the decommissioning process for 30,000 SSDs leaving the fleet, some of which held customer data. Compare Format NVM, Sanitize and physical destruction, and say how you prove the result.

    Model answer

    Format NVM changes the namespace's format and can request a secure erase setting (user data erase or cryptographic erase), but its scope is per namespace or all namespaces, depending on the controller, and it is not designed to cover every hidden area (spare blocks, caches) with the guarantees needed for media sanitisation. Sanitize is the purpose-built operation: it acts on the whole NVM subsystem, including caches and unallocated media, with three action types (Block Erase, Cryptographic Erase and Overwrite, each optional), runs in the background, continues across resets and power cycles until complete, and reports progress and result in the Sanitize Status log. If it fails, the drive enters a failure state that requires a specific recovery action. Cryptographic erase is fast (it discards the media encryption key) but depends on trusting that all data was encrypted with that key; block erase is slower but physically clears flash blocks.

    Process: inventory and chain of custody per serial number; run Sanitize with the strongest supported action (often crypto erase followed by block erase, if supported, for defence in depth); poll the Sanitize Status log until completion; verify by reading sampled LBAs (and expecting the documented post-sanitize pattern or errors); record the evidence per drive. Drives that fail Sanitize, cannot be accessed, or held the most sensitive data go to physical destruction. Align with the organisation's media sanitisation standard and with any customer contract terms.

    The NVMe 2.4 "restore manufacturing default settings" capability returns a subsystem to its as-shipped configuration; it is not a substitute for Sanitize unless the specification and the vendor say exactly what data it erases, so do not rely on it for data destruction without confirmation.

    A strong answer includes: Format versus Sanitize scope, the three Sanitize actions, persistence across power cycles, Sanitize Status verification, chain of custody, a destruction fallback and caution about the 2.4 manufacturing reset.

    A common weak answer: "Run a quick format and resell the drives."

  17. [Architecture] Leadership asks: "Which SSD model is the most reliable in our fleet?" Define the reliability metrics you would use, how you avoid misleading comparisons, and how vendors' MTBF claims fit in.

    Model answer

    First define failure. A drive "failed" if it was removed from service because of: no response or repeated recovery failure; a critical warning (read-only, reliability degraded, backup failure); data integrity events; or unrecovered errors above the replacement rule. Separate those categories, because they have different causes and costs. Then metrics: Annualized Failure Rate (AFR) = failures per drive-year of exposure (not per drive shipped), broken down by failure category; rate of service-affecting incidents (resets, timeouts) per drive-year; unrecovered read errors per petabyte read; and latency-SLO violations attributable to drives.

    Avoid misleading comparisons: compare models at similar ages (failure rates change over life: early failures, then a flat period, then wear-out), similar workloads (write-heavy versus read-heavy), similar platforms and temperatures; use exposure-based rates with confidence intervals, because a small cohort with zero failures is not proven reliable; and watch for selection effects (a model used only in a gentle tier looks better).

    Vendor Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF) figures are statistical predictions under specified conditions and convert to an AFR of roughly 8,760 hours ÷ MTBF; for 2 million hours that is about 0.44%. They are useful for planning but not comparable to your field AFR unless the failure definition and conditions match. Use field data to hold vendors accountable, and share it with them per firmware revision so they can act.

    A strong answer includes: a categorised failure definition, exposure-based AFR with confidence, age and workload normalisation, selection effects, and the correct MTBF-to-AFR relationship with its limits.

    A common weak answer: "The one with the fewest RMAs."

  1. [Performance] You are tuning an NVMe over TCP (NVMe/TCP) target that serves 4 KiB random reads and 128 KiB sequential writes to hundreds of hosts. Which transport-level settings and design choices matter most, and what does each trade?

    Model answer

    (1) Queue and connection mapping: each NVMe-oF I/O queue is a TCP connection. Map queues to target CPU cores so that one core handles one connection's protocol processing, keep receive-side steering of the network card consistent with that mapping, and avoid more queues than cores can serve. (2) Digests: header and data digests (CRC32C) protect PDUs end to end beyond TCP's own checksum. They catch corruption in network cards, offload engines and memory, but cost CPU unless offloaded; enable at least header digest where corruption risk justifies it, and measure. (3) In-capsule data: small writes can carry data inside the command capsule (up to the size the controller advertises), saving a Ready to Transfer (R2T) round trip; large writes use R2T and H2CData PDUs, limited by the controller's maximum H2CData size from connection initialisation. Bigger in-capsule limits help small-write latency but consume target buffer memory per queue. (4) Transport Layer Security (TLS): TLS 1.3 for NVMe/TCP protects confidentiality and integrity on untrusted networks at CPU cost, offloadable on some network cards. (5) TCP itself: socket buffer sizes, congestion control suited to the data centre, and avoiding Nagle-style delays for small PDUs. (6) Polling versus interrupts on the target, as on PCIe.

    For this mix: dedicated cores and steering for read IOPS, in-capsule data sized for small metadata writes, and large R2T transfers for the 128 KiB writes. Measure p99 latency and CPU per I/O with each change, because most of these settings trade CPU for latency or safety.

    A strong answer includes: queue-to-connection-to-core mapping, digest purpose and cost, in-capsule versus R2T, TLS, TCP settings, and measurement of CPU per I/O.

    A common weak answer: "Use jumbo frames," with nothing about the NVMe/TCP layer.

  3. [Troubleshoot] During a network congestion event, dozens of NVMe/TCP hosts lose their connections to a storage target at the same moment and then reconnect in a burst that overloads the target. Explain the keep-alive mechanics, and redesign the settings.

    Model answer

    When a host connects, it sets a Keep Alive Timeout (KATO). The host sends Keep Alive commands periodically (or, with traffic-based keep-alive where supported, ordinary commands count), and if the controller sees no qualifying command within KATO, it assumes the host is gone and tears down the association, freeing its resources. The host also detects failures and starts reconnection: Linux retries every reconnect_delay (default 10 seconds) until ctrl_loss_tmo (default 600 seconds) expires, and can fail I/O early with fast_io_fail_tmo if configured, so multipath can use another path.

    In congestion, keep-alive commands sit in the same TCP streams as I/O, so they are delayed; many associations hit KATO at once, the target tears them all down, hosts notice and reconnect, and the synchronised reconnects plus resubmitted I/O hit the target together. That is a classic thundering herd.

    Redesign: choose KATO longer than the realistic worst network stall, balanced against how fast you need to detect a dead host (for reservation fencing and resource cleanup); use traffic-based keep-alive where both sides support it, so busy connections do not need extra commands; add jitter to host reconnect timing (or rely on randomised backoff) so reconnects spread out; configure multipath so I/O fails over to another path quickly rather than waiting through a long reconnect; and give the target admission control for connection setup. Test by injecting congestion and measuring both false disconnects and failover time.

    A strong answer includes: KATO semantics and who enforces it, Linux reconnect parameters, why congestion synchronises failures, traffic-based keep-alive, jittered reconnects and a test with injected congestion.

    A common weak answer: "Set KATO to a very large value," which delays detection of genuinely dead hosts.

    Learn it in: Chapter 65, Chapter 68, Module 50.

  4. [Architecture] A team wants NVMe over RDMA (NVMe/RDMA) on RDMA over Converged Ethernet version 2 (RoCEv2) for its lowest latency tier. What must the network provide, what fails at scale, and when would you choose NVMe/TCP instead?

    Model answer

    NVMe/RDMA maps NVMe queues onto RDMA queue pairs: command and response capsules travel as RDMA sends, and data moves by RDMA reads and writes directly between host memory and the target, with very low CPU cost and latency. RoCEv2 traditionally expects a lossless or near-lossless Ethernet fabric, because RDMA transport recovery from packet loss is expensive (go-back-N style retransmission in many network cards). That is usually provided by Priority Flow Control (PFC) plus Explicit Congestion Notification (ECN) with a congestion control algorithm such as Data Center Quantized Congestion Notification (DCQCN).

    What fails at scale: PFC pause frames can spread congestion upstream (head-of-line blocking across unrelated flows), cause pause storms from a misbehaving network card, and in rare topologies deadlock; tuning ECN thresholds and congestion control across switch generations is specialised work; and debugging needs network-card and switch counters that storage teams rarely monitor. Memory registration, queue-pair limits and connection management add host-side complexity. Newer network cards handle loss better, which relaxes but does not remove these concerns.

    Choose NVMe/TCP when the fabric is a general-purpose network, when operational simplicity and debuggability matter more than the last tens of microseconds, or when hosts are diverse; choose RDMA for a dedicated, well-run fabric where the latency and CPU savings are measured to matter for the workload. Decide with a pilot that measures p99 latency and CPU per I/O for both under realistic congestion, not on an idle network.

    A strong answer includes: capsules and RDMA data movement, lossless expectations, PFC and ECN/DCQCN, failure modes at scale, the operational cost and a pilot under congestion.

    A common weak answer: "RDMA is faster, so it is always better."

    Learn it in: Chapter 67, Module 51, Chapter 68.

  5. [Architecture] Hosts in a 2,000-server NVMe/TCP environment currently use static configuration files listing every target. Design the discovery architecture that replaces them.

    Model answer

    NVMe-oF already has discovery controllers: a host connects to a discovery controller, reads the Discovery Log Page (a list of subsystems with their transport addresses and NVMe Qualified Names (NQNs)), and then connects to the I/O controllers it wants. A per-target discovery controller does not scale operationally, because hosts must still know every target. A Centralized Discovery Controller (CDC) solves this: subsystems and hosts register with it, an administrator applies zoning (which hosts may see which subsystems), and each host receives a filtered Discovery Log Page. Hosts keep a persistent connection to the discovery controller and receive asynchronous event notifications when their discovery information changes, so new namespaces or subsystems appear without polling. Automated discovery of the CDC itself (for example through DNS-based service discovery on the local network) removes the last static configuration item.

    Design points: redundancy (at least two CDC instances, with consistent state); zoning as security policy, managed from the same source of truth as host and tenant inventory; NVMe in-band authentication (DH-HMAC-CHAP) and TLS so that a host cannot claim another host's NQN; a migration plan that runs static and discovered configurations side by side and compares them; and monitoring of discovery health (registration counts, change events, connection failures). Failure analysis: if the CDC is down, existing I/O connections keep working; only changes and new connections are affected, which is the right failure mode to verify.

    A strong answer includes: discovery log pages, CDC registration and zoning, persistent connection with change notifications, redundancy, authentication tied to NQNs, and graceful failure behaviour.

    A common weak answer: "Put the target list in a configuration management tool."

  6. [Architecture] NVMe 2.4 reportedly lets Command and Feature Lockdown settings persist across reboots and adds separate lockdown controls for virtualized environments. Your security team wants to lock down firmware commands on all drives permanently. What do you ask before agreeing?

    Model answer

    Command and Feature Lockdown (present in earlier revisions) lets an authorised party prohibit specific admin commands or Set Features identifiers on a given interface, for example preventing in-band hosts from issuing firmware download and commit, while still allowing them through a management interface. The 2.4 improvements, as reported in coverage of the release, make lockdown settings able to persist across reboots and allow separate controls for virtualized environments (so a host can lock down what guests on secondary controllers can do). The exact scope, fields and persistence rules must be read in the NVM Express Base Specification, Revision 2.4; the course has not verified them.

    Questions before agreeing: (1) Recovery path: if in-band firmware commands are permanently prohibited, how do we update firmware for a critical bug? Through NVMe Management Interface (NVMe-MI) out of band via the BMC, through a specific unlock procedure, or not at all? Test that path before locking anything. (2) Who can unlock, and how is that authority protected and audited? (3) Scope: which commands and features, which interfaces (in-band host, guests, management endpoint)? Locking Format or Sanitize might break decommissioning. (4) Persistence semantics: does lockdown survive Sanitize, firmware update, or the 2.4 manufacturing reset? (5) Fleet heterogeneity: drives without 2.4 support need a different control, so policy must be per capability. (6) Failure modes: a mistaken lockdown applied fleet-wide could be very expensive to reverse.

    Plan: pilot on a small cohort, exercise firmware update and decommissioning through the approved path, and only then roll out in waves.

    A strong answer includes: what lockdown does, honest scoping of the 2.4 change, the recovery path, authority and audit, scope and persistence interactions, and a pilot.

    A common weak answer: "Locking everything is more secure, do it."

  7. [Architecture] A confidential-computing platform wants to assign an SSD Virtual Function directly into a trusted execution environment (TEE) virtual machine whose memory the hypervisor cannot read. Map the roles of SPDM, IDE, TDISP and the NVMe 2.4 TDISP information package.

    Model answer

    The problem: the hypervisor, host OS and PCIe fabric are outside the TEE's trust boundary, yet the VM wants a device that can DMA into its private memory. The building blocks: SPDM authenticates the device and its firmware measurements and establishes session keys; PCIe Integrity and Data Encryption (IDE) uses those keys to encrypt and integrity-protect TLPs on the link (or end to end through switches), so the untrusted fabric cannot read or modify traffic; TDISP defines how a device interface (here, a VF) is configured, locked so its configuration cannot be changed by the untrusted host, attested to the TEE, and then moved to a running state, with an error state if anything changes after locking. Only after the TEE has verified the locked interface does it accept DMA to private memory.

    Where NVMe 2.4 fits: coverage of the release describes a TDISP information package through which an NVMe controller reports detailed state information, which the TEE (or a migration orchestrator) can use to validate that the controller behind the locked interface is in the expected state. Its exact contents and the commands that return it must be read in the 2.4 specification.

    Open questions a principal raises: performance cost of IDE at line rate; how NVMe-specific state (namespaces attached, features set by the host before locking) is covered by what the TEE verifies; error handling, since a TDISP error state stops the VM's storage; and how live migration interacts with locked interfaces (see the 2.4 Exported NVM Subsystem migration flow). Plan a proof of concept with one platform and one SSD, because ecosystem support is still maturing.

    A strong answer includes: the trust boundary, SPDM for identity and keys, IDE for link protection, TDISP for lock and attest, the 2.4 package as state evidence named without invented details, and the open questions.

    A common weak answer: "Encrypt the disk and pass through the VF."

  8. [Architecture] A log-structured cache service suffers from high write amplification on conventional SSDs. Compare Zoned Namespaces (ZNS) and Flexible Data Placement (FDP) as remedies, and recommend one.

    Model answer

    Write amplification comes from the SSD mixing data with different lifetimes in the same erase units, so garbage collection must copy still-valid data. Both features let the host separate data by lifetime, with different contracts. ZNS exposes zones that must be written sequentially and reset explicitly; the host takes over placement and much of the garbage-collection responsibility, and the device can use far less over-provisioning and mapping memory. Write amplification inside the device can approach one, but the host software must respect zone rules (write pointer, active and open zone limits, Zone Append for concurrent writers), which is a large change to file systems and applications. FDP keeps the conventional block interface: the host tags writes with placement identifiers that map to reclaim units, so data with similar lifetimes lands together; if the host tags well, garbage collection copies less; if it does not, the drive still works like a normal SSD. FDP is a hint-based contract, so the host gets no hard guarantee, but adoption is incremental and failure is graceful.

    For a log-structured cache, data already arrives in large sequential segments with known lifetimes, which suits ZNS well; but the deciding factors are software cost and fleet diversity. If the service's storage engine can be adapted and the drive supply includes ZNS models, ZNS gives the strongest results. If the team must run on mixed drives, keep a conventional fallback, and move quickly, FDP is the better first step. I would pilot FDP with the service's existing segment structure, measure device write amplification through vendor or standard statistics, and keep ZNS as the second step if FDP's gain is insufficient.

    A strong answer includes: the lifetime-mixing root cause, hard versus hint contracts, host software cost, graceful degradation, a measured pilot and a reversal condition.

    A common weak answer: "ZNS is the future; rewrite everything."

    Learn it in: Chapter 59, Module 44, Chapter 58.

  9. [Architecture] Build the scorecard for choosing between three SSD vendors for a five-year, high-volume purchase. What do you measure beyond datasheet IOPS, and how do you weight it?

    Model answer

    Categories and examples: (1) Correctness and robustness (gate, not score): data integrity under power cuts, error injection and reset tests; protocol compliance in your qualification suite; no hangs requiring power cycles.   10. [Spec] A vendor's datasheet says "NVMe 2.4 compliant." The drive's Version register reads 0x0002_0400. What do you know, and what do you still have to ask?

    Model answer

    The Version register holds the major version in bits 31:16, the minor version in bits 15:8 and the tertiary version in bits 7:0, so 0x0002_0400 means 2.4.0: the controller claims conformance to the NVM Express Base Specification, Revision 2.4. That tells you which revision's mandatory requirements the controller claims to meet. It does not tell you which optional features are implemented, and most of what matters in the 2.4 release (rate limiting, Exported NVM Subsystem migration, lockdown persistence, voltage monitoring, idle I/O exit latency limits, the manufacturing reset, the TDISP information package, post-quantum-ready key exchange) is reported through capability fields, supported log pages and features, and the Commands Supported and Effects log, not through the version number. It also tells you nothing about which command set specification revisions (NVM Command Set 1.3, ZNS 1.5 and so on) or transport revision are implemented.

    Ask for: a feature matrix that maps each 2.4 feature you care about to its support status and firmware revision; results from an independent conformance programme (for example, an NVMe interoperability test lab's test suite), with the test suite revision; and errata lists. Then verify on the device: read Identify Controller, the supported log pages and the Commands Supported and Effects log, and exercise each feature you plan to use. Drivers should also branch on capability fields, not on the version number.

    A strong answer includes: correct decoding of VS, mandatory versus optional, capability fields as the real evidence, command set and transport revisions as separate, independent conformance testing and on-device verification.

    A common weak answer: "It supports all 2.4 features."

  11. [Architecture] Design the architecture of an in-house trace-analysis platform that ingests PCIe analyzer exports, kernel logs and NVMe logs, and produces root-cause candidates engineers can trust.

    Model answer

    Layers: (1) Ingest and provenance: parsers for each analyzer export format, kernel and RAS logs, and NVMe log pages; every record keeps its source, file, row, capture settings and capture-loss markers, so any conclusion can be traced back to raw evidence. (2) Normalisation: a common event schema (time, direction, layer, type, identifiers such as Requester ID, Tag, queue, CID, address) with clock alignment and explicit uncertainty windows between sources. (3) Correlation: reconstruct transactions (requests with their completions), then NVMe commands (doorbell → SQE fetch → data → CQE → interrupt), using a queue ledger built from Create Queue commands and doorbell addresses (for the Lab SSD, SQy tail at BAR0 + 0x1000 + 8y). (4) Checking: a rule engine of protocol invariants (4 KiB crossing, tag reuse, Byte Count sequences, queue occupancy, phase, ordering of CQE versus MSI-X) and statistical baselines (latency distributions versus golden traces of the same workload). (5) Reporting: first abnormal event, the chain of consequences, candidate root causes ranked with the evidence for and against each, explicitly listing missing evidence and the next experiment.

    Trust comes from testing the platform itself: a corpus of synthetic traces with injected, known faults (including capture gaps and decoy errors), scored for detection and false positives on every release; versioned rules with spec references by name; and a rule that every finding links to raw rows. If machine-learning components are added, keep them as rankers of candidates on top of deterministic checks, never as the source of protocol facts.

    A strong answer includes: provenance, normalised schema with clock uncertainty, transaction and command reconstruction, invariant rules plus baselines, evidence-linked reports, and a fault-injected test corpus.

    A common weak answer: "Feed the trace to an AI and ask what went wrong."

  12. [Coding] Review this snippet from a fleet latency report. It computes a "fleet p99 read latency" from per-drive, per-minute samples. What is wrong with it, and how would you fix it?

    p99s = []
for drive in drives:
    for minute in minutes:
        lat = completed_read_latencies[drive][minute]     # only I/Os that completed
        lat.sort()
        p99s.append(lat[int(0.99 * len(lat))])
fleet_p99 = sum(p99s) / len(p99s)
print(f"fleet p99 = {fleet_p99:.1f} us")
    Model answer

    Problems, in order of how badly they mislead: (1) Averaging percentiles is not a percentile. The mean of per-minute p99 values has no defined meaning for the population; a few terrible minutes on a few drives disappear into thousands of good ones. (2) Equal weighting: a minute with 10 samples counts as much as one with a million; idle drives dominate. (3) Survivorship: only completed I/Os are included, so timed-out or failed reads, the worst latencies of all, are excluded. They should be counted, at least as values at or above the timeout. (4) Edge cases: empty lists raise an error; with fewer than 100 samples, "p99" is just the maximum, and the index formula is a crude estimator. (5) Measurement bias: if samples come from a closed-loop load generator, stalls reduce the number of samples taken during the stall (coordinated omission), so the tail is understated before this code even runs.

    Fix: collect latency histograms with fixed, log-spaced buckets per drive per interval (cheap to store, mergeable); merge histograms across the population and time range you care about, then compute percentiles from the merged histogram; count failures and timeouts as a separate rate and include them in the tail at the timeout value; report sample counts and the worst drives alongside the fleet number (a fleet percentile can hide a per-drive outage); and for benchmarks, use open-loop load or correct for coordinated omission. State the definition of the metric in the report itself.

    A strong answer includes: averaging percentiles, weighting, survivorship of failures, small-sample edges, coordinated omission and mergeable histograms as the fix.

    A common weak answer: fixing only the empty-list crash.

  13. [Performance] Write the benchmark methodology your team must follow when comparing SSD models, so that results are reproducible and predict production behaviour.

    Model answer

    (1) Environment: record platform, BIOS, kernel, driver parameters, PCIe link speed and width, NUMA placement of the benchmark threads relative to the SSD's root port, IOMMU mode, CPU frequency policy, drive firmware and temperature; pin threads and keep the configuration identical across models. (2) Preconditioning: bring each drive to steady state before measuring (for example, sequentially fill the whole capacity, then run the target workload until throughput and latency stop drifting, following an established performance test specification such as SNIA's), because fresh-out-of-box results are much better than sustained behaviour. (3) Workloads: measure both throughput-versus-latency curves (vary queue depth and job count) and fixed-rate tests (for example, with fio's rate limiting at the production IOPS target), because production systems run at a chosen load, not at saturation. Include mixed read/write ratios, block sizes matching production, deallocation and flush patterns, and long runs (hours) to expose periodic background work. (4) Tools and flags: direct I/O to bypass the page cache, a modern asynchronous engine (io_uring or libaio), non-repeating random offsets across the full range, time-based runs with ramp time excluded, latency percentiles reported up to p99.99 with sample counts. (5) Statistics: several drives per model from different lots, repeated runs, reported variation; never one run on one drive. (6) Thermal: run at the platform's real airflow, record throttling counters. (7) Outputs: raw logs and configurations archived so anyone can rerun.

    A strong answer includes: environment capture, steady-state preconditioning, fixed-rate as well as saturation tests, correct tool flags, multiple drives and runs, thermal realism and archived raw data.

    A common weak answer: "Run fio with iodepth=256 for one minute and compare IOPS."

  14. [Architecture] A kernel team proposes changing how the NVMe driver chooses queue counts and interrupt mappings. Your fleet runs 20 SSD models from 6 vendors on 8 server platforms. Design the validation and rollout so the change cannot cause a fleet-wide incident.

    Model answer

    Start by finding what the change touches: queue negotiation (Number of Queues responses that differ by model, including drives that grant very few), MSI-X vector allocation (models with fewer vectors than queues), CPU hotplug and interrupt migration, suspend and resume, reset and error recovery paths that recreate queues, and poll-queue configuration. Then build an interoperability matrix: 20 models × 8 platforms is 160 combinations, too many for full coverage of every test. Run the core functional and stress suite on every combination that exists in the fleet (weighted by population), and use pairwise coverage for parameter combinations (queue counts, vector counts, CPU counts, kernel parameters). Include the unusual devices: VFs with tiny queue grants, drives with known quirks, the oldest firmware still deployed.

    Measure: correctness (no hangs, no lost completions, recovery works after injected resets), performance (IOPS, latency percentiles and CPU per I/O against the old kernel), and evidence quality (logs and telemetry still work). Rollout: canary hosts covering every model and platform combination, then waves by population with automatic halt conditions (timeouts, resets, latency regression beyond a threshold per model), and a fast rollback path (boot to the previous kernel or a module parameter that restores old behaviour). Keep per-model quirk handling explicit, so a single model's problem can be mitigated without reverting the whole change.

    A strong answer includes: identification of touched paths, a population-weighted matrix with pairwise reduction, extreme devices, multi-dimensional pass criteria, canaries covering every combination, halt conditions and a per-model escape hatch.
