Chapter 56 showed how Single Root I/O Virtualization (SR-IOV) and NVMe virtualization combine to give each virtual machine (VM) its own NVMe controller. This module studies what happens at the edges of a Virtual Function's (VF's) life: reset, reassignment, interrupt delivery through a hypervisor, fault attribution by Requester ID, and the data a VF leaves behind. That is where SR-IOV deployments fail in production.

Orientation

Chapter 56 taught the SR-IOV Extended Capability, VF Routing IDs (RIDs) and BARs, primary and secondary controllers, Virtual Queue (VQ) and Virtual Interrupt (VI) resources and the Virtualization Management command, with a bring-up trace. Here the central concept is the VF as an isolation and reset boundary: what a Function Level Reset (FLR) clears and what it leaves, in which order a hypervisor must tear down and hand over a VF, how interrupts and faults are attributed, and how to prove from a trace whether the device or the host is at fault.

  • SR-IOV: everything in it, especially the VF RID formula and the four Virtualization Management actions.
  • Advanced Error Handling: Access Control Services (ACS), IOMMU groups.
  • PCIe Reset and NVMe Reset: FLR versus controller reset versus NVM Subsystem Reset.
  • MSI-X and DMA: vector tables, I/O virtual addresses (IOVAs).
  • Namespaces: attachment to controllers.

Where it goes next: 2026 NVMe 2.4 Updates covers Exported NVM Subsystems and live migration, which rest on the reset and state boundaries studied here; Validation Engineering turns the failure patterns into a test campaign.

Kid story

A hotel has one big kitchen and many rooms. Each room has its own phone line to the kitchen and its own little cupboard where the kitchen leaves food. When a guest checks out, the hotel must do three things in the right order: hang up the guest's phone line so no more orders come, wait until the kitchen has finished delivering any food already on the way, and only then lock the room. If the hotel locks the room first, a waiter arrives with a tray and finds the door shut, and the tray ends up on the floor.

And one more thing: the cupboard. Hanging up the phone does not empty the cupboard. If the hotel forgets to clear it, the next guest opens it and finds the last guest's leftovers.

Analogy boundary

What it represents. The room's phone line is the VF's register set and doorbells; hanging up is the FLR. Food on the way is DMA already issued by the device. Locking the room is the hypervisor removing the guest's IOMMU mappings. The tray on the floor is an IOMMU fault. The cupboard is the namespace attached to that VF's secondary controller; its contents survive FLR.

What it hides. Two separate parties manage the room: the PCIe side (the VF, reset by FLR) and the NVMe side (the secondary controller, its resources and its namespace attachments, managed through the primary controller). The kitchen is also shared: a reset of the whole kitchen (PF reset, link reset, NVM Subsystem Reset) disturbs every room.

Where it misleads. In a hotel, a waiter who finds a locked door simply goes back. On PCIe, a posted Memory Write that the IOMMU blocks is dropped, logged and never reported to the device as a failure, so the device thinks it delivered. And "waiting for food on the way" is not guaranteed by any single signal: the device's Transactions Pending indication covers its own outstanding non-posted requests, not posted writes already sent.

The central concept on the ladder: the VF as an isolation and reset boundary

Each hotel room has its own phone and cupboard. Hanging up the phone stops orders; it does not empty the cupboard. Lock the room only after the last tray has arrived.

A VF is a separate PCIe function with its own RID, registers, queues and vectors. FLR resets that function and its NVMe controller state. Namespace contents, secondary-controller resources and attachments are managed through the PF and survive.

The device keeps per-function register files, doorbell arrays, MSI-X tables and DMA contexts tagged with the RID. The IOMMU picks translation tables by RID. FLR clears the per-function state inside the device; IOMMU state is the host's to change.

The PCI Express Base Specification defines SR-IOV, FLR, Transactions Pending and ACS. NVMe Base 2.4 defines primary and secondary controllers, VQ/VI resources and Virtualization Management, plus 2.4 rate limiting and exported subsystems. What a secondary keeps across VF FLR must be confirmed in Base 2.4's virtualization text.

Configuration requests to the VF's RID start FLR; MMIO to the VF BAR drives the controller; DMA and MSI-X writes leave with the VF's RID; management commands go through the PF's admin queue with RID 0x0100.

In a capture: a CfgWr0 to RID 0x0101, then silence from 0x0101 until a guest enables it again. Any MWr/MRd with RID 0x0101 after the IOMMU unmap is a fault candidate; any guest-pattern DMA with RID 0x0100 is an isolation red flag.

Order the evidence: was there DMA after unmap (host lifecycle bug) or DMA with the wrong RID (device bug)? Was data visible to a new tenant (missing erase)? The answer decides who fixes it.

  • Neither specification says a hypervisor must erase a namespace between tenants; that is a product and security policy.
  • The specifications do not define VFIO, posted interrupts or how a hypervisor schedules vCPUs.
  • We have not verified, and do not state, Base 2.4 field layouts for rate limiting or exported-subsystem migration. Read them in Base 2.4 and the Revision Changes document.
  • Whether every secondary-controller property survives a VF FLR must be read in Base 2.4; do not assume from one device's behaviour.

Never assume FLR clears media. Never assume Transactions Pending = 0 means no DMA is in flight. Never assume a fault logged against the PF RID was caused by PF software.

Hardware view

Participation and observability
CategoryItems
Must participateSSD with SR-IOV and NVMe virtualization; root port with ARI forwarding; IOMMU with DMA remapping; interrupt remapping for safe guest vectors.
May participatePCIe switch with ACS; Address Translation Services (ATS) if the device caches translations; posted-interrupt hardware; NVMe 2.4 rate limiting in the device.

Internal, unobservableHow the device maps VFs to internal queues and NAND; how firmware quiesces a VF during FLR; vCPU scheduling inside the host.

Where FLR stops at the hardware. Inside the device, FLR of a VF must abort or complete that function's outstanding work and discard its queue state, without touching other functions' contexts. Firmware commonly implements this by draining the VF's command slots from shared engines. A device that cannot cancel a NAND operation quickly may still emit a late completion write for that VF if its design is wrong; a correct design suppresses DMA for a reset function. Only an analyzer sees the difference. Implementation-dependent.

ATS caveat. If a device uses ATS, it holds cached translations; changing a VF's domain then also requires invalidating the device's translation cache. Most NVMe SSDs do not use ATS today, but check before assuming that an IOMMU unmap is immediately effective. Uncertain for any specific product.

Ordering. PCIe ordering lets a VF's posted writes (data, CQE, MSI-X) pass nothing that came before them in the same direction, so a CQE write is never seen before its data. Configuration writes to start FLR are non-posted; their completion means the write was accepted, not that the reset has finished. Errors. A device DMA blocked by the IOMMU becomes an Unsupported Request completion for a read, and a silently dropped, logged write for a posted MWr. Errors that belong to one function (for example a completion timeout on a VF's read) are reported with that function's identity; link errors belong to all functions.

Why it is ambiguous. From the log alone, two very different stories fit. H1, device isolation bug: VF2's DMA leaves with the PF's RID, so it is translated in the host's domain and faults; this is a security defect. H2, host software bug: the management agent passed a stale or freed buffer address in a PF admin command; the number coincides with VM B's IOVA range because IOVA spaces are per domain and numerically overlap. Conclusion here: H2, high confidence with this capture. Without the capture, both remain open and the RID audit test from the validation section is the safe next step.

Labs

Beginner lab: classify state across a VF FLR

Objective. For each piece of state, decide whether VF FLR clears it, and who owns it. Safe lab (paper).

Prerequisites. Chapter 56; PCIe Reset.

Setup. The list: VF1 CC and CSTS; VF1's I/O queues; VF1's MSI-X table; VF Enable in the PF; secondary CNTLID 2 VQ/VI assignment; NS 5 attachment; NS 5 data; VM A's IOMMU domain; interrupt-remapping entries; VF2's queues.

Procedure. Make a table: state · owner (device-VF, device-primary, host) · cleared by VF FLR? · cleared by what otherwise.

Observations. Notice that the host-owned rows are never touched by a device reset.

Questions. Which rows explain the "failed" trace? Which row explains a data leak between tenants?

Expected reasoning. Ownership decides reset scope.

Solution

Cleared by VF FLR: VF1 CC/CSTS, its queues and outstanding commands, its MSI-X configuration. Not cleared: VF Enable (PF), secondary resources and attachment (primary; confirm in Base 2.4), NS 5 data (media), IOMMU domain and remapping entries (host), VF2's queues (another function). The failed trace comes from the host-owned IOMMU domain being changed before the device-owned queues were reset; a tenant data leak comes from NS 5 data surviving everything except an explicit erase.

Extension. Add the rows for a PF controller reset and for NVM Subsystem Reset, and mark which VFs each disturbs.

Intermediate lab: check a runbook for ordering bugs

Objective. Encode lifecycle rules and find the first error in a runbook. Safe lab

Prerequisites. The mechanism table above; Python 3.8+.

Setup. The toy model below encodes the rules taught here. Its minimum-resource rule (2 VQ, 1 VI) is a teaching simplification; real minimums come from the device and Base 2.4.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""vf_lifecycle.py - check an SR-IOV + NVMe secondary-controller runbook for ordering bugs.
Simulation: a toy model of one VF; rules are the ones taught in Chapter 56 and Module 52."""

class VF:
    def __init__(self):
        self.vf_enabled = self.online = self.bound = self.dma_mapped = False
        self.vq = self.vi = 0
        self.ns_attached = False
        self.tenant, self.ns_owner = None, None

    def step(self, op, arg=None):
        err = None
        if op == "enable_vfs":
            self.vf_enabled = True
        elif op == "offline":
            if self.bound: err = "secondary taken offline while a driver/VM owns the VF"
            self.online = False
        elif op in ("assign_vq", "assign_vi"):
            if self.online: err = "resources assigned to an online secondary (must be offline)"
            if op == "assign_vq": self.vq += arg
            else: self.vi += arg
        elif op == "online":
            if self.vq < 2 or self.vi < 1: err = f"online with VQ={self.vq} VI={self.vi} (needs admin + 1 I/O queue, 1 vector)"
            self.online = True
        elif op == "attach_ns":
            self.ns_attached = True
        elif op == "assign_to_vm":
            if not self.vf_enabled: err = "VF does not exist"
            elif not self.online: err = "VM gets a VF whose secondary controller is offline"
            elif self.ns_owner not in (None, arg): err = f"namespace still holds {self.ns_owner}'s data; erase before giving it to {arg}"
            self.bound, self.dma_mapped, self.tenant = True, True, arg
            self.ns_owner = arg
        elif op == "unmap_guest_memory":
            if self.bound: err = "guest memory unmapped while the VF can still DMA (reset the VF first)"
            self.dma_mapped = False
        elif op == "flr":
            self.bound = False                       # VF function and controller state reset
        elif op == "erase_ns":
            self.ns_owner = None
        elif op == "release_vm":
            self.tenant = None
        return err

def run(name, ops):
    vf, bad = VF(), 0
    print(f"--- {name}")
    for n, (op, *arg) in enumerate(ops, 1):
        err = vf.step(op, *arg)
        if err:
            bad += 1
            print(f"  step {n:2} {op:<20} FIRST ERROR: {err}" if bad == 1 else f"  step {n:2} {op:<20} error: {err}")
    print("  result:", "clean" if not bad else f"{bad} error(s)")

Advanced lab: an emulated SR-IOV NVMe device

Objective. Bring up secondary controllers on an emulated device and observe FLR and reassignment from inside a VM. Caution lab: run inside a disposable VM; commands create and reset devices.

Prerequisites. Lab Environment; a recent QEMU whose emulated NVMe device supports SR-IOV and virtualization management. Parameter names (for example a maximum-VFs parameter and flexible VQ/VI pool sizes) differ between QEMU versions: read your version's NVMe device documentation Uncertain.

Setup. Boot a guest with an emulated NVMe device configured for SR-IOV, an emulated IOMMU if available, and a file-backed namespace.

Procedure. Inside the guest: (1) read nvme primary-ctrl-caps and nvme list-secondary; (2) disable VF autoprobe, create two VFs, then Offline/Assign/Online secondary 2 following Chapter 56's Caution sequence; (3) bind VF1 to the nvme driver, write a known pattern to its namespace; (4) trigger FLR with echo 1 > /sys/bus/pci/devices/<VF>/reset; (5) check controller state and read back the pattern.

Observations. The controller re-initialises after reset; the pattern is still on the namespace; list-secondary still shows resources.

Questions. (a) What does the persistence of the pattern prove? (b) What would you add to make VF1 safe to give to another tenant?

Expected reasoning. Separate controller state from media state.

Solution

(a) FLR resets controller state, not stored data (in this emulation; real devices follow the same principle by specification design). (b) An erase step (Format NVM with a secure-erase setting, Sanitize, or namespace delete and re-create) and detach/attach to the new controller, followed by a read-back that returns no prior data. Emulator behaviour is not evidence of a specific SSD's behaviour.

Extension. Attach the emulated device's trace events (if your QEMU build exposes NVMe trace points) and correlate them with the guest's resets.

Senior forensic lab: tenant B reads tenant A's data

Objective. Root-cause a reported cross-tenant data exposure and design the control that prevents it. Safe lab (paper exercise on synthetic evidence).

Prerequisites. All of this module; Namespaces.

Setup: evidence (synthetic).

  • Tenant B, new on host H7, ran a raw read of its NVMe namespace and found readable database pages carrying tenant A's identifiers.
  • Hypervisor log: VF3 moved from VM A to VM B at 02:14; VF FLR at 02:14:03; IOMMU domain switched at 02:14:04.
  • Management log: NS 9 detached from CNTLID 4, then reattached to CNTLID 4 at 02:14:05. No Format NVM, Sanitize or namespace re-create recorded.
  • Analyzer (installed for another investigation): every DMA during VM B's reads carries RID 0x0103 (VF3) and targets domain B's IOVAs.
  • Identify Namespace for NS 9 shows it is a private namespace (not shared); list-ctrl shows only CNTLID 4 attached.

Procedure. Classify the evidence; eliminate hypotheses: (H1) device RID or isolation bug; (H2) namespace shared with VM A's controller; (H3) data remanence on reassignment.

Questions. (a) Which evidence eliminates H1? (b) H2? (c) What control fixes H3, and how is it verified?

Expected reasoning. Exposure needs a path; find the one that exists.

Solution

(a) All DMA carries VF3's RID into domain B: the device and IOMMU isolated correctly. (b) The namespace is private and attached only to CNTLID 4. (c) H3: the namespace was handed to a new tenant without erasure; FLR and detach/attach do not change media. Control: a mandatory erase (cryptographic erase where supported, or Sanitize/Format NVM, or namespace re-create) in the reassignment workflow, gated by verifying completion status, plus an audit log entry. Verification: a reassignment test writes a unique pattern as tenant 1, reassigns, and asserts tenant 2 reads none of it across 1 000 cycles. Severity: a security incident; report it as such.

Extension. Discuss how NVMe 2.4 exported subsystems and migration change this workflow: state that must move with a tenant versus state that must never move to another tenant.

Interview

Beginner

  1. What does FLR of a VF reset?

    Model answer

    That function's state and its NVMe controller state: registers, queues, outstanding commands, MSI-X configuration. Not other functions, not namespace data.

  2. Why does every VF have its own RID?

    Model answer

    So the IOMMU can select the right VM's translation tables and so errors and interrupts are attributed to the right function.

  3. Why can't the hypervisor give a guest direct control of the VF's MSI-X table?

    Model answer

    The table holds physical interrupt addresses and data; the hypervisor intercepts guest writes and programs safe, remapped values.

  4. Where do namespace management commands for a VF's controller go?

    Model answer

    To the primary controller's admin queue on the PF, by the host's management stack.

  5. What does Transactions Pending tell you?

    Model answer

    That the function has non-posted requests awaiting completions; not whether posted writes are in flight.

Intermediate

  1. Give the order for revoking a VF from a VM.

    Model answer

    Quiesce (shutdown or stop), wait for Transactions Pending to clear, FLR, wait, then unmap IOMMU and remove remapping entries; erase and reattach storage before the next tenant.

  2. Why is a blocked posted write more dangerous for debugging than a blocked read?

    Model answer

    A read gets a UR completion the device sees and may report; a posted write is silently dropped and logged only in the host, so the device believes it succeeded.

  3. Guest reports 2 ms latency, the device says 80 µs. Who is right?

    Model answer

    Both, measured at different points; the analyzer shows interrupt-to-CQ-head-doorbell time, which is host scheduling.

  4. Why must a secondary controller be offline to change its resources?

    Model answer

    Its queues and vectors are in use while online; changing them underneath a running driver would break it, so NVMe requires Offline before Assign.

  5. What does a PF driver unbind do to VFs?

    Model answer

    Typically disables SR-IOV, removing every VF, so every guest loses its device: a shared reset scope.

Advanced

  1. How do you prove that a fault naming the PF is not VF traffic?

    Model answer

    Correlate the faulting TLP with the doorbell and SQE that caused it in an analyzer capture; matching a PF admin SQE proves host-side origin.

  2. Why does ATS complicate VF reassignment?

    Model answer

    The device caches translations; the host must invalidate them, or the device could use stale translations after the domain changes.

  3. How would you validate that FLR of VF1 never disturbs VF2?

    Model answer

    Run verified-pattern I/O on VF2 while looping FLR on VF1; pass if VF2 has no errors, no latency outliers beyond a bound, and no resets, over many iterations.

  4. What is the difference between VF FLR and a controller reset of the secondary?

    Model answer

    Controller reset (CC.EN 1 → 0) resets NVMe controller state only; FLR resets the whole PCIe function including configuration-visible state and MSI-X, and implies controller reset.

  5. How does NVMe 2.4 rate limiting change noisy-neighbour debugging?

    Model answer

    It gives a standard, controller-enforced cap to test against; debugging shifts to verifying configured limits and their enforcement rather than vendor-specific QoS.

Senior

  1. Write acceptance criteria for an SR-IOV NVMe SSD in a multi-tenant cloud.

    Model answer

    RID audit: all VF DMA uses the VF's RID; FLR under load isolates; reassignment with erase leaves no data; resources assign/online correctly at limits; rate limits enforced (if 2.4); error containment per VF; recovery times bounded.

  2. Which failures are the device's responsibility and which the platform's?

    Model answer

    Device: correct RIDs, FLR scope, rate enforcement, resource semantics. Platform/host: teardown order, IOMMU and interrupt remapping, erase policy, scheduling. Evidence must name the owner.

  3. How would live migration with Exported NVM Subsystems change your test plan?

    Model answer

    Add identity and configuration-template equivalence, runtime-state capture and apply, in-flight I/O correctness across migration, and source cleanup verification, all reading Base 2.4 for definitions.

  4. A customer wants to skip erasure for faster VM churn. Your response?

    Model answer

    Not without a different isolation guarantee: cryptographic erase is fast where supported, or per-tenant namespaces re-created; document the risk and require security sign-off otherwise.

  5. What evidence do you insist on before escalating "SSD leaks across VMs" to a vendor?

    Model answer

    Analyzer proof of wrong-RID DMA or cross-function data, with attachment lists and erase logs ruling out host policy causes.

Quiz

  1. VF FLR clears:

    • Namespace data
    • The VF's controller registers and queues
    • VF Enable in the PF
    • The IOMMU domain
    Answer and reasoning

    FLR resets function and controller state only.

  2. Correct teardown order:

    • Unmap, FLR, quiesce
    • FLR, quiesce, unmap
    • Quiesce, FLR, unmap
    • Unmap only
    Answer and reasoning

    Stop new work, reset the function so it stops DMA, then remove translations.

  3. A blocked device MWr results in:

    • A dropped write and a host-side fault log
    • A UR completion to the device
    • A Nak DLLP
    • A CQE error status
    Answer and reasoning

    Posted writes have no completion; the IOMMU drops and logs.

  4. Which address header size is used for a write to VF BAR0 at 0x3_8000_0014?

    • 3 DW (MWr32)
    • 4 DW (MWr64)
    • Either; the TLP decides at random
    • None; configuration request
    Answer and reasoning

    The address is above 4 GiB, so a 64-bit (4-DW) header is required.

  5. An interrupt is on time but the CQ head doorbell is 2 ms late. Most likely:

    • NAND read retry
    • Link replay
    • Wrong MSI-X data
    • Host vCPU scheduling
    Answer and reasoning

    The device's work ended at the interrupt.

  6. Resources can be assigned to a secondary controller only when it is:

    • Offline
    • Online
    • Bound to a VM
    • Reset by FLR
    Answer and reasoning

    NVMe requires Offline before Assign.

  7. Same IOVA number valid in VM B, faulting for the PF. Why possible?

    • IOVA is global
    • The PF shares VM B's domain
    • IOVA spaces are per domain and overlap numerically
    • The IOMMU is disabled
    Answer and reasoning

    Each RID's domain has its own address space.

  8. Open: why is "FLR resets everything" a dangerous belief in a multi-tenant cloud?

    Model answer

    It leads to skipping erase on reassignment, exposing the previous tenant's data.

  9. Open: name two shared-reset events that disturb every VF.

    Model answer

    PF driver unbind or PF reset, link Recovery or hot reset, NVM Subsystem Reset, DPC containment at the root port.

Teach-back

  1. To a child: explain why the hotel waits for the last tray before locking the room, and why it empties the cupboard.

    Model explanation

    "If we lock the door while a waiter is still walking, the food falls on the floor. And the next guest shouldn't find the last guest's leftovers, so we clean the cupboard every time."

  2. To a junior engineer: walk through VF reassignment, naming the plane (configuration, management, guest, IOMMU) of each step.

    Model explanation

    "Guest plane: shut down the controller. Configuration plane: check Transactions Pending, FLR the VF. IOMMU: unmap the old domain. Management plane via the PF: erase, detach and attach the namespace, adjust VQ/VI. IOMMU: map the new domain. Guest plane: the new VM enables the controller."

  3. To a senior engineer: argue how you would assign ownership of a DMA fault naming the PF during guest I/O.

    Model explanation

    "The RID tells us which domain translated it, not who asked. I tie the faulting TLP to its doorbell and SQE in a capture: a PF admin SQE means host software; a VF doorbell and VF SQE with PF RID means a device isolation bug. Until then it is an open security question."
