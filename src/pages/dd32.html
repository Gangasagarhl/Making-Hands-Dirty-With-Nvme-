Flush is four packets on the wire and a promise that every layer above and below can quietly break. This module goes past the definition into how a controller implements the promise, how the promise behaves with many queues, many namespaces, resets and virtual machines, how to measure what a Flush really costs, and how to prove with power cuts that a device keeps it.

Orientation

Chapter 34 (FLUSH) taught the Non-Volatile Memory Express (NVMe) Flush command: opcode 00h, no data, scope "writes completed before the Flush was submitted", the Volatile Write Cache (VWC) field and feature, the latency model, and three traces (a normal drain, a slow Flush hurting reads, a rejected broadcast Flush). This module deepens four things: the flush epoch (how a controller decides what a Flush covers when commands arrive on many queues), Flush across resets, timeouts and shutdown, Flush through intermediate layers (block layer, hypervisors, RAID), and validation by power-cut testing with a statistical stopping rule. Every trace here is new; the basics are linked, not repeated.

Read first: FLUSH (scope, VWC bits, CC.SHN/CSTS.SHST, cost model), WRITE — what completion means for durability, End-to-End Write — durability, and Deep dive: WRITE. For the reset path, NVMe Reset.

Where it goes next: Deep dive: Dataset Management (deallocation has the same durability questions), Deep dive: Reset and Deep dive: Validation Engineering.

Kid story

A class is making a giant paper chain. Children add links on four tables at once, and a helper carries finished pieces to the glue station, where they are stuck to the classroom wall for good. Pieces wait on the tables until the helper comes by.

Before the bell, the teacher calls: "Helper, glue to the wall every piece that was finished when I called you. Tell me when it's done." The helper writes down which pieces were finished at that moment, on every table, then glues them. Children keep making links meanwhile, and some new ones might get glued too, but the helper's "done!" only promises the pieces on the list.

One day a new helper says "done!" instantly, every time. Is she very fast, or is she not gluing at all? You cannot tell by watching her answer. You can only tell by switching off the lights at a random moment and checking the wall.

Analogy boundary

What it represents. Tables are I/O Submission Queues; finished pieces are completed writes; the helper's list is the flush epoch; gluing is committing data and mapping metadata to non-volatile media; "done!" is the Flush Completion Queue Entry (CQE); switching off the lights is a power-cut test.

What it hides. The helper must also glue the labels that say where each piece hangs: a controller must persist the flash translation layer (FTL) mapping, or flushed data cannot be found after power loss. The story also hides that there may be several helpers (controllers sharing a namespace) and several adults relaying the teacher's call (filesystem, hypervisor, RAID adapter), each of whom can forget to pass it on.

Where it becomes misleading. In the story pieces finished while the teacher was calling are uncertain; in NVMe the cut-off is precise — completion seen before submission of the Flush — but the host only knows completion times as it observed them, so the host must use its own order of events. And "instant done" is not always a lie: a drive with power-loss protection that reports a volatile cache can legitimately answer quickly.

Mechanism

A database calls fdatasync() after two writes on CPU 2 (I/O queue pair 2), while another process writes on queue pair 1.

  1. Writes complete. Who: controller. What: CQEs for CIDs 0x0101 and 0x0102 posted to CQ2. Where: host memory at CQ2 base 0x1_2343_0000. Evidence: CQE Memory Writes (MWr) and nvme_complete_rq.
  2. Host decides a flush is needed. Who: filesystem and block layer. What: a flush request is created only if the queue's write_cache is "write back"; concurrent flush requests may be merged. Where: kernel memory. Evidence: block tracepoints with the flush flag.
  3. Driver submits Flush. Who: NVMe driver on CPU 2. What: SQE with opcode 00h, NSID 1, CID 0x0300 in SQ2; tail doorbell. Where: SQ2 ring 0x1_2342_0000, doorbell BAR0 + 0x1010. Evidence: nvme_setup_cmd with nvme_cmd_flush; MWr32 to 0xFE60_1010.
  4. Controller fetches and snapshots. Who: command processor. What: records the epoch — every write it had completed (for that namespace, from any queue) when it fetched the Flush; the epoch is at least the host-visible set because the host saw those completions before submitting. Where: controller SRAM. Evidence: SQE fetch only; the snapshot is invisible.
  5. Drain. Who: cache manager and NAND back end. What: dirty buffers in the epoch are programmed, possibly padding partial program units. Where: NAND. Evidence: none on PCIe; concurrent reads may slow.
  6. Persist mapping. Who: FTL. What: map deltas or journal entries for the drained data are made non-volatile. Where: NAND metadata area. Evidence: none; dominates latency on some designs.
  7. Complete. Who: completion engine. What: CQE with status 0 for CID 0x0300, then Message Signaled Interrupts – Extended (MSI-X). Where: CQ2. Evidence: CQE MWr, MSI-X MWr32.
  8. Dependent work. Who: filesystem or database. What: only now submits the commit record (often with FUA). Where: SQ. Evidence: the commit write's SQE appears after the Flush CQE.

The central concept on the ladder: the flush epoch

The helper writes down every finished piece on every table at the moment the teacher calls, glues those, then says "done".

A Flush covers all writes to its namespace that completed before it was submitted, regardless of which queue they used. The controller implements this with an internal snapshot of its completions.

A completion sequence counter per namespace (or per controller), dirty-buffer lists tagged with sequence numbers, and an FTL journal pointer. The Flush waits until every buffer tagged at or below the snapshot is on NAND and the journal entries covering them are persisted.

NVM Express Base Specification, Revision 2.4 — Flush command defines the scope by completion; Identify Controller VWC reports presence and broadcast support; the Volatile Write Cache feature (06h) enables the cache. The epoch is an implementation device, not a specification term Implementation-dependent.

Doorbell MWr, SQE Memory Read (MRd) and Completion with Data (CplD), silence for this command, CQE MWr, MSI-X. No data packets ever.

Mark every write CQE before the Flush doorbell: those are covered. Writes whose CQE comes after the Flush doorbell are outside the guarantee, even if they complete before the Flush CQE.

Data lost "despite fsync": prove the lost write's CQE precedes the Flush submission, the Flush CQE was success, and every layer forwarded it; if all hold, power-cut test the device.

The sixteen questions for Flush are answered in Chapter 34; this module extends answers 10–16 for multi-queue, reset and layered stacks.

The specification does not define: any upper bound on Flush latency; any event telling the host that cached data has reached media without a Flush; an ordering barrier; whether a Flush submitted through one controller covers writes completed through another controller of the same subsystem (confirm the wording; do not assume — Uncertain); or what a power failure during a Flush leaves behind beyond the atomicity rules. Check in the specification: the exact Flush scope sentence for your revision; the status returned for an unsupported broadcast NSID; the Abort completion's indication of whether the command was aborted.

Never assume: that a fast Flush is a fake Flush; that a slow Flush is a broken drive; that an aborted or timed-out Flush made anything durable; that the guest operating system's Flush reached the physical drive; or that VWC = 0 on one firmware revision stays 0 after an update.

Making one 4 KiB write durable may require programming a whole multi-plane, multi-page unit (padding the rest) and committing a mapping-journal page. A DRAM-less design whose map lives partly in the Host Memory Buffer must also make that map change durable on NAND. The cost of a Flush therefore has a large fixed part whenever the dirty set is non-empty, and a near-zero cost only when it is empty Derived.

Completion semantics of a failed or lost Flush

A Flush that returns an error, is aborted, or vanishes in a reset gives the host no durability information about its epoch. Some of the data may already be on media (the controller drains continuously), some may not. The only safe host action is to treat the epoch as not durable and re-establish durability: after recovery, issue a new Flush (which covers everything completed before it, including the old epoch, if the data is still in the device) and only then report success upward. If a reset happened with power still applied, the data is usually still in the device; if power was lost, recovery has to rely on the atomicity and journal mechanisms of the filesystem.

"Completed before the Flush was submitted" is evaluated from the host's point of view. If the host has seen a CQE before ringing the Flush doorbell, the write is covered. A write whose CQE was already posted in memory but not yet read by the host is also covered (it completed first), but the host cannot know it is covered, so correct software only counts completions it has consumed Derived.

Disambiguating experiment: plot Flush latency against dirty bytes for 0, 1, 4, 64 and 1024 blocks; a large intercept with a shallow slope confirms the fixed-cost model.

Analysis. The device accepted the Flush (rows 2–3), so this is not a lost doorbell. Evidence to collect: the kernel log's timeout and reset lines naming the queue and command, the SMART log (critical warning, temperature, media errors), the Error Information log after recovery, and a vendor telemetry log if supported. The root cause is inside the device (firmware hang in the drain path) unless the capture shows the controller waiting on the host (for example, DRAM-less drive's Host Memory Buffer reads failing). Labs

Beginner lab — covered or not covered?

Safe lab Paper exercise.

Objective
Apply the scope rule precisely to a timeline.
Prerequisites
Chapter 34, Beginner explanation.
Setup
Host events on namespace 1 (times in µs): W1 submitted 0, completed 9; W2 submitted 2, completed 30; Flush F submitted 20, completed 700; W3 submitted 25, completed 40; W4 (namespace 2) submitted 5, completed 12; W5 submitted 710, completed 720.
Procedure
Classify W1–W5 relative to F as covered, not covered, or out of scope.
Observations
Note which writes completed between F's submission and F's completion.
Questions
(1) Which writes may the host treat as durable at t = 700? (2) Would NSID FFFFFFFFh change the answer for W4? (3) What must the host do to make W2 durable?
Expected reasoning
Only completion-before-submission counts; namespace scope; later Flush for the rest.
Solution

W1 covered (completed 9 < 20). W2 not covered (completed 30 > 20), although it completed before F did. W3 not covered (submitted after F). W4 out of scope (namespace 2) for a namespace-1 Flush; with NSID FFFFFFFFh on a controller whose VWC bits 2:1 = 11b it would be covered (completed 12 < 20). W5 is after F entirely. (1) Only W1. (3) Issue another Flush after consuming W2's completion; it will also cover W3 and W5 if their completions were seen first.

Extension. Redo the exercise assuming the host polls completions only every 15 µs: which classifications change, and why does correct software count only consumed completions?

submissions (S) and completions (C) across queues, in time order, for one namespace.
A Flush covers exactly the writes whose completion was seen BEFORE the Flush was submitted.
The checker also flags dependent writes submitted before the Flush they depend on completed.
"""
LOG = [  # (time_us, event, queue, cid, kind, note)
    (0,   "S", 2, 0x0101, "write", ""),
    (3,   "S", 3, 0x0102, "write", ""),
    (11,  "C", 2, 0x0101, "write", ""),
    (12,  "S", 1, 0x0300, "flush", ""),
    (14,  "C", 3, 0x0102, "write", ""),
    (15,  "S", 4, 0x0103, "write", "commit"),     # depends on flush 0x0300 - submitted too early!
    (19,  "C", 4, 0x0103, "write", "commit"),
    (640, "C", 1, 0x0300, "flush", ""),
    (641, "S", 1, 0x0301, "flush", ""),
    (900, "C", 1, 0x0301, "flush", ""),
]

def analyse(log):
    completed, inflight_flush, results = set(), {}, []
    for t, ev, q, cid, kind, note in log:
        key = (q, cid)
        if ev == "S" and kind == "flush":
            inflight_flush[key] = (t, set(completed))
        elif ev == "S" and note == "commit" and inflight_flush:
            results.append(f"t={t:>4} us: commit write q{q}/0x{cid:04X} submitted while "
                           f"{len(inflight_flush)} flush(es) still outstanding -> ordering bug")
        elif ev == "C" and kind == "flush":
            t0, covered = inflight_flush.pop(key)
            names = ", ".join(f"q{a}/0x{b:04X}" for a, b in sorted(covered)) or "nothing"
            results.append(f"t={t:>4} us: flush q{q}/0x{cid:04X} (submitted t={t0}) guarantees: {names}")
        elif ev == "C":
            completed.add(key)
    return results

if __name__ == "__main__":
    for line in analyse(LOG):
        print(line)
Procedure
(1) Run it and read the three lines it prints. (2) Fix the log so the commit write is correct (move it after t = 640) and rerun. (3) Encode Trace M32-N as a log and confirm the checker says 0x0480 is not covered by 0x0300.

Why is write 0x0102 not guaranteed by the first Flush?
Questions
(a) The commit write in the original log is on a different queue from the Flush. Does that matter? (b) Why does the second Flush guarantee everything?
Expected reasoning
Queues are irrelevant to scope and to ordering; only completion and submission times matter.
Solution

Output: an ordering bug at t = 15 (commit submitted while Flush 0x0300 is outstanding); Flush 0x0300 guarantees only q2/0x0101; Flush 0x0301 guarantees all three writes. 0x0102 completed at 14, after the Flush was submitted at 12. (a) No; the commit could become durable before 0x0101/0x0102 regardless of queue. (b) Its submission (641) follows all three completions. After step (2), the ordering-bug line disappears. For step (3) the output lists 0x0101 and 0x0102 for 0x0300, and 0x0480 appears in no guarantee.

Advanced lab — how many power cuts prove a Flush is honest?

Safe lab (simulation). A real power-cut rig is an Expert lab: dedicated hardware, a controllable supply, a drive whose data does not matter.

Objective
Understand the statistics of power-cut validation and why one passing cut proves little.
Prerequisites
Chapter 34, Debugging.
Setup
Save as powercut_sim.py.
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""powercut_sim.py - how many power cuts does it take to catch a device that fakes Flush?

Synthetic teaching model (standard library only). Each trial: the host writes a batch of
signed blocks, waits for completions, issues Flush, records the blocks it believes durable,
keeps writing un-flushed blocks, then power is cut at a random moment. An honest device keeps
every flushed block. A dishonest device acknowledges Flush early; whatever was still only in
its cache when power failed is lost (old data returns).
"""
import math
import random

def trial(rng, honest, destage_per_ms=4, cut_window_ms=3.0):
    flushed = 64                          # blocks the host believes durable
    in_cache = 0 if honest else flushed   # dishonest: Flush returned without draining
    cut = rng.uniform(0.0, cut_window_ms) # power fails this long after the Flush CQE
    still_volatile = max(0, in_cache - int(cut * destage_per_ms * 8))
    return still_volatile                 # flushed blocks lost (0 means pass)

def run(n, honest, seed=7):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    return sum(1 for _ in range(n) if trial(rng, honest) > 0)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    for honest in (True, False):
        fails = run(200, honest)
        print(f"{'honest   ' if honest else 'dishonest'}: {fails} of 200 cuts lost flushed data")
    q = run(2000, False) / 2000           # per-cut detection probability for this model
    n99 = math.ceil(math.log(0.01) / math.log(1 - q))
    print(f"per-cut detection probability q = {q:.2f}; cuts for 99% confidence = {n99}")
Procedure
(1) Run it. (2) Make the dishonest device destage faster (destage_per_ms=16) and widen the cut window to 20 ms; rerun. (3) Explain why q falls and the required number of cuts rises.
Observations
Record q and the cuts needed for 99% confidence in each configuration.
Questions
(a) Derive n = ⌈ln(1 − C) / ln(1 − q)⌉. (b) Where should a real rig place its cuts relative to the Flush CQE to maximise q? (c) Why must the verifier record which completions it observed before the cut?
Expected reasoning
Detection requires the cut to land while falsely acknowledged data is still volatile.
n = ⌈ ln(1 − C) ÷ ln(1 − q) ⌉
n
number of independent power cuts to run
C
desired confidence of catching a dishonest device (for example 0.99)
q
probability that one cut exposes the dishonesty (depends on cut timing and how fast the device destages)
Solution

With the defaults the script reports 0 of 200 failures for the honest model, 140 of 200 for the dishonest one, q ≈ 0.67 and 5 cuts for 99%. With faster destage and a 20 ms window, the dishonest device's cache empties within 0.5 ms, so only cuts in that first 0.5 ms of the 20 ms window detect it: the script estimates q ≈ 0.03 and about 144 cuts (the exact q of 0.025 gives 182; the difference is sampling noise in the estimate, itself a lesson about estimating q from few trials). (a) Probability of missing in all n cuts is (1 − q)ⁿ; set it ≤ 1 − C. (b) As close after the Flush CQE as the rig can manage, and with a large dirty set at Flush time. (c) Only data whose completion (and covering Flush completion) the host saw may be counted as "should have survived"; anything else is legally lost.

Extension. Add a "torn" outcome for in-flight writes larger than AWUPF and make the verifier distinguish legal tears from illegal loss of flushed data.

Senior forensic lab — the database that lost its last second

Safe lab Evidence analysis.

Objective
Locate the layer that broke the Flush promise in a virtualised stack.
Prerequisites
This module; Chapter 34, Senior engineer insight.
Setup (evidence pack, synthetic)
  • A database in a virtual machine (VM) lost the last ≈1 s of committed transactions after the physical host lost power. Its log shows those transactions as committed (fsync returned).
  • Guest-side tracepoints: every commit issued a flush to the guest's virtual disk, and each flush completed in 20–40 µs.
  • Physical-host NVMe tracepoints captured during a re-run show Write commands from the hypervisor, but no Flush commands for minutes at a time. Physical drive: VWC bit 0 = 1, cache enabled, no power-loss protection.
  • The VM's virtual disk is configured with a cache mode that does not forward guest flush requests (QEMU's cache=unsafe is an example of such a mode).
  • The physical drive's SMART log shows the unsafe-shutdown counter incremented by one.
Procedure
Find the first layer where a Flush disappears, decide the owner, and propose a fix and a regression test.
Observations
Compare guest flush latency with physical Flush latency for this drive.
Questions
(1) Is the SSD at fault? (2) What single comparison exposes the problem fastest? (3) What test would have caught it?
Expected reasoning
Follow the flush through each boundary; a flush that vanishes between two tracepoint layers was dropped by the layer between them.
Solution

The first abnormal event is at the hypervisor boundary: guest flushes are acknowledged in tens of microseconds while no physical Flush is issued. The SSD behaved correctly: it reported a volatile cache, was never asked to flush, and lost unflushed data on power loss, which the specification permits. Owner: the virtualisation configuration. Fix: a cache mode that forwards flushes (or a drive with power-loss protection that reports no volatile cache, and even then the hypervisor's own page cache must not be in the path). Fastest comparison: guest flush latency (20–40 µs) versus the physical drive's Flush latency for a non-empty dirty set (hundreds of µs or more) — a layer that answers faster than the device can is not forwarding. Regression test: a flush-forwarding check that issues guest flushes and asserts that physical Flush commands appear in the host trace, plus periodic power-cut testing of the whole stack. Confidence: high.

Extension. List every layer in your own production stack that can acknowledge a flush, and the evidence you would use for each.

Debugging

Six Flush failure patterns beyond those in Chapter 34.

fsync latency is high and erratic for one tenant

Symptom
A small service's fsync takes 0.3 ms usually and 40 ms at times; its own writes are tiny.
Possible causes
Another tenant's large dirty set on the same namespace (Flush covers everything completed before it); garbage collection; thermal throttling.
Evidence
Bytes written by all processes to the namespace between Flushes; Flush CQE latency; SMART temperature and throttle counters.
First check
Correlate slow Flushes with other tenants' write bursts.
Second check
Is the namespace shared? Separate namespaces do not share Flush scope (the controller may still drain shared NAND).
Disambiguating experiment
Pause the bulk writer; if spikes vanish, scope sharing is the cause.
Likely conclusion
Shared dirty set; mitigate with separate namespaces, bulk writer flushing more often, or a protected drive.
Confidence
High with correlation and the pause experiment.

Flush latency constant and tiny on a drive that reports a cache

Symptom
Trace M32-A behaviour at all dirty sizes.
Possible causes
Power-loss-protected cache; aggressive continuous destaging; dishonest Flush.
Evidence
Product documentation, Flush latency versus dirty size, power-cut results.
First check
Vendor statement on power-loss protection for this model and firmware.
Second check
Latency slope: does a Flush after 1 GiB of writes take as long as after 4 KiB?
Disambiguating experiment
Power-cut test with cuts immediately after Flush CQE (advanced lab sizing).
Likely conclusion
Undetermined without the test.
Confidence
Low before testing; high after enough cuts.

Flushed data lost after power failure

Symptom
Data whose fsync returned is old after power loss.
Possible causes
A layer dropped the flush (hypervisor, RAID, write_cache override); host submitted Flush before the write completed; device dishonest; FTL metadata not persisted with data.
Evidence
Tracepoints at every layer; order of write CQE and Flush submission; Flush CQE status.
First check
Is there a physical Flush, submitted after the lost write's completion, with success?
Second check
Is the lost data stale (old version) or garbage? Stale points to unflushed cache or lost mapping; garbage points to atomicity or media.
Disambiguating experiment
Reproduce with tracing at the physical device and a power-cut rig.
Likely conclusion
Most often a forwarding layer (senior lab); device fault only when the physical sequence is correct.
Confidence
High once the physical trace exists.

Flush times out and the controller resets

Symptom
Trace M32-F: Flush outstanding for the driver timeout; Abort refused; reset.
Possible causes
Firmware hang in drain or metadata commit; media failure during drain; host not answering the device's Host Memory Buffer reads; thermal shutdown.
Evidence
Kernel timeout/reset lines; SMART critical warning and temperature; Error Information log; capture of the device's upstream requests during the stall.
First check
Did other commands complete during the stall? Front-end alive vs whole controller dead.
Second check
Any outstanding device MRd without completion (host side stuck)?
Disambiguating experiment
Repeat with a smaller dirty set and with Host Memory Buffer disabled (if the drive uses it and policy allows) to see whether the hang tracks the drain or the host.
Likely conclusion
Device firmware, usually; vendor telemetry required.
Confidence
Medium.

Unsafe-shutdown counter grows on every reboot

Symptom
SMART unsafe shutdowns increments on orderly reboots.
Possible causes
Platform removes power before CSTS.SHST = 10b; driver shutdown timeout shorter than device needs; firmware reports shutdown complete late.
Evidence
RTD3E, shutdown_timeout, kernel shutdown messages (if captured on a serial console), power-sequencing timing.
First check
Compare RTD3E with the effective host shutdown wait.
Second check
Does it also happen with the drive idle (empty dirty set) before reboot?
Disambiguating experiment
Issue nvme flush and wait a few seconds before reboot; if the counter stops growing, shutdown time was too short for the dirty set.
Likely conclusion
Platform/driver timing; harmless only if the drive has power-loss protection.
Confidence
Medium to high.

Broadcast Flush starts failing after a firmware update

Symptom
A custom tool's Flush with NSID FFFFFFFFh returns an error status after an update.
Possible causes
New firmware reports VWC bits 2:1 = 10b (not supported) where the old reported 00b and accepted it anyway.
Evidence
Identify Controller VWC before and after; CQE status.
First check
Read VWC bits 2:1.
Second check
Does the tool check those bits at all?
Disambiguating experiment
Flush each namespace by its NSID; success confirms.
Likely conclusion
Tool relied on undefined behaviour; fix the tool and treat earlier "successful" broadcast Flushes on 00b firmware as unverified.
Confidence
High.

Interview

Beginner

  1. What does a successful Flush promise?

    Model answer

    That data and metadata of writes to its namespace completed before the Flush was submitted are on non-volatile media.

  2. Does a Flush carry data?

    Model answer

    No: opcode 00h, no data pointer, no LBA range.

  3. Why might a Flush complete almost instantly?

    Model answer

    No volatile cache, an empty dirty set, or a protected cache; or, rarely, a dishonest device.

  4. Is Flush a barrier for later writes?

    Model answer

    No; later commands can run and complete before it. Dependent writes must wait for its completion.

  5. Where do you find whether the drive has a volatile write cache?

    Model answer

    Identify Controller VWC bit 0; current state via Get Features 06h.

Intermediate

  1. A write on queue 3 completes before a Flush is submitted on queue 1. Covered?

    Model answer

    Yes, if same namespace: scope is by completion and namespace, not queue.

  2. When may a host use NSID FFFFFFFFh with Flush?

    Model answer

    When VWC bits 2:1 = 11b; 10b means not supported, 00b means support not indicated.

  3. How does Linux turn a journal commit into NVMe commands on a cached drive?

    Model answer

    REQ_PREFLUSH becomes a Flush before the commit block; REQ_FUA sets FUA on the commit write (or is emulated with a post-flush if FUA is unsupported). Without a volatile cache both are dropped.

  4. Why does a Flush with one dirty block sometimes take as long as one with a megabyte?

    Model answer

    Fixed costs: padding a program unit and committing mapping metadata dominate small dirty sets.

  5. What does an incremented unsafe-shutdown counter tell you?

    Model answer

    Power was removed without completed shutdown notification; it does not prove data loss.

Advanced

  1. A Flush is outstanding when the controller is reset. What does the host know?

    Model answer

    Nothing about the epoch; it must treat the data as not durable and issue a new Flush after recovery before acknowledging upward.

  2. How do you detect a hypervisor that swallows flushes, without power cuts?

    Model answer

    Trace the physical device during guest flushes; or compare guest flush latency with the physical device's Flush latency under a non-empty dirty set.

  3. Explain the epoch implementation and one bug it can have.

    Model answer

    A sequence counter snapshots completions at Flush fetch; dirty buffers at or below it must drain. Bug: assigning sequence numbers at submission instead of completion, so a write completed before the Flush but submitted late is left out.

  4. Why must FTL mapping persistence be part of Flush?

    Model answer

    Data on NAND without a persisted mapping is unreachable after power loss; the LBA would return its old content.

  5. How many power cuts to catch a device with per-cut detection probability 0.1 at 99% confidence?

    Model answer

    ln(0.01)/ln(0.9) ≈ 43.7 → 44 cuts.

Senior

  1. Design a durability qualification for a new SSD firmware.

    Model answer

    Power-cut rig with signature verifier recording observed completions; cuts timed at random and right after Flush and FUA CQEs; large and small dirty sets; VWC on/off; shutdown-timeout boundary tests; Flush during heavy I/O and during reset; pass = zero loss of covered data, tears only above atomic units; sample size from the confidence formula; repeat per firmware release.

  2. A database team wants to disable the drive cache "for safety". Advise.

    Model answer

    It gives durability at the cost of small-write latency and endurance; correct use of fsync/FUA with group commit is usually better, or buy power-loss-protected drives. Never disable kernel flushing via write_cache instead.

  3. How do multi-controller subsystems complicate Flush?

    Model answer

    The scope is framed around the controller processing the Flush; whether writes completed via another controller are covered must be checked in the specification and product. Hosts with multipath should flush through each path that wrote, or confirm subsystem-wide behaviour.

  4. What would make you trust a VWC = 0 report?

    Model answer

    Documented power-loss protection, passing power-cut tests on this firmware, capacitor health monitoring, and regression testing in every release.

  5. Where in a storage review do you look first for Flush bugs?

    Model answer

    At every layer boundary: where flush requests enter, leave, and what the layer acknowledges before the layer below has; then at custom drivers' completion bookkeeping across queues.

Quiz

  1. Write A completes at t = 10; Flush submitted at t = 12; Write B completes at t = 13; Flush completes at t = 500. Which writes are guaranteed durable at t = 500?

    • A only
    • A and B
    • B only
    • Neither
    Answer and reasoning

    Only completions before submission count.

  2. VWC = 05h. Which is correct?

    • No cache; broadcast Flush supported
    • Cache present; broadcast support not indicated
    • Cache present; broadcast Flush not supported
    • Cache present; broadcast Flush supported
    Answer and reasoning

    05h = 101b: bit 0 = 1 (cache), bits 2:1 = 10b (not supported).

  3. A Flush is aborted successfully. What can the host assume?

    • Its epoch is durable
    • Nothing was written
    • Only half the epoch is durable
    • Nothing about durability of its epoch
    Answer and reasoning

    The controller may have drained part or all of it; the host has no guarantee.

  4. Flush latency: 35 µs (empty), 2.1 ms (4 KiB dirty), 2.6 ms (1 MiB dirty). The dominant cost for small sets is:

    • PCIe transfer
    • A fixed per-Flush media/metadata cost
    • Host interrupt latency
    • The data drain rate
    Answer and reasoning

    The intercept dwarfs the slope.

  5. Which Linux setting stops the kernel from sending flushes without changing the device?

    • Writing "write through" to queue/write_cache
    • Set Features 06h WCE = 0
    • nvme flush
    • Disabling FUA in the filesystem
    Answer and reasoning

    It changes the kernel's belief, not the drive — dangerous on a cached drive.

  6. Per-cut detection probability 0.25, confidence 95%. Cuts needed?

    • 4
    • 8
    • 11
    • 20
    Answer and reasoning

    ln(0.05)/ln(0.75) ≈ 10.4 → 11.

  7. Why is a guest flush latency of 20 µs suspicious on a drive without power-loss protection?

    Model answer

    A real Flush with dirty data takes hundreds of microseconds or more on such a drive; answering faster means some layer did not forward it, or nothing was dirty.

  8. What must the host do after recovering from a reset that lost an outstanding Flush?

    Model answer

    Issue a new Flush (covering everything completed before it) before reporting durability; if power was lost, rely on filesystem/database recovery.

  9. Why do correct hosts count only completions they have consumed when deciding Flush coverage?

    Model answer

    A CQE written but not yet read is covered in fact, but the host cannot prove it; acting only on observed completions keeps its reasoning sound.

Teach-back

  1. To a child: why the helper's "done!" covers only the pieces finished when the teacher called.

    Model explanation

    The helper writes a list at the moment you call and promises only that list. Pieces finished after you called might get glued too, but if you need them safe, call again.

  2. To a junior engineer: how to use Flush correctly in a commit protocol.

    Model explanation

    Write the data, wait for all its completions, submit Flush, wait for the Flush completion, then write the commit record (with FUA if the record itself must be durable at once). Never overlap these steps; queues do not matter, completions do. Check VWC once at start-up to know whether Flush does anything.

  3. To a senior engineer: why a protocol trace cannot qualify Flush and what can.

    Model explanation

    Flush's footprint is four packets that look identical whether the device drained 64 MiB or nothing; correctness lives inside the device. Latency versus dirty size gives circumstantial evidence; only power-cut testing with a verifier that records observed completions, cuts placed right after Flush CQEs, and a sample size from n = ln(1−C)/ln(1−q), qualifies it — per firmware release, and per layer stack, because every layer between application and drive can drop or fake the flush.
