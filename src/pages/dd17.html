Chapters 16 and 53 taught the six credit types, the gating rule and the credit-loop arithmetic. This module follows credits where they cause real trouble: across switches, through ordering rules that let one starved credit type freeze unrelated traffic, into silicon bugs that leak credits, and into captures that cannot tell you, on their own, why a transmitter went quiet.

Orientation

PCI Express (PCIe) flow control guarantees that a transmitter never sends a Transaction Layer Packet (TLP) its neighbour cannot store. This module deepens that guarantee into an engineering skill: computing credit availability from a capture, following backpressure hop by hop, recognising when ordering turns a credit shortage into a hang, and separating credit stalls from replay stalls and from a device that simply has nothing to send.

You need Chapter 16 — PCIe Flow Control (credit types and units, InitFC/UpdateFC, infinite credits, the gating rule) and Chapter 53 — Flow Control and Credits (credit loop, bandwidth–delay product, Scaled Flow Control, the four starvation patterns). The passing rules come from Chapter 14 — PCIe Ordering; the replay loop that shares the link from Module 16 — DLLP and Replay.

Next: the errors flow control can raise are read in Module 18 — AER; ordering in depth is Module 14 — PCIe Ordering; credit limits as a performance tool are in Module 53 — Performance Engineering.

Kid story

Sam runs toy trains to his sister Ava's station on the other side of the bedroom. Ava's station has sidings in three colours: red for delivery wagons, blue for question wagons and green for answer wagons. Some sidings hold the wagon's front car, some hold its cargo cars.

Ava keeps a chalkboard Sam can see: "Since breakfast I have emptied 57 red front-car spaces and 900 red cargo spaces." Sam keeps his own tally of everything he has sent. Before sending a red train he subtracts: if sending it would make his tally pass Ava's board, he waits. When Ava unloads trains, she rubs out the old number and writes a bigger one.

One day Ava stops unloading red trains because her toy crane is stuck. Sam's red trains wait. And because Ava's rule says a question train may never overtake a delivery train on the same track, Sam's blue question train waits too, even though the blue sidings are empty.

Analogy boundary

What it represents. Colours are credit classes: posted (P), non-posted (NP) and completion (Cpl). Front-car spaces are header credits; cargo spaces are 16-byte data credits. Ava's chalkboard is the running total carried by UpdateFC Data Link Layer Packets (DLLPs); Sam's tally is CREDITS_CONSUMED; the subtraction is the gating rule; the stuck crane is a consumer stall; the blocked question train is the ordering rule that stops a non-posted request passing a posted one.

What it hides. The board numbers wrap around (8-bit and 12-bit counters), so the subtraction is modular. A smudged board is not repaired on request; the next update simply supersedes it. Sam may also have been told "green is unlimited" — infinite credits. And a real system has a chain of bedrooms: each link has its own board.

Where it misleads. It suggests Sam can see Ava's buffer. He cannot; he sees only her last message, which is always out of date by the credit loop time. It also suggests the blue train waits out of politeness; in PCIe the ordering rule exists to make producer–consumer patterns correct, and the price is that a posted stall can freeze reads.

Architecture

Every receiving port partitions its ingress buffer by class and by header versus data, per VC. Every transmitting port keeps a pair of counters per partition of its neighbour. A switch has a full set on every port, so a path through one switch has two independent credit systems per direction.

Mechanism

Follow one 256-byte posted Memory Write (MWr) of read data from the Lab SSD across a direct link, then see what changes with a switch.

  1. Classify and size. Who: SSD Transaction Layer. What changes: the MWr needs 1 Posted Header (PH) and ⌈256 ÷ 16⌉ = 16 Posted Data (PD) credits. Where: SSD transmit queue for class P. Evidence: none outside the chip.
  2. Gate. Who: SSD gating logic. What changes: it tests both PH and PD with the modular rule; if either fails, the TLP waits in the P queue. Where: SSD counters. Evidence: an analyzer sees either the TLP or an idle link.
  3. Consume. Who: SSD. What changes: CREDITS_CONSUMED(PH) += 1, (PD) += 16; the TLP passes to the Data Link Layer. Evidence: the MWr on the wire.
  4. Buffer. Who: Root Port receiver. What changes: the TLP occupies one header slot and 256 bytes of posted data space; CREDITS_RECEIVED advances if implemented. Where: Root Port ingress SRAM. Evidence: none until it drains.
  5. Drain. Who: the Root Complex's memory path (including the input–output memory management unit (IOMMU) translation). What changes: the write moves into the coherent fabric; buffer space frees. Evidence: none on the link; this step's duration dominates the credit loop.
  6. Return. Who: Root Port Data Link Layer. What changes: CREDITS_ALLOCATED advances; an UpdateFC-P carrying the new totals is scheduled, sooner if the SSD had been blocked. Evidence: UpdateFC-P DLLP whose HdrFC and DataFC exceed the previous ones.
  7. Refresh. Who: SSD. What changes: CREDIT_LIMIT := received totals (the DLLP's CRC must be good; a bad one is dropped and the next update supersedes it). Evidence: the SSD resumes if it was blocked.

With a switch, steps 4–6 happen once per link; the Requester ID survives, sequence numbers and credits do not.

The gating rule across wrap: a worked example

send if (CL − (CC + R)) mod 2F ≤ 2F ÷ 2
CL
CREDIT_LIMIT, the last total received for this class and VC
CC
CREDITS_CONSUMED so far
R
credits the candidate TLP needs
F
counter width in bits: 8 for headers and 12 for data without scaling

PD counters (F = 12, modulus 4096). Suppose CL = 0x010 after a wrap and CC = 0xFF0. A 256-byte MWr needs R = 16: (0x010 − (0xFF0 + 16)) mod 4096 = (0x010 − 0x000) = 16 ≤ 2048, so it may go; CC becomes 0x000. The next needs (0x010 − 0x010) = 0 ≤ 2048: it may go; CC = 0x010. The third: (0x010 − 0x020) mod 4096 = 4080 > 2048: blocked until an UpdateFC raises CL. Derived A decoder that subtracts without the modulus reports "−16 credits available" here and confuses everyone; the analyzer columns you trust must use modular arithmetic.

Scaled Flow Control. When both ports negotiated scaling, the value in an FC DLLP is multiplied by the scale factor for that field, and the counters behave as if wider. The commonly described encodings are 00b (not supported), 01b (×1), 10b (×4), 11b (×16). Version-dependent Uncertain Confirm encodings, counter widths and any rate-based requirement in the Flow Control section of your revision, as Chapter 53 also cautions. An InitFC1-P with HdrScale = 01b, HdrFC = 32, DataScale = 01b, DataFC = 256 means 32 headers and 4 KiB of data.

When credits meet ordering

The ordering rules of Chapter 14 say, among other things, that a non-posted request must not pass a posted request in the same direction and class of traffic, and that a completion without Relaxed Ordering must not pass a posted request. So when posted credits run out at a receiver, the transmitter's posted queue blocks — and any read request or completion queued behind those writes blocks with it, even with plenty of NP or Cpl credits. Only posted traffic is allowed to pass blocked non-posted traffic, which is what keeps the fabric deadlock-free. Derived This asymmetry is why a posted-credit problem shows up as "the CPU's register read hung" or "completions are late", never as "posted credits are low".

The ladder

Sam may only send a train when Ava's board shows space; question trains may not overtake delivery trains.

Each receiver advertises header and data credits per class; transmitters subtract before sending and wait at zero; UpdateFC carries running totals back.

Ingress SRAM partitions and transmit queues per class, modular counters per class per VC per link, a DLLP scheduler, and a consumer whose drain rate sets the credit loop.

PCI Express Base Specification: credit types and units, gating rule, InitFC/UpdateFC, infinite advertisement, minimum advertisements, Scaled Flow Control, UpdateFC timing, Receiver Overflow and Flow Control Protocol Error; ordering rules in the Transaction Ordering section.

InitFC1/InitFC2 at link-up, UpdateFC-P/NP/Cpl in steady state, always opposite to the TLPs they govern, independently on every link.

Credit balance per class over time; idle gaps that end on an UpdateFC; UpdateFC streams that stop for one class while others continue; totals that stop advancing although TLPs were absorbed.

Throughput low or reads hanging with no errors: find which class reached zero and on which link, then ask why that receiver's consumer stopped — or whether it stopped returning credits at all.

Specification discipline

Applicable specification and revision. PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 — Transaction Layer Specification, Flow Control; DLLP formats and initialisation in PCI Express Base Specification — Data Link Layer Specification; passing rules in PCI Express Base Specification — Transaction Ordering; errors in the AER Extended Capability. NVM Express documents do not define link-level flow control; the NVMe 2.4 rate-limiting features allocate bandwidth and IOPS between controllers at a different layer and must not be confused with credits.

Normative Six credit types (PH, PD, NPH, NPD, CplH, CplD) per VC; one header per header credit and 16 bytes per data credit; initialisation with InitFC1/InitFC2 for VC0 before TLPs; an initial value of 0 means infinite; UpdateFC carries running totals; gating per class and per header/data; per-link (hop-by-hop) operation; Receiver Overflow and Flow Control Protocol Error as uncorrectable errors (fatal by default); ordering rules that forbid non-posted requests and (non-Relaxed-Ordering) completions from passing posted requests.

Informative Explanatory figures and implementation notes. The modular worked example, the ordering-driven hang argument and all bandwidth estimates here are derived.

Optional / Implementation-dependent Advertised amounts above minimums; buffer partitioning and sharing; UpdateFC batching within the rules; overflow checking (CREDITS_RECEIVED); vendor credit counters or performance-monitoring events; whether a port re-advertises after an internal reset (it cannot, short of link re-initialisation).

Version-dependent Scaled Flow Control and its negotiation (4.0 and later); additional shared-credit mechanisms and different FC information carriage in Flit Mode (6.0 and later); the UpdateFC timing guidance and any optional flow-control watchdog. Commonly quoted values are tens of microseconds for the periodic update and longer for the watchdog. Uncertain Confirm the values for your revision.

The specification does not say how many credits a product should advertise for good performance, how quickly a receiver must drain, or what credit-loop time a platform achieves. It does not require receivers to detect overflow or leaks. Check in your revision: the minimum initial advertisement table, the exact rules for infinite completion credits at Root Complexes and switches, header-credit accounting for TLP prefixes, and which flow-control violations must be reported as Flow Control Protocol Error.

Never assume that credits are end to end, that a stalled transmitter logged anything, that "infinite" completion credits mean unlimited completion throughput, that a capture starting mid-stream tells you absolute availability, or that a missing MRd on the wire means the device ignored it — it may never have left the Root Port.

Labs

Lab 1 (beginner) — Credit budget of NVMe commands

Safe lab Pure computation.

Objective. Compute which credits an NVMe READ and WRITE consume at each receiver. Prerequisites. Credit types (Chapter 16). Setup. Python 3.

"""nvme_credit_budget.py - PCIe credits one NVMe command consumes, per receiver.
Derived teaching arithmetic for the lab system (MPS 256 B, MRRS 512 B); assumes the
transfer is a multiple of 512 B and the Root Port returns read data in 256-byte CplDs."""
from math import ceil

MPS, MRRS, CPL = 256, 512, 256

def d(nbytes):                       # data credits: one per 16 bytes
    return ceil(nbytes / 16)

def command(op, nbytes):
    rp = {"PH": 0, "PD": 0, "NPH": 0}            # consumed at the Root Port (SSD -> host)
    ssd = {"PH": 2, "PD": 2, "CplH": 1, "CplD": d(64)}   # 2 doorbells in; SQE completion in
    rp["NPH"] += 1                                # SQE fetch (MRd 64 B)
    if op == "read":                              # data: SSD writes host memory
        n = nbytes // MPS
        rp["PH"] += n
        rp["PD"] += n * d(MPS)
    else:                                         # write: SSD reads host memory
        rp["NPH"] += nbytes // MRRS
        ssd["CplH"] += nbytes // CPL
        ssd["CplD"] += d(nbytes)
    rp["PH"] += 2                                 # CQE (16 B) + MSI-X (4 B)
    rp["PD"] += d(16) + d(4)
    return rp, ssd

if __name__ == "__main__":
    for op, size in [("read", 4096), ("write", 4096), ("read", 131072), ("write", 131072)]:
        rp, ssd = command(op, size)
        print(f"{op:5} {size:6} B | at Root Port: {rp} | at SSD: {ssd}")
    print("SSD completion credits are infinite (advertised as 0), so the CplH/CplD figures")
    print("measure the SSD's own completion buffer, not a credit the Root Port must wait for.")

Procedure. Predict the four result lines by hand, run, then change MPS to 128 and MRRS to 256 and explain every changed number.

Observations. Reads load the Root Port's posted credits; writes load its NPH credits and the SSD's completion buffer. Halving MPS doubles header credits for the same bytes.

Questions. Which receiver's credits limit a 128 KiB READ? Why does halving MRRS hurt a WRITE?

Expected reasoning. The READ is bounded by Root Port PH/PD return; the WRITE by NPH credits and the SSD's outstanding-read capacity; more, smaller requests consume more headers.

Solution

128 KiB READ: 514 PH and 8,194 PD at the Root Port. With MPS 128: 1,026 PH and 8,194 PD — data credits barely change, header credits double. 128 KiB WRITE with MRRS 256: 513 NPH instead of 257, so with a fixed NPH advertisement only half as many bytes can be outstanding at the Root Port.

Expected reasoning. It under-reports availability by the scale factor and "sees" starvation that does not exist.

Solution

(2) (0x100 − 0x108) mod 4096 = 4088 > 2048 → blocked. (3) Under the commonly described encoding 10b = ×4: 64 headers and 512 × 4 × 16 = 32 KiB (confirm encodings in your revision). (4) 0x10 × 4 × 16 = 1 KiB.

Lab 3 (advanced) — Watch backpressure cross a switch

Safe lab Simulation.

Objective. See a Root Port drain stall reach the SSD through a switch. Prerequisites. Trace 2. Setup. Python 3.

"""two_hop_credits.py - posted-data backpressure through a switch (simulation, not a model of
any real device). One step = 10 ns; one 256-byte MWr = 16 PD credits and ~4 steps of link time."""
CR = 16                                            # PD credits per TLP

class Link:
    """Credits the transmitter holds for one hop, and UpdateFCs flying back."""
    def __init__(self, advertised, loop_steps):
        self.avail, self.loop, self.returning = advertised, loop_steps, []
    def take(self):
        if self.avail < CR:
            return False
        self.avail -= CR
        return True
    def give_back(self, now):                      # receiver freed one TLP's buffer
        self.returning.append(now + self.loop)
    def tick(self, now):
        due = [t for t in self.returning if t <= now]
        self.returning = [t for t in self.returning if t > now]
        self.avail += CR * len(due)

def run(stall=(300, 700), steps=1200):
    ssd_sw = Link(advertised=8 * CR, loop_steps=30)   # switch ingress: 8 TLPs
    sw_rp = Link(advertised=8 * CR, loop_steps=40)    # Root Port ingress: 8 TLPs
    sw_buf = rp_buf = sent = drained = 0
    for now in range(steps):
        ssd_sw.tick(now); sw_rp.tick(now)
        if now % 4 == 0:                               # one TLP slot per hop every 40 ns
            if sw_buf and sw_rp.take():                # switch forwards upstream
                sw_buf -= 1; rp_buf += 1; ssd_sw.give_back(now)
            if ssd_sw.take():                          # SSD sends new read data
                sw_buf += 1; sent += 1
            stalled = stall[0] <= now < stall[1]
            if rp_buf and not stalled:                 # Root Port drains into memory
                rp_buf -= 1; drained += 1; sw_rp.give_back(now)
        if now % 100 == 0:
            print(f"t={now * 10:6} ns sent={sent:4} drained={drained:4} "
                  f"switch_buf={sw_buf} rp_buf={rp_buf} "
                  f"SSD_credits={ssd_sw.avail // CR} switch_credits={sw_rp.avail // CR}")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    run()

Procedure. Run with the default 4 µs stall. Then (a) double the switch's advertisement to the SSD, (b) halve the Link A loop, (c) remove the stall, and record "sent" at 11 µs each time.

Observations. During the stall the Root Port buffer fills, then the switch buffer, then the SSD stops.

Questions. Why does more switch buffering delay but not prevent the SSD stall? What sets the steady-state rate?

Expected reasoning. Extra buffering absorbs only (stall time × rate) bytes; the drain is the bottleneck. Steady state is credits × unit ÷ loop on the tighter hop.

Solution

Default: sent stalls at 76 from about 4 µs to 7 µs. (a) The SSD stalls later and sends more before stalling, but stalls. (b) Steady-state rate rises (Link A was the tighter loop). (c) The SSD sends continuously at the credit-limited rate. This is the simulation's behaviour, not a measurement of any product.

Extension. Add a second SSD sharing Link A and show how one device's stall throttles the other.

Lab 4 (senior forensic) — "Drives disappear after irqbalance runs"

Safe lab Synthetic evidence. Reproducing with real affinity changes is a Caution lab on a test machine with no data at risk.

Objective. Turn Trace 3 plus system evidence into a root cause, mitigation and test. Prerequisites. This module; Chapter 43 — MSI-X.

Setup (synthetic). Kernel log: periodic "controller is down; will reset: CSTS=0xffffffff", then successful resets; no AER from the SSD; irqbalance activity before each event; one firmware version affected; a link-up capture as in Trace 3.

Procedure. Write the causal chain from the first abnormal event to the reset; list alternative hypotheses and why the evidence rejects them; propose mitigation, fix and regression test.

Questions. Why does the device look dead although it is healthy? Why does a reset "fix" it?

Solution

Chain: table write → SSD frees data but not header space → PH balance drops by one per write → after enough affinity changes PH = 0 → next doorbell blocks in the Root Port → the driver's CSTS read blocks behind it (no pass) → platform completion timeout → all-ones → driver reset → link re-initialisation restores credits. Rejected: SSD firmware hang (UpdateFC-NP and periodic UpdateFC-P continue, so its Data Link Layer is alive); signal integrity (no Bad DLLP, no Naks); Root Port fault (other devices fine; balance computation pins the leak on the SSD). Mitigation: static interrupt affinity for the NVMe vectors. Fix: SSD firmware returning header credits for BAR writes to the MSI-X region. Regression: a loop of mask/rewrite/unmask on every vector, verifying from a capture that HdrFC advances one per write. Confidence: high, because the leak is visible directly in the DLLPs.

Extension. Draft the validation rule "every class's running total must advance by exactly the credits of each TLP it absorbs, within the UpdateFC latency" as an automated analyzer check.

Interview

Beginner

  1. What does one data credit represent?

    Model answer

    16 bytes of buffer for one class and VC.

  2. Why do UpdateFCs carry running totals?

    Model answer

    A lost update is corrected by the next; increments would drift forever.

  3. Are credits end to end?

    Model answer

    No, per link. A switch has its own credits on each port.

  4. Which credits does an MSI-X write use?

    Model answer

    1 PH and 1 PD at the Root Port, like any small posted write.

  5. What does an advertisement of 0 mean?

    Model answer

    Infinite credits for that type; the transmitter never gates on it.

Intermediate

  1. CL = 0x010, CC = 0xFF0 (12-bit), R = 16. May the TLP go?

    Model answer

    Yes: (0x010 − 0x000) mod 4096 = 16 ≤ 2048.

  2. How do you tell a credit stall from a replay-buffer stall?

    Model answer

    Credit stalls end on an UpdateFC for the starved class; replay stalls end on an Ack. If both arrive together, look across many gaps or delay one DLLP type in a lab.

  3. Why can a posted-credit shortage hang a register read?

    Model answer

    A non-posted request may not pass a posted request; the read waits behind the blocked write in the Root Port.

  4. Why do endpoints advertise infinite completion credits?

    Model answer

    They only receive completions for their own requests and must reserve space before requesting; refusing completions could deadlock the fabric.

  5. What does a capture without InitFC prevent?

    Model answer

    Computing absolute availability; only changes are known.

Advanced

  1. Size the Root Port's PD advertisement for 7 GB/s with a 1 µs loop.

    Model answer

    7 bytes/ns × 1,000 ns ÷ 16 ≈ 440 credits (≈ 7 KB); scaling needed beyond the unscaled 2,048-credit half-range only at higher rates or loops.

  2. How does backpressure propagate through a switch, and how fast?

    Model answer

    Hop by hop at buffer-filling speed: the downstream port stops returning credits once its buffer cannot drain upstream; delay equals buffer size ÷ arrival rate per hop.

  3. What is a credit leak and how do you prove one?

    Model answer

    A receiver absorbs TLPs without ever returning their credits. Prove it from a link-up capture: running totals fail to advance by the credits of absorbed TLPs, while the receiver is otherwise alive.

  4. Why can Bad DLLP errors look like flow-control problems?

    Model answer

    A corrupted UpdateFC is dropped; the transmitter waits for the next one, producing short stalls.

  5. How does Scaled Flow Control change decoding?

    Model answer

    Field values are multiplied by negotiated scale factors; decoders must track the scale bits from initialisation onward.

Senior

  1. A customer says reads are fast and writes slow on one platform. Plan?

    Model answer

    Check MRRS and topology; capture to see NPH balance and MRd rate; compare SSD tags in use; test larger MRRS; separate Root Port NPH limits from the SSD's completion-buffer self-throttle.

  2. Write a validation rule for credit return.

    Model answer

    For every class, the receiver's running total must advance by the credits of each absorbed TLP within the UpdateFC latency guidance, and never exceed what the transmitter could have consumed; flag freezes while the receiver is alive.

  3. How do you mitigate a credit leak in the field before a firmware fix?

    Model answer

    Avoid the triggering traffic (for example pin interrupt affinity), and rely on reset-based recovery; document the trigger and monitor resets.

  4. How would you explain to management why "no errors" coexists with a 50 % throughput loss?

    Model answer

    Flow control works by waiting, not failing; starvation is legal and silent. The loss is measurable only with captures or performance counters.

  5. Would you accept a design with 16 PH credits at an NVMe endpoint?

    Model answer

    Only if bursts of doorbells and table writes from many CPUs never exceed it within one credit loop, and leaks are impossible; I would ask for measured worst-case doorbell bursts and a leak test.

Quiz

  1. Which credits does an SQ tail doorbell consume?

    • NPH at the SSD
    • PH and PD at the SSD
    • PH at the Root Port
    • None
    Answer and reasoning

    A host MWr to the SSD's BAR is posted traffic received by the SSD.

  2. UpdateFC-NP keeps arriving but UpdateFC-P stops. Most likely:

    • Link down
    • Data Link Layer dead
    • Posted drain stalled
    • Replay buffer full
    Answer and reasoning

    The port is alive; only one class stopped draining.

  3. (0x100 − 0x108) mod 4096 means:

    • Blocked
    • 8 credits available
    • Infinite
    • Overflow error
    Answer and reasoning

    4088 > 2048: the TLP would exceed the limit.

  4. A completion without Relaxed Ordering behind a blocked posted write:

    • Passes it
    • Is dropped
    • Causes Receiver Overflow
    • Waits
    Answer and reasoning

    Completions may not pass posted requests unless ordering is relaxed.

  5. Which error does starvation log?

    • Receiver Overflow
    • Flow Control Protocol Error
    • None
    • Completion Timeout always
    Answer and reasoning

    Starvation is legal; any timeout is a downstream consequence, not a flow-control error.

  6. Through a switch, the SSD's credits for its MWrs come from:

    • The Root Port
    • The switch downstream port
    • The IOMMU
    • The NVMe driver
    Answer and reasoning

    Credits are per link.

  7. A credit leak is best proven by:

    • Running totals that stop advancing for absorbed TLPs
    • A Completion Timeout
    • Low throughput
    • Bad DLLP
    Answer and reasoning

    Only the DLLP totals separate a leak from a slow consumer.

  8. Open: Why is "the device never answered the read" sometimes false?

    Model answer

    The read may be blocked in the Root Port behind posted writes that lack credits; it never reached the link.

  9. Open: What must you capture to compute absolute credit availability?

    Model answer

    The InitFC exchange at link-up (and scale negotiation) plus every TLP and UpdateFC since.

Teach-back

To a child: Ava's chalkboard, Sam's tally, and why a stuck crane stops the blue train.

Model explanation

"Sam only sends a train if Ava's board says there's room. Ava writes bigger numbers as she unloads. When her crane sticks, red trains wait — and the blue train waits too, because the rule says it can't overtake a red one."

To a junior engineer: how to decide whether an idle gap in a capture is a credit stall.

Model explanation

"Find the DLLP that ends the gap. If it's an UpdateFC for the class the transmitter was sending, and your computed balance for that class was below one TLP, it's a credit stall. If it's an Ack, look at the replay buffer. If you didn't capture link-up, you only know changes, so say 'undetermined' and re-capture."

To a senior engineer: why a posted-header credit leak at an SSD can look like a dead controller.

Model explanation

"When PH reaches zero, the Root Port holds the next doorbell, and ordering holds the CSTS read behind it. The CPU times out, reads all-ones, the driver resets, and the reset restores credits — so it looks like a transient device hang. The proof is in the UpdateFC totals from link-up; the trigger is whatever traffic leaks the credit."
