The documents, tools, books and papers this course is built on, annotated so you know what each one is for, which chapters lean on it, and what to look for when you open it. Specifications come first because they are the only authority on what is required; everything else is explanation, implementation or evidence.

How to use these references

A reference list is only useful if you know which document answers which kind of question. Engineers waste days reading a textbook to settle a question only the specification can answer, or reading a specification to find out what Linux actually does, which only the kernel source can answer. Before opening anything, classify your question:

This page reflects the specification set released by NVM Express on 4 August 2026 (NVMe Base Specification 2.4, ratified 31 July 2026). Specifications are revised, errata are published and Technical Proposals are ratified between revisions. Before citing a requirement in a bug report, design review or test plan, confirm the current revision at nvmexpress.org/specifications and check the Revision Changes document for anything that moved. For PCIe, confirm the revision your device and platform claim to support; behaviour that is required in one revision may be optional or absent in another.

Citation style in this course: specifications are cited by document name, revision and section name — never by section number, because section numbers change between revisions and an invented number is worse than none. For example: NVM Express Base Specification, Revision 2.4 — Controller Registers. When you write your own reports, do the same and add the revision explicitly.

Supporting NVM Express material

Ratified Technical Proposals (TPs) and Engineering Change Notices (ECNs)
Changes approved after a revision was published and before they are folded into the next one. A feature can exist in a ratified TP long before it appears in a numbered revision; devices may implement it and report it in Identify data. When a device advertises something your copy of the specification does not describe, look here. Version-dependent
Errata
Corrections to published revisions. An erratum can change the meaning of a field; check before arguing from a single sentence.
Older revisions (1.0 through 1.4, 2.0 to 2.3)
Essential when you debug older devices or read older driver code: NVMe 1.x was a single document that included the PCIe transport; 2.0 split it into the family above. A field's name, location or meaning may differ. The Specification Reading School shows how to diff revisions.
2026 release coverage
The August 2026 features (post-quantum cryptography preparation, PCIe Exported NVM Subsystem Migration, the TDISP information package, lockdown improvements, rate limiting, voltage monitoring, idle I/O exit latency limits, restore manufacturing default settings) are summarised in 2026 NVMe 2.4 Updates and M60. Press coverage of the release is a secondary source; field-level details must come from the Base 2.4 specification and the Revision Changes document. Field-level details not verified in this course

Do not read front to back. Read the Theory of Operation / architecture overview of the Base Specification, then the controller register (property) definitions, then the queueing model and the command format, then Identify and Get Log Page, then the NVM Command Set's Read and Write. Only then open the NVMe over PCIe Transport Specification and read doorbells, PRPs and interrupts. That is the order this course follows from Chapter 21 onward; Specification Reading School and Reading Plan expand it.

PCI-SIG documentation

The Peripheral Component Interconnect Special Interest Group (PCI-SIG) develops the PCI Express (PCIe) specifications. Unlike NVMe, the full PCIe specifications are distributed to PCI-SIG members; non-members can see the specification list, overviews, FAQs, press material and some white papers at pcisig.com (the specification library is at pcisig.com/specifications). If your employer is a member, request access through it. If not, the books listed below explain the specifications in depth, and Linux's include/uapi/linux/pci_regs.h is a precise public record of register layouts the kernel relies on.

Vendor documentation

Everything in this section describes one company's product. It is invaluable for understanding that product and never authoritative about what the standards require. In reports, label it explicitly: "per the vendor datasheet" or "per the analyzer user manual", never as if it were the specification.

Protocol analyzer and exerciser manuals Vendor
PCIe protocol analyzer vendors (Teledyne LeCroy is the vendor the course's analyzer chapter names; others exist) document capture setup, interposer selection, triggering, filtering, decoding NVMe from PCIe traffic, and their capture file formats and export options. Read the manual for your analyzer and software version before trusting a decode: decoding NVMe from TLPs requires the tool to know BAR addresses and queue locations, and a mis-configured decode looks plausible while being wrong. See Teledyne LeCroy Analyzer Training and M55.
PCIe switch documentation Vendor
Switch data books and application notes describe port configuration, per-port error counters, integrated debug and capture features, and their register maps. These are the source for PEX / Capture File Training and M56; formats are proprietary and differ between product families. Often available only under non-disclosure agreement.
SSD datasheets and product manuals Vendor
Supported NVMe revision and optional features, power states and their latencies, endurance ratings (TBW/DWPD), sustained versus burst performance, power-loss protection, vendor-specific log pages and telemetry decode (sometimes only through a vendor tool). Compare the datasheet's claims with Identify Controller data.
CPU, chipset and platform documentation Vendor
Root complex behaviour, root-port error handling, firmware-first versus OS-first AER, IOMMU implementation, MSI address windows, and BIOS settings for ASPM, MPS and bifurcation. Server vendor documentation explains how drive bays map to root ports and how hot-plug is wired.
Test equipment application notes Vendor
Oscilloscope and bit-error-rate tester vendors publish material on PCIe transmitter and receiver compliance, equalization and PAM4 measurement. Useful background for Equalization and Validation Engineering.

Books

Books explain; specifications decide. Every book below predates the 2026 specification set, so use books for mental models and specifications for current facts. Each entry says what the book is good for and where it is likely to be out of date.

PCI Express

Ravi Budruk, Don Anderson and Tom Shanley — PCI Express System Architecture (MindShare / Addison-Wesley, 2003)
The classic explanation of PCIe 1.x: layers, TLP and DLLP formats, Ack/Nak, flow control, ordering, configuration, interrupts, power management. Still the clearest description of the transaction and data link layers. Predates 8 GT/s and later; nothing on 128b/130b, equalization, flit mode or SR-IOV.
Mike Jackson and Ravi Budruk — PCI Express Technology 3.0 (MindShare, 2012)
Updates the MindShare treatment to PCIe 3.0: 8 GT/s, 128b/130b encoding, equalization, and newer protocol features. The best single book for Part IV to Part VI. Predates Gen4 and later.

Solid-state storage and NAND

Rino Micheloni, Alessia Marelli and Kam Eshghi (eds.) — Inside Solid State Drives (SSDs) (Springer, first edition 2013; second edition 2018)
SSD architecture from the inside: NAND, controllers, FTL, ECC, reliability. Background for Chapter 2 and Chapter 57.
Seiichi Aritome — NAND Flash Memory Technologies (Wiley–IEEE Press, 2015)
Device-level NAND physics, scaling and reliability mechanisms. Deeper than most protocol engineers need, but the right place to understand why read latency rises with wear.

Operating systems and Linux

Remzi H. Arpaci-Dusseau and Andrea C. Arpaci-Dusseau — Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces (free online at pages.cs.wisc.edu/~remzi/OSTEP)
Its persistence chapters (I/O devices, hard disk drives, flash-based SSDs, file systems, crash consistency) are an excellent beginner path into Part I and Part II.
Jonathan Corbet, Alessandro Rubini and Greg Kroah-Hartman — Linux Device Drivers, 3rd edition (O'Reilly, 2005; free online via LWN)
Still the best explanation of how drivers talk to PCI devices, map memory and handle interrupts. Many kernel APIs have changed since; use it for concepts and the current kernel documentation for APIs.
Robert Love — Linux Kernel Development, 3rd edition (Addison-Wesley, 2010)
Kernel internals (interrupts, bottom halves, block layer basics) at the level needed to read drivers/nvme. The block layer has since moved to blk-mq.

Performance, queueing and measurement

Brendan Gregg — Systems Performance: Enterprise and the Cloud, 2nd edition (Addison-Wesley, 2020)
Methodology for performance analysis, with disk and file-system chapters and Linux observability tools. Pairs with Performance Engineering.
Brendan Gregg — BPF Performance Tools (Addison-Wesley, 2019)
bpftrace and BCC tools for disk I/O latency histograms and block-layer tracing.
Raj Jain — The Art of Computer Systems Performance Analysis (Wiley, 1991)
Experimental design, measurement and statistics, and basic queueing theory. The reference for "how do I know this benchmark result is real".
Mor Harchol-Balter — Performance Modeling and Design of Computer Systems: Queueing Theory in Action (Cambridge University Press, 2013)
Rigorous but readable queueing theory: Little's law, M/M/1, utilization and response time. Background for Mathematics for Storage and Links.
John L. Hennessy and David A. Patterson — Computer Architecture: A Quantitative Approach (Morgan Kaufmann, multiple editions)
Memory hierarchy, I/O and storage chapters; the quantitative style this course asks of you.

Signal integrity and debugging method

Eric Bogatin — Signal and Power Integrity — Simplified (Prentice Hall, multiple editions)
Intuition for loss, reflections, crosstalk and eyes; why an interposer or a long trace changes link behaviour. Background for Equalization.
David J. Agans — Debugging: The 9 Indispensable Rules for Finding Even the Most Elusive Software and Hardware Problems (AMACOM, 2002)
A short, practical book on method: make it fail, quit thinking and look, change one thing at a time. The spirit of Debugging Methodology.

Research papers

The papers below are well-known, peer-reviewed works. Venue and year are given so you can find them in the ACM Digital Library, USENIX proceedings (open access) or IEEE Xplore. Where this course is less certain about a specific paper, it describes the research category instead of naming one.

SSD architecture and flash translation layers

  • Nitin Agrawal, Vijayan Prabhakaran, Ted Wobber, John D. Davis, Mark Manasse and Rina Panigrahy — "Design Tradeoffs for SSD Performance", USENIX Annual Technical Conference, 2008. The paper that explained to the systems community how parallelism, FTL mapping and cleaning shape SSD performance.
  • Aayush Gupta, Youngjae Kim and Bhuvan Urgaonkar — "DFTL: A Flash Translation Layer Employing Demand-based Selective Caching of Page-level Address Mappings", ASPLOS, 2009. Page-level mapping with a cached mapping table; the root of the L2P-caching ideas behind DRAM-less SSDs and HMB.
  • Feng Chen, David A. Koufaty and Xiaodong Zhang — "Understanding Intrinsic Characteristics and System Implications of Flash Memory based Solid State Drives", ACM SIGMETRICS, 2009. Measurement-driven study of SSD behaviour visible from the host.
  • Xiao-Yu Hu, Evangelos Eleftheriou, Robert Haas, Ilias Iliadis and Roman Pletka — "Write Amplification Analysis in Flash-Based Solid State Drives", SYSTOR, 2009. Analytical model linking over-provisioning and garbage collection to write amplification; used in the mathematics page.
  • Jun He, Sudarsun Kannan, Andrea C. Arpaci-Dusseau and Remzi H. Arpaci-Dusseau — "The Unwritten Contract of Solid State Drives", EuroSys, 2017. Rules that host software must follow to get good SSD behaviour, and what happens when it does not.

Flash reliability

  • Laura M. Grupp, Adrian M. Caulfield, Joel Coburn, Steven Swanson, Eitan Yaakobi, Paul H. Siegel and Jack K. Wolf — "Characterizing Flash Memory: Anomalies, Observations, and Applications", IEEE/ACM MICRO, 2009.
  • Laura M. Grupp, John D. Davis and Steven Swanson — "The Bleak Future of NAND Flash Memory", USENIX FAST, 2012.
  • Yu Cai, Saugata Ghose, Erich F. Haratsch, Yixin Luo and Onur Mutlu — "Error Characterization, Mitigation, and Recovery in Flash-Memory-Based Solid-State Drives", Proceedings of the IEEE, 2017. A comprehensive survey of NAND error mechanisms and controller countermeasures.
  • Justin Meza, Qiang Wu, Sanjeev Kumar and Onur Mutlu — "A Large-Scale Study of Flash Memory Failures in the Field", ACM SIGMETRICS, 2015.
  • Bianca Schroeder, Raghav Lagisetty and Arif Merchant — "Flash Reliability in Production: The Expected and the Unexpected", USENIX FAST, 2016. Field data on uncorrectable errors and bad blocks; read it before assuming SMART counters predict failure.

  • Jaehyun Hwang, Qizhe Cai, Ao Tang and Rachit Agarwal — "TCP ≈ RDMA: CPU-efficient Remote Storage Access with i10", USENIX NSDI, 2020. Relevant to the NVMe/TCP versus NVMe/RDMA trade-off in Chapter 66 and Chapter 67.

Computational storage

  • Erik Riedel, Garth Gibson and Christos Faloutsos — "Active Storage for Large-Scale Data Mining and Multimedia", VLDB, 1998. An early statement of moving computation to storage.
  • Sudharsan Seshadri, Mark Gahagan, Sundaram Bhaskaran, Trevor Bunker, Arup De, Yanqin Jin, Yang Liu and Steven Swanson — "Willow: A User-Programmable SSD", USENIX OSDI, 2014.
  • Boncheol Gu and co-authors — "Biscuit: A Framework for Near-Data Processing of Big Data Workloads", ISCA, 2016.

Queueing theory

  • John D. C. Little — "A Proof for the Queuing Formula: L = λW", Operations Research 9(3), 1961. The original proof of Little's law used throughout the mathematics page.

Read the abstract, then the evaluation setup: which device, which interface, which revision, which kernel. Research prototypes are often FPGA or emulator based and older devices behave differently from current ones. Take the mechanism and the question it asks into your work; re-measure the numbers on your own hardware.

Community, training and conference material

NVM Express educational material
NVM Express, Inc. publishes webinars, presentations and white papers alongside the specifications, including overviews of each release. Good for orientation; the specifications remain authoritative.
PCI-SIG Developers Conference presentations
Overviews of new revisions (for example flit mode and 64/128 GT/s signalling) are often presented here; some material is public.
Flash Memory Summit / FMS and Storage Developer Conference (SNIA SDC)
Talks on SSD architecture, NVMe features, ZNS, FDP and computational storage, often by the engineers who wrote the specifications. Category reference: search the proceedings for the feature you are studying.
Linux Plumbers Conference, LSF/MM and LWN.net
Discussion of NVMe driver and block-layer design decisions. LWN.net articles are carefully edited and dated, which helps when behaviour is version-dependent.
UNH-IOL NVMe and PCIe testing services
The University of New Hampshire InterOperability Laboratory runs NVMe conformance and interoperability testing and publishes test plans that are a useful model for Validation Engineering.
