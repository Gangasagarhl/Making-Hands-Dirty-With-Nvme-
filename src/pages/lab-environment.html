This page tells you how to build the places where you will practise: from offline trace files and a QEMU virtual machine with an emulated NVMe controller, through a physical Linux machine with a spare SSD, to a protocol-analyzer bench. It defines the course's three safety tiers (SAFE, CAUTION, EXPERT), gives a spare-drive policy, and shows how to make the risky labs happen only where nothing of value can be lost.

Why a lab environment guide

NVMe and PCIe are learned with your hands: by running commands, reading registers, capturing traces and breaking things on purpose. The same commands that teach you also format drives, rewrite firmware, reset controllers under a live filesystem and take links down. Every year engineers destroy data, and occasionally hardware, by running a forum one-liner against the wrong /dev/nvme node.

The course's answer is simple. Prefer safe environments. Learn every command first where the worst outcome is deleting an image file. Move to real hardware only for what emulation cannot teach (timing, power, link behaviour), and then only on hardware dedicated to the lab. Never run destructive tests on production drives, on drives holding data anyone cares about, or on the disk your operating system boots from.

The tools used here are taught in Chapter 69 (lspci, setpci, sysfs), Chapter 70 (nvme-cli, tracepoints) and Chapter 71 (kernel logs). Analyzer use is covered in Teledyne LeCroy Analyzer Training and PEX / Capture File Training.

A chemistry teaching lab has a demonstration bench, student benches with safe reagents, and a fume cupboard for dangerous reactions, plus rules about which bottle may go where. This guide is the same idea: an offline desk (trace files), a virtual bench (QEMU), a physical bench (spare drive) and a fume cupboard (expert operations on sacrificial hardware), with labels on every bottle.

Where the analogy breaks: in a chemistry lab the dangerous bottle looks dangerous. In a Linux shell, nvme format and nvme fw-log look equally harmless, the "wrong bench" is a one-character difference in a device name, and a passthrough VM can make a virtual bench touch a real drive. The labels must therefore be procedural (checklists, allowlists, guard scripts), not visual.

The three safety tiers

Every lab, command and hardware procedure in this course carries one of three labels. These definitions are the course's policy.

SAFE LAB Safe lab

Read-only inspection. Nothing you do can change stored data, device configuration or link state in a way that persists or that another user would notice. Examples: lspci (including -vvv), setpci without =, reading sysfs, nvme list/id-ctrl/id-ns/smart-log/error-log/fw-log/get-feature/show-regs, dmesg/journalctl, tracepoints, blktrace, reading offline trace files, and everything done inside a QEMU guest against an emulated NVMe controller backed by a scratch image.

Residual effects you should still know about: queries consume admin queue slots and firmware time; config reads can wake a sleeping device; tracing adds overhead. On production machines, even SAFE operations are deliberate, minimal and announced.

CAUTION LAB Caution lab

Operations that can affect devices, recoverably. They change device state, interrupt I/O in flight, or change how the system treats the device, but do not by design destroy data. Examples: nvme reset (controller reset), writing reset_controller/rescan_controller or PCI reset/remove/rescan in sysfs, nvme set-feature without save (APST, thermal thresholds, write cache), short device self-test, host-initiated telemetry capture, unbinding a device from nvme and binding it to vfio-pci or SPDK, kernel parameters that change power management or error handling (pcie_aspm=off, nvme_core.default_ps_max_latency_us=0), enabling kernel fault injection, reading LBAs with raw passthrough reads.

Rules: only on a lab machine or an emulated device; nothing mounted from the target; the target is not the boot disk; you have written down the command, the expected result and how to recover.

EXPERT LAB Expert lab

Potentially destructive commands. They erase or overwrite data, change identity or firmware, change persistent settings, affect every controller in a subsystem, or can leave a device unusable. Examples: nvme format, sanitize, write/write-zeroes/write-uncor/dsm (deallocate), namespace create/delete/attach/detach, fw-download/fw-commit, security-send, set-feature --save, subsystem-reset, arbitrary admin-passthru/io-passthru, any 2.4-era manufacturing-default restore or lockdown change; setpci writes (Command, Device Control, Link Control); mapping and writing resource0; error-injection hardware (jammers, exercisers); power-loss testing; hot-removing a drive that is not designed or configured for surprise removal.

Rules: only on an emulated device or a designated spare drive in a lab machine (see the spare-drive policy); a second person or a written checklist confirms the target serial number; you accept that the drive may be lost; never on production drives, never on drives with data, never on the boot disk.

Never encourage, perform or "just quickly try" destructive testing on production drives. Never run an EXPERT command whose target you identified by device name alone. Never pass a production drive through to a VM "to experiment safely": PCI passthrough gives the guest the real hardware.

Pre-flight checklist for every CAUTION or EXPERT step

  • I am on a lab machine or inside a VM with emulated storage, not on a production or shared system.
  • I identified the target by serial number (nvme list -v, /sys/class/nvme/nvmeX/serial), and it is on my lab allowlist.
  • Nothing from the target is mounted or used as swap (findmnt, lsblk, /proc/swaps), and it is not the boot disk.
  • I wrote down the exact command, the expected outcome and the recovery path.
  • For EXPERT: I accept that this drive may be lost, and nobody else depends on it.
  • I am capturing evidence (kernel log, tracepoints, analyzer) so the experiment teaches something even if it goes wrong.

Safety tiers on the ladder

Looking at the library's books is always fine. Moving books around needs the teacher's permission. Throwing books away happens only in the practice library made of cardboard.

Read-only commands are SAFE; state-changing commands are CAUTION; data-, firmware- or identity-changing commands are EXPERT and run only on emulated or sacrificial devices.

The danger lives in specific hardware actions: media erase, firmware slot writes, link disable, bus-master disable, DMA to wrong addresses.

The NVMe Base Specification's Commands Supported and Effects log lets a controller declare, per command, whether it changes logical block content, the namespace inventory or controller capabilities, and whether it needs I/O to be quiesced. That is the specification's own safety label.

On the wire, a SAFE command is an admin command with a data-in transfer; an EXPERT command may be a short admin command whose effect is entirely inside the device (a Format NVM SQE is 64 bytes, like an Identify).

In a trace you cannot tell danger from size: you must decode the opcode. That is why lab captures always record the command opcodes issued.

When an incident follows an "inspection", seniors ask for the exact commands run, check the effects log, and look for state-changing opcodes in the trace before blaming the device.

Offline trace files

You can do a large part of this course without running anything against hardware:

  • Synthetic traces in this course (labelled synthetic), the Trace Analysis Laboratory, and the interactive TLP Header Decoder, NVMe Command Decoder and queue simulator.
  • Saved Linux evidence: lspci -xxxx dumps decoded later with lspci -F; nvme-cli JSON and binary (-b) outputs; trace-cmd .dat files (re-readable with trace-cmd report -i); journal exports (journalctl -o export); blktrace binary files with blkparse/btt.
  • Analyzer capture files: vendor analysis software can often open saved captures without the analyzer hardware attached. Licensing and features vary. Vendor-dependent
  • QEMU trace logs from the NVMe device model (pci_nvme_* events), a "device-side" view of emulated traffic.

Keep an evidence directory per experiment: commands run, tool versions, kernel version, raw outputs, and a README with what you expected and what happened. Offline evidence is how you learn from a lab session weeks later, and it is how real investigations are handed between engineers. See Synthetic vs Real Traces for provenance rules.

Linux VM with QEMU emulated NVMe

Safe lab This is the course's default practice environment. A Linux host runs QEMU with KVM acceleration; the guest sees an NVMe controller implemented in software by QEMU and backed by an ordinary file. Format it, sanitize it, delete its namespaces, reset it a thousand times: the worst outcome is recreating the image.

Host prerequisites

grep -cE 'vmx|svm' /proc/cpuinfo      # > 0: CPU virtualization extensions present
ls -l /dev/kvm                        # KVM available and accessible
qemu-system-x86_64 --version
qemu-system-x86_64 -device nvme,help | head -40     # properties your QEMU's NVMe model supports

Install QEMU from your distribution (package names vary: qemu-system-x86, qemu-kvm, qemu-img). Use any Linux cloud image or installer for the guest; inside the guest install pciutils nvme-cli trace-cmd bpftrace blktrace fio sysstat.

A realistic topology: q35 with a PCIe root port

The q35 machine type gives the guest a PCI Express topology, so the emulated controller sits behind an emulated root port, as on real hardware, and lspci -tv looks familiar.

qemu-img create -f raw nvme-ns1.img 4G
qemu-img create -f raw nvme-ns2.img 1G

qemu-system-x86_64 -machine q35,accel=kvm -cpu host -m 4G -smp 4 \
  -drive file=guest.qcow2,if=virtio \
  -device pcie-root-port,id=rp1,bus=pcie.0,chassis=1,slot=1 \
  -device nvme-subsys,id=subsys0,nqn=nqn.2026-01.example.lab:subsys0 \
  -device nvme,id=ctrl0,serial=LAB0000000001,subsys=subsys0,bus=rp1 \
  -drive file=nvme-ns1.img,if=none,id=ns1,format=raw \
  -device nvme-ns,drive=ns1,bus=ctrl0,nsid=1 \
  -drive file=nvme-ns2.img,if=none,id=ns2,format=raw \
  -device nvme-ns,drive=ns2,bus=ctrl0,nsid=2,logical_block_size=4096,physical_block_size=4096 \
  -trace 'pci_nvme_*' -D qemu-nvme-trace.log \
  -monitor unix:qemu-monitor.sock,server,nowait \
  -nographic
  • pcie-root-port: the emulated root port, so the controller appears at a bus below it, like 01:00.0.
  • nvme-subsys + subsys=: an explicit NVM subsystem with an NQN, so nvme list-subsys has something to show (and you can add a second controller to the same subsystem to practise multipath naming).
  • nvme-ns: one device per namespace; namespace 2 uses 4 KiB logical blocks, like the reference system's namespace 2.
  • -trace 'pci_nvme_*' -D file: QEMU writes its NVMe model's events (doorbells, command processing, completions) to a log, your device-side trace.
  • -monitor unix:…: a control socket for hot-plug experiments (device_del).

Property names (nvme-subsys, nvme-ns, logical_block_size, mdts, max_ioqpairs, msix_qsize and others) and the set of emulated NVMe features depend on the QEMU version. Older QEMU releases accept only -device nvme,drive=…,serial=… with a single namespace. Always check -device nvme,help and -device nvme-ns,help and the QEMU NVMe documentation for your release.

Assigning a real NVMe SSD to a VM with VFIO passthrough (-device vfio-pci,host=…) gives the guest direct control of the physical drive. Everything the guest does happens to real hardware. A passthrough VM is a physical environment wearing a virtual hat: apply the physical-system rules and the spare-drive policy.

Physical Linux system and NVMe SSD

Emulation cannot show you a link training to Gen4, a correctable error burst, a drive throttling at 80 °C or the latency of leaving a deep power state. For that you need real hardware. There are two kinds of physical system in this course, and the difference matters.

Your everyday machine: SAFE only

A laptop or workstation whose only NVMe SSD holds your operating system and data is a perfectly good place for SAFE labs: lspci, sysfs, nvme-cli queries, kernel logs, tracepoints and read-only benchmarks through the filesystem. It is never a place for CAUTION or EXPERT steps. A controller reset of your boot disk can hang the system; a format destroys it.

A dedicated lab machine with a spare SSD

  • Machine: any x86-64 or Arm server/desktop with a free M.2 slot or a U.2/E3.S bay, ideally with a CPU-attached root port (for clean Gen4/Gen5 links) and IOMMU support. Boot from a different device (a SATA SSD, a USB drive, or a separate NVMe drive that is never a target).
  • Firmware settings: note (do not blindly change) PCIe speed settings, ASPM, IOMMU (VT-d / AMD-Vi), bifurcation, hot-plug, error handling mode (OS-native vs firmware-first). Record them in the lab notebook; they change what you will observe.
  • Operating system: a current distribution kernel, plus pciutils nvme-cli trace-cmd bpftrace blktrace fio sysstat rasdaemon. Make the journal persistent so you keep kernel logs across the reboots experiments cause.
  • The spare SSD: see the policy below. Several cheap drives of different controller families teach more than one expensive drive.
  • Power and cooling: a heatsink or airflow on M.2 drives (thermal throttling otherwise contaminates performance labs); a smart power strip or a remote power controller makes power-cycle experiments repeatable.
mkdir -p ~/lab/baseline && cd ~/lab/baseline

Spare-drive policy

Every CAUTION and EXPERT lab on real hardware uses a designated spare drive. The policy below exists because every rule in it was written after somebody lost something.

  1. Designate and label. A spare drive is physically labelled "LAB — DATA WILL BE DESTROYED" with its serial number. It never holds real data, not even temporarily.
  2. Register the serial. Its serial number goes into the lab allowlist file (one serial per line). Tools and scripts refuse to act on any drive whose serial is not listed.
  3. Never the boot disk. The operating system boots from another device. A spare is never mounted at /, /boot, /home or used as swap.
  4. Target by serial, every time. Before each CAUTION/EXPERT command, re-check the serial of the device node you are about to use. Device names change across reboots, hot-plug and multipath.
  5. Unmount and detach. Nothing from the spare is mounted, part of a RAID/LVM/ZFS pool, or in use by a VM.
  6. Separate roles. Keep a "reference" drive that only ever sees SAFE and CAUTION labs (your known-good comparison) and "abuse" drives for EXPERT work (format, sanitize, firmware, power-loss, error injection).
  7. Firmware with care. Only vendor-provided images for that exact model; record the previous revision (fw-log); know that a failed or interrupted update can make a drive unusable. Treat every firmware lab as consuming the drive.
  8. Power-loss and hot-removal tests only on drives and slots intended for them, with the filesystem unmounted and with evidence capture running.
  9. Retire visibly. A drive that behaves strangely after abuse is labelled and retired, not quietly returned to the pool where it will confuse someone's next experiment.
  10. Production is out of scope. No drive from a production system, a customer return under investigation (unless that is your job and you follow its procedure), or a colleague's machine is ever a spare.

The guard script below makes rules 2–5 mechanical. It only reads; it prints GO or NO-GO and exits non-zero on NO-GO, so you can put it in front of any lab command: python3 labguard.py /dev/nvme1n1 && sudo nvme format ….

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Read-only GO/NO-GO check before a CAUTION/EXPERT lab command.

  python3 labguard.py /dev/nvme1        # controller node
  python3 labguard.py /dev/nvme1n1      # namespace node
  python3 labguard.py --selftest        # runs against a fake sysfs tree, no hardware

Allowlist: one serial number per line in ~/.nvme-lab-allowlist (or $NVME_LAB_ALLOWLIST).
"""
import os
import re
import sys
import tempfile


def controller_of(dev, sysroot):
    """Return the controller name (nvmeX) behind /dev/nvmeX or /dev/nvmeXnY."""
    name = os.path.basename(dev)
    if re.fullmatch(r"nvme\d+", name):
        return name
    # Namespace: /sys/block/nvmeXnY/device points at the controller (nvmeX).
    link = os.path.join(sysroot, "block", name, "device")
    if os.path.islink(link):
        return os.path.basename(os.readlink(link))
    m = re.fullmatch(r"(nvme\d+)n\d+", name)
    return m.group(1) if m else None


def read(path, default=""):
    try:
        with open(path) as f:
            return f.read().strip()
    except OSError:
        return default


def check(dev, sysroot="/sys", mounts="/proc/mounts", swaps="/proc/swaps", allowlist=None):
    problems = []
    ctrl = controller_of(dev, sysroot)
    if not ctrl:
        return ["cannot determine the controller for " + dev], None
    serial = read(os.path.join(sysroot, "class", "nvme", ctrl, "serial"))
    if not serial:
        problems.append(f"no serial readable for {ctrl}")
    allow_file = allowlist or os.environ.get("NVME_LAB_ALLOWLIST",
                                             os.path.expanduser("~/.nvme-lab-allowlist"))
    allowed = {l.strip() for l in read(allow_file).splitlines() if l.strip()}
    if serial and serial not in allowed:
        problems.append(f"serial {serial!r} is not in the allowlist {allow_file}")
    # Any block device of this controller (namespaces, partitions, multipath names).
    ctrl_num = ctrl[4:]
    pat = re.compile(rf"/dev/nvme{ctrl_num}n\d+(p\d+)?\b|/dev/nvme\d+c{ctrl_num}n\d+")
    for line in read(mounts).splitlines():
        parts = line.split()
        if parts and pat.search(parts[0]):
            problems.append(f"mounted: {parts[0]} on {parts[1]}")
            if parts[1] in ("/", "/boot", "/boot/efi", "/home"):
                problems.append("this is a system disk")
    for line in read(swaps).splitlines()[1:]:
        if pat.search(line):
            problems.append("used as swap: " + line.split()[0])
    state = read(os.path.join(sysroot, "class", "nvme", ctrl, "state"))
    if state and state != "live":
        problems.append(f"controller state is {state!r}, not 'live'")
    return problems, serial


def selftest():
    with tempfile.TemporaryDirectory() as root:
        os.makedirs(f"{root}/sys/class/nvme/nvme1")
        os.makedirs(f"{root}/sys/block/nvme1n1")
        with open(f"{root}/sys/class/nvme/nvme1/serial", "w") as f:
            f.write("LAB0000000001\n")
        with open(f"{root}/sys/class/nvme/nvme1/state", "w") as f:
            f.write("live\n")
        os.symlink("../../class/nvme/nvme1", f"{root}/sys/block/nvme1n1/device")
        with open(f"{root}/allow", "w") as f:
            f.write("LAB0000000001\n")
        with open(f"{root}/swaps", "w") as f:
            f.write("Filename Type Size Used Priority\n")
        with open(f"{root}/mounts_ok", "w") as f:
            f.write("/dev/sda2 / ext4 rw 0 0\n")
        with open(f"{root}/mounts_bad", "w") as f:
            f.write("/dev/sda2 / ext4 rw 0 0\n/dev/nvme1n1p1 /mnt/data xfs rw 0 0\n")
        for label, mounts in (("unmounted spare", "mounts_ok"), ("mounted spare", "mounts_bad")):
            p, s = check("/dev/nvme1n1", f"{root}/sys", f"{root}/{mounts}",
                         f"{root}/swaps", f"{root}/allow")
            print(f"[{label}] serial={s}: {'GO' if not p else 'NO-GO: ' + '; '.join(p)}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    if len(sys.argv) < 2:
        sys.exit(__doc__)
    if sys.argv[1] == "--selftest":
        selftest()
        sys.exit(0)
    problems, serial = check(sys.argv[1])
    if problems:
        print("NO-GO for", sys.argv[1], f"(serial {serial}):")
        for p in problems:
            print("  -", p)
        sys.exit(1)
    print(f"GO: {sys.argv[1]} is allowlisted spare {serial}, not mounted, not swap, live.")

Expected --selftest output: [unmounted spare] serial=LAB0000000001: GO and [mounted spare] serial=LAB0000000001: NO-GO: mounted: /dev/nvme1n1p1 on /mnt/data. The guard is a seatbelt, not a substitute for reading the command you are about to run.

SPDK where useful

The Storage Performance Development Kit (SPDK) provides a user-space, polled-mode NVMe driver. It is useful in this course for two things: seeing the NVMe initialization and queue mechanics implemented in readable user-space C, and measuring a drive without the kernel block layer in the way.

  • Binding: SPDK's setup script unbinds NVMe devices from the kernel nvme driver and binds them to vfio-pci (or a UIO driver). The device disappears from /dev; anything mounted from it breaks. That is Caution lab. Restrict the script to your spare by its PCI address (the setup script supports allow-listing devices; check its help for the variable name in your version) and verify with the guard script first.
  • Identify and perf tools: SPDK's example applications include an identify tool (comparable to nvme id-ctrl) and a perf tool (a high-rate I/O generator). Read workloads are CAUTION on a spare; write workloads are EXPERT.
  • Hugepages and IOMMU: SPDK needs hugepages and, with vfio-pci, a working IOMMU group for the device (see iommu_group in Chapter 69).
  • Return to the kernel: the setup script's reset mode rebinds devices to nvme. Check with lspci -k.

Run SPDK inside the QEMU guest first. The emulated controller is a perfect target: you learn the bind/unbind procedure and SPDK's tools with zero risk, and you can compare QEMU's pci_nvme_* trace of SPDK's initialization with the Linux driver's.

PCIe analyzer bench, if available

A protocol analyzer is the only instrument that shows what actually crossed the link. If you have access to one, set up a bench that is repeatable and safe for the equipment, the drives and you. Tool-specific procedures are in Teledyne LeCroy Analyzer Training; this section is the vendor-neutral setup.

Bench rules

  • Power off before inserting or removing an interposer, drive or cable unless the slot and the equipment are explicitly designed for hot-plug. Use ESD protection. Caution lab
  • Expect the interposer to change the link. It adds channel loss and may cause the link to train at a lower speed or width, or to show errors that do not occur without it. Always record LnkSta with and without the interposer (Chapter 69). Equipment-dependent
  • Match the analyzer to the link: generation, width, clocking mode (common clock vs separate reference clocks with spread spectrum), and, for PCIe 6.0 and later, flit mode support.
  • Triggers and buffers: plan the trigger (an LTSSM transition, an AER Message, a specific doorbell write, a TLP to a given address) and the pre/post-trigger buffer split before the run; a full buffer of idle traffic teaches nothing.
  • Time correlation: generate a marker visible in both worlds, for example a distinctive admin command with a known CID, or a config read of a specific register via setpci, and record its kernel tracepoint timestamp.
  • Passive capture is CAUTION; injection is EXPERT. Jammers and exercisers that corrupt, drop or inject packets are error-injection tools: spare drives only, evidence capture running, never with a production host.
  • Record everything: equipment, firmware/software versions, interposer model, cable lengths, slot, BIOS settings, kernel version, workload, capture file names. A capture without context is a puzzle without the picture on the box.

Lab 1: build and verify the QEMU NVMe lab

Objective

Create a reproducible VM with an emulated NVMe subsystem (two namespaces, one with 4 KiB LBAs) behind an emulated root port, with tracing working on both sides.

Skills

QEMU command lines, guest verification with lspci/nvme-cli, QEMU device-side tracing, kernel tracepoints.

Safety

Safe lab Image files only. Do not add any vfio-pci device.

Hardware required

A Linux host with virtualization extensions and 8 GB of RAM or more.

Software required

QEMU, a Linux guest image; in the guest: pciutils, nvme-cli, trace-cmd.

Input

The qemu-nvme-lab.sh command line from this page.

Expected output

In the guest: one NVMe controller of class 0108 behind a root port; nvme list showing /dev/nvme0n1 (512-byte LBAs) and /dev/nvme0n2 (4096-byte LBAs); list-subsys showing the configured NQN; NVMe tracepoints producing events; a qemu-nvme-trace.log on the host with pci_nvme_ events.

Background

QEMU section of this page; Chapter 70.

Step 1

Create the images and start the VM. If QEMU rejects a property, consult -device nvme,help and simplify.

Step 2

lspci -nn -tv; sudo nvme list -v; sudo nvme list-subsys
sudo nvme id-ns /dev/nvme0n2 -H | grep -i 'in use'
sudo trace-cmd record -e nvme -- dd if=/dev/nvme0n1 of=/dev/null bs=64k count=16 iflag=direct
trace-cmd report | head -20

Step 3

On the host, look at the end of qemu-nvme-trace.log and find the events for the same reads. Match one guest CID to the QEMU log.

Observation

Note the guest's BDF for the controller, its vendor/device IDs, the LBA formats, and how QEMU names its trace events.

Questions

  1. Why does the controller appear behind a root port with q35 but not necessarily with the older default machine type?
  2. What does /sys/block/nvme0n2/queue/logical_block_size report, and why?
  3. Which side's timestamps would you trust for "device processing time" in this setup?

Challenge

Add a second controller to the same subsystem (another -device nvme with the same subsys) and explain the resulting nvme list -v naming.

Hints

With native multipath, namespaces are named after the subsystem; controllers get their own numbers. Shared namespaces need the appropriate nvme-ns sharing property in recent QEMU (check -device nvme-ns,help).

Solution

1: q35 emulates a PCI Express chipset where devices are placed behind root ports; the older i440FX type emulates conventional PCI with devices on bus 0. 2: 4096, from the LBA Format in use for namespace 2 (LBADS = 12). 3: neither precisely; the QEMU log measures the emulator's processing on the host, the guest tracepoints include virtualization overhead; both are useful for ordering, not for real device latency. Challenge: two controllers (nvme0, nvme1) appear under one subsystem; a shared namespace appears once as a multipath block device named after the subsystem, with per-path hidden nodes.

What a senior engineer notices

That the lab is scripted and versioned (the QEMU command line lives in a file under version control), that tool versions are recorded, and that the emulated controller's MDTS, queue count and features are noted so nobody later mistakes emulator behaviour for drive behaviour.

Failure variants

  • /dev/kvm missing: enable virtualization in firmware, or run without KVM (slow but works).
  • No nvme0n2: your QEMU predates nvme-ns; use one namespace per controller.
  • No QEMU trace output: the binary was built without the trace backend; try -trace help.

Extension

Script the whole lab (images, command line, guest provisioning) so it can be rebuilt from nothing in minutes; you will rebuild it after every EXPERT lab.

The machine after a clean boot.

Expected output

Step 1

Questions

  1. Why capture a baseline before anything goes wrong?
  2. Which baseline files are live register reads and which are kernel bookkeeping?
  3. What tool versions must be recorded alongside the files, and why?

Challenge

Write a script that diffs a new capture against the baseline and prints only meaningful changes (link speed/width, firmware revision, AER counts, SMART critical fields).

Hints

Compare JSON fields by name, not text lines; normalize lspci output to the LnkSta, DevCtl and AER lines.

  • JSON output not supported by an old nvme-cli: capture text and the version.

Lab 3: an EXPERT operation, done the professional way (emulated)

Objective

Reformat an emulated namespace to a different LBA format following the full EXPERT procedure, and observe every layer's reaction.

Skills

Pre-flight checklist, guard script, effects log, Format NVM, rescanning, evidence capture.

Safety

Expert lab procedure performed on an emulated controller in the Lab 1 VM. The image file is the only thing destroyed. Do not repeat on real hardware except on an allowlisted spare drive, and never on production drives.

Hardware required

The Lab 1 VM.

Software required

nvme-cli, trace-cmd, labguard.py (inside the guest).

Input

Namespace 1 of the emulated controller (512-byte LBAs).

Expected output

Namespace 1 reports a 4096-byte LBA format in use; the trace shows a Format NVM admin command (opcode 80h) followed by the driver rescanning; the kernel log shows the capacity/format change; the guard script output is saved.

Background

Chapter 30 (admin commands), Chapter 58 (namespaces), Chapter 70.

Step 1: pre-flight

cat /sys/class/nvme/nvme0/serial                    # LAB0000000001 (the QEMU serial you set)
echo LAB0000000001 > ~/.nvme-lab-allowlist
python3 labguard.py /dev/nvme0n1
sudo nvme effects-log /dev/nvme0 -H | grep -i -A3 'format'
sudo nvme id-ctrl /dev/nvme0 -H | grep -i -A4 '^fna'
sudo nvme id-ns /dev/nvme0n1 -H | grep -i lbaf

Read FNA: does a format of one namespace affect all namespaces on this controller? Read the LBA formats: which index is 4096 bytes with no metadata?

Step 2: format with evidence capture

# EMULATED DEVICE ONLY. Replace N with the 4096-byte LBA format index from step 1.
python3 labguard.py /dev/nvme0n1 && \
sudo trace-cmd record -e nvme -- nvme format /dev/nvme0n1 --lbaf=N
sudo dmesg | tail -10
trace-cmd report | grep -E 'qid=0' | tail -20

Check the option spelling with nvme help format; nvme-cli asks for confirmation on destructive operations unless forced, which is one more chance to read the target.

Step 3: verify

sudo nvme id-ns /dev/nvme0n1 -H | grep -i 'in use'
cat /sys/block/nvme0n1/queue/logical_block_size /sys/block/nvme0n1/size

Observation

Record the Format NVM command in the trace, the admin commands that followed it, and the new block size and sector count.

Questions

  1. Which Identify fields told you the blast radius of the format before you ran it?
  2. Which commands did the driver issue after the format, and why?
  3. Why does /sys/block/nvme0n1/size stay roughly the same in bytes while the LBA count changes?
  4. What would you have to add to this procedure on a real spare drive?

Challenge

Format back to 512-byte LBAs and confirm that the data written before the first format is gone (read a block you wrote earlier).

Hints

FNA in Identify Controller; the Commands Supported and Effects entry for Format NVM; the driver rescans namespaces when a command's effects say the namespace inventory or format may have changed.

Solution

1: FNA (whether format applies to all namespaces, whether secure erase is per namespace) and the effects log entry for Format NVM (logical block content change, namespace capability change). 2: typically Identify Namespace (and related Identify calls) to re-read the format, after the driver froze I/O around the command; the exact set is kernel-dependent. 3: the namespace's capacity in bytes is fixed by the backing image, so the number of LBAs is divided by 8 when the LBA size is multiplied by 8; size is in 512-byte units. 4: the full spare-drive policy: physical label check, serial confirmation by a second person or checklist, unmount, guard script, acceptance that the drive may be lost, vendor guidance on supported formats, and patience: real formats can take from seconds to many minutes.

What a senior engineer notices

That the procedure produced evidence (guard output, effects log, trace, kernel log) before and after the destructive step, and that the operator had to type the target three times. Procedure is what makes EXPERT labs boring, and boring is the goal.

Failure variants

  • Format rejected with Invalid Format: the chosen LBA format index is not supported.
  • Guard says NO-GO: something is mounted or the serial is not allowlisted. Fix the cause, do not bypass the guard.
  • The kernel does not pick up the new format: rescan (nvme ns-rescan, CAUTION) or check kernel messages.

Extension

Repeat with nvme sanitize on the emulated controller (if your QEMU supports it) and compare the procedure, the command's duration and the log page used to monitor progress.

Quiz

  1. Which tier is nvme get-feature /dev/nvme0 -f 0x07 -H on a production server?

    • SAFE
    • CAUTION
    • EXPERT
    Answer and reasoning

    Get Features reads a setting (Number of Queues); it changes nothing. Still deliberate and minimal on production.

  2. Which tier is nvme reset /dev/nvme1 on an allowlisted, unmounted spare drive?

    • SAFE
    • CAUTION
    • EXPERT
    Answer and reasoning

    A controller reset aborts in-flight I/O and changes state, but is recoverable and does not destroy data.

  3. A VM uses -device vfio-pci,host=0000:01:00.0. Inside it you run nvme format. What is destroyed?

    • Nothing, it is a VM
    • A QEMU image file
    • The data on the physical drive at 01:00.0
    • The host's boot disk
    Answer and reasoning

    Passthrough gives the guest the real device; the command reaches real hardware.

  4. Why identify targets by serial number rather than device name?

    Model answer

    Device names (nvme0, nvme0n1) are assigned at enumeration and can change across reboots, hot-plug and multipath; namespace names follow subsystem instances. The serial number is a property of the drive.

  5. Which of these can a QEMU emulated NVMe controller teach well?

    • Gen4 link equalization
    • The Linux driver's reset and timeout handling, and nvme-cli procedures
    • NAND read latency
    • AER correctable error rates
    Answer and reasoning

    The guest runs the real driver against a specification-based model; physical link and media behaviour are not emulated.

  6. Why should an analyzer bench always record LnkSta with and without the interposer?

    Model answer

    The interposer adds channel loss and can change training (speed/width) or introduce errors; without the comparison you could attribute interposer effects to the device.

  7. Which specification structure lets a controller declare which commands change data or namespace inventory?

    • Identify Namespace
    • SMART / Health log
    • Firmware Slot log
    • Commands Supported and Effects log
    Answer and reasoning

    Log 05h, Commands Supported and Effects, is the specification's per-command effects declaration.

  8. Name three rules of the spare-drive policy and the failure each prevents.

    Model answer

    Never the boot disk (prevents destroying the OS); allowlisted serials checked before each command (prevents wrong-device formats after renaming); unmounted and not in a pool (prevents corrupting live filesystems); separate reference and abuse drives (prevents confusing results from a damaged drive); firmware only from vendor images (prevents bricking with wrong images).
