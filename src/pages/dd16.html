Chapter 15 taught you what a Data Link Layer Packet (DLLP) is and how Ack/Nak replay repairs a damaged packet. This module goes underneath that: the exact state each port keeps, the timers that decide when an Ack is late, the arithmetic that decides whether a link is fast or merely error-free, and the forensic skill of telling a sick channel from a sick protocol engine from a sick analyzer.

Orientation

This module deepens one mechanism: the PCI Express (PCIe) Data Link Layer's reliability loop — sequence numbers, the 32-bit Link Cyclic Redundancy Check (LCRC), the replay buffer, Ack and Nak DLLPs, the replay timer, the AckNak latency timer and the 2-bit REPLAY_NUM counter. You already know the four receiver cases (good, bad LCRC, duplicate, gap) and the DLLP type table. Here you will learn to reason about the loop as a control system with latencies and capacities, to read it in traces that look nothing like the textbook examples, and to debug it when the evidence is partial, one-sided or contradictory.

Before you start, be fluent with Chapter 15 — DLLP (DLLP format and types, the receiver decision table, REPLAY_NUM rollover, the Data Link Control and Management State Machine). This module also leans on Chapter 7 — PCIe Layer Model, the Recovery state from Chapter 8 — PCIe LTSSM, and the Advanced Error Reporting (AER) bit map from Chapter 46 — AER. The wider Data Link Layer picture is in Module 15 — PCIe Data Link Layer.

Where it goes next: the flow-control DLLPs that share the link with Acks are the subject of Module 17 — PCIe Flow Control; the error bits that replay leaves behind are read in depth in Module 18 — AER; and the electrical causes of replays are in Chapter 54 — Equalization and Module 07 — PCIe Physical Layer.

Kid story

Mia and Leo are building a long domino line across two rooms, and the doorway between the rooms is narrow. Mia stands on one side and slides dominoes through the doorway one at a time. Each domino has a number painted on it. Before she slides one, she puts a sticky-note copy of the number on her own wall.

Leo, on the other side, stands every domino up in order. Every so often he knocks on the door: one knock and a shout of "up to 41!" means he has stood up every domino through number 41, so Mia peels those sticky notes off her wall. If a domino comes through chipped, Leo throws it in the bin and shouts "only up to 40 — again!" Mia then slides copies of everything from 41 onwards, in order, even the ones that were fine, because Leo threw away everything after the chipped one.

Mia's wall only has room for twenty sticky notes. If Leo is slow to shout, her wall fills up and she has to stop sliding, even though nothing is broken. And if she slides the same batch four times and Leo never says anything new, she stops and asks everyone to check the doorway, because something is wrong with the door, not with the dominoes.

Analogy boundary

What the analogy represents. The numbered dominoes are TLPs with 12-bit sequence numbers. The sticky notes are replay-buffer entries. Leo's "up to 41" is a cumulative Ack DLLP; "only up to 40 — again" is a Nak DLLP whose AckNak_Seq_Num is 40. The full wall is a replay buffer that stalls the transmitter. Sliding the same batch four times without progress is REPLAY_NUM rolling over, and "check the doorway" is the Data Link Layer directing the Physical Layer into Recovery.

What it hides. (1) Leo's shouts can themselves be garbled; a bad DLLP is silently dropped and Mia relies on a timer — the replay timer — rather than on any confirmation of the confirmation. (2) Mia has a stopwatch: if no shout arrives in time she replays anyway. (3) Leo is also sliding dominoes the other way through the same doorway, and both conversations are interleaved. (4) Leo is not the final destination; in a real system the domino might be handed on to another room, and the next doorway has its own numbering. (5) Leo also tells Mia how much floor space he has left — flow control — which is a separate rule that can stop Mia even with an empty wall.

Where it misleads. The story suggests Leo inspects each domino and decides to throw it away; in hardware the LCRC check is a pure arithmetic test with no judgement, and a TLP that fails it is never partly used. It also suggests the replay is slow and deliberate; in a real port a replay starts within a few hundred nanoseconds. Most importantly, it suggests that a chipped domino is the domino's fault. In PCIe, a Nak almost never means the transmitter built a bad packet; it means the channel between two ports corrupted it.

Architecture

Think of each end of the link as two independent half-machines: a transmit side that owns sequence numbers and the replay buffer, and a receive side that owns the expected sequence number and decides between Ack and Nak. The two directions of a link are two completely independent reliability loops that happen to share wires and DLLP slots.

Mechanism

We follow one TLP from the Lab SSD to the Root Port — a 256-byte posted Memory Write (MWr) of NVMe read data — through a corruption and a replay. Every step names who acts, what changes, where it changes, and what evidence exists.

  1. The Transaction Layer hands down a TLP. Who: the SSD's Transaction Layer, after its flow-control check passed. What changes: nothing yet in the Data Link Layer. Where: the internal interface between layers. Evidence: none outside the chip.
  2. The transmitter numbers, seals and stores it. Who: the SSD's transmit Data Link Layer. What changes: the TLP receives sequence number NEXT_TRANSMIT_SEQ (say 0x2B7), NEXT_TRANSMIT_SEQ becomes 0x2B8, the LCRC is computed, a copy enters the replay buffer, and the replay timer starts if it was idle. Where: SSD replay RAM and counters. Evidence: an analyzer shows the TLP with Seq=0x2B7; nothing in software.
  3. The channel corrupts some bits. Who: nobody — physics. What changes: some symbols on one or more lanes. Where: between the SSD's transmitter and the Root Port's receiver. Evidence: possibly a Receiver Error at the Root Port (framing or block-level), and an LCRC mismatch.
  4. The receiver rejects it. Who: the Root Port's receive Data Link Layer. What changes: TLP discarded; Bad TLP status bit set; NAK_SCHEDULED set; a Nak with AckNak_Seq_Num = NEXT_RCV_SEQ − 1 = 0x2B6 queued at high priority. Where: Root Port counters and AER Correctable Error Status. Evidence: AER bit 6 at the Root Port; Nak DLLP on the wire.
  5. Later TLPs arrive and are discarded. Who: Root Port receiver. What changes: TLPs 0x2B8, 0x2B9 … are good on the wire but later than expected; they are discarded; no second Nak because NAK_SCHEDULED is set. Where: receive pipeline. Evidence: analyzer shows clean TLPs that never cause an Ack.
  6. The Nak reaches the transmitter. Who: SSD's DLLP receiver. What changes: the 16-bit CRC passes; entries up to 0x2B6 are purged (that part is forward progress, so REPLAY_NUM resets to 0 first); then a replay starts and REPLAY_NUM becomes 1; new TLPs from the Transaction Layer are blocked until the replay has been sent. Where: SSD replay logic. Evidence: on the wire, the next SSD TLPs carry already-used sequence numbers.
  7. The replay is sent. Who: SSD transmitter. What changes: every buffered TLP from 0x2B7 onwards is re-sent unchanged, in order; the replay timer restarts. Where: link. Evidence: duplicates of the original bytes; analyzers often label them "replayed".
  8. The receiver accepts and acknowledges. Who: Root Port receiver. What changes: 0x2B7 matches NEXT_RCV_SEQ, is passed up, NAK_SCHEDULED clears; subsequent TLPs are accepted; an Ack is sent before the AckNak latency timer expires. Where: Root Port Transaction Layer receives the MWr — exactly once. Evidence: Ack DLLP with a sequence number at or above 0x2B7.
  9. The transmitter retires the copies. Who: SSD transmitter. What changes: replay buffer purged; ACKD_SEQ advanced; REPLAY_NUM reset to 0; replay timer stopped if the buffer is empty. Where: SSD replay logic. Evidence: none beyond the Ack itself. Software at most sees a correctable error message from the Root Port.

The timers, quantitatively

The two timers are the part of the mechanism that Chapter 15 only named. They are coupled: the receiver promises to Ack within its AckNak latency limit, and the transmitter waits a multiple of that before it gives up and replays. If the receiver keeps its promise and no DLLP is lost, the replay timer never fires.

In the original revisions of the PCI Express Base Specification, both limits were written as a formula built from the same ingredients: the maximum payload size plus a fixed TLP overhead, multiplied by an "Ack factor" and divided by the link width, plus an internal-delay allowance — with the replay timer set to roughly three times the Ack latency limit, plus adjustments for L0s exit. Later revisions present the values as tables per data rate and width, with a stated tolerance. Uncertain The exact constants, the multiplier and the treatment at 16.0 GT/s and above differ by revision; read the replay-timer and AckNak-latency material in the Data Link Layer chapter of the revision you work with, and never hard-code a value from a blog post.

Treplay ≈ k × Tacklimit + Tadj
Treplay
the transmitter's replay-timer limit
Tacklimit
the receiver's AckNak latency limit for the same speed, width and maximum payload size
k

Tadj

Derived The practical consequence is a margin rule you can check in a capture: measured Ack latency should sit well below the peer's replay limit. A port whose Ack latency drifts toward the limit — because it coalesces too aggressively, or because it is stuck in a power-management transition — will cause spurious Replay Timer Timeouts at its peer even on a perfect channel. That is the "slow" trace later in this module.

Throughput bounds the Data Link Layer imposes

Two bounds come out of the mechanism. Neither produces an error when it binds.

BWDLL ≤ Breplay ÷ TackRT
BWDLL
maximum one-direction throughput (bytes per second of link time, including TLP overhead) the replay buffer allows
Breplay
replay-buffer capacity in bytes (including sequence field, header, payload and LCRC of each stored TLP)
TackRT
time from a TLP's first byte leaving the transmitter until the Ack that retires it has been processed: serialisation + flight + receiver pipeline + Ack coalescing delay + DLLP flight + processing
ηreplay ≈ 1 − r × Nreplayed
ηreplay
fraction of link time carrying new (not replayed) TLPs

Naks (or timer-driven replays) per transmitted TLP
Nreplayed
average number of TLPs re-sent per replay — roughly the number of TLPs in flight during one Nak round trip, plus one

Derived Example on the lab link: 256-byte MWrs occupy about 280 bytes of link time each (≈ 35.5 ns at ≈ 7.88 GB/s). With a 400 ns Nak round trip, each replay re-sends about 12 TLPs. A Nak rate of one per 1,000 TLPs costs about 1.2 % of bandwidth; one per 100 TLPs costs about 12 % — and adds a ~0.4 µs bump to the tail latency of every command whose data was caught in a replay. Both are "only correctable errors".

The ladder for the replay loop

Number every domino, keep a sticky note until your friend shouts "up to here", resend from the first chipped one, and if four resends get no new shout, check the doorway.

Each port's Data Link Layer numbers TLPs, protects them with an LCRC, keeps copies until a cumulative Ack, replays on Nak or timeout, and asks for link retraining after four replays without progress.

Per direction per port: a replay SRAM sized for bandwidth × Ack round trip, a CRC-32 engine on the TLP path, a CRC-16 engine on the DLLP path, 12-bit counters, a 2-bit REPLAY_NUM, two timers scaled by negotiated speed and width, and wires into AER status bits.

PCI Express Base Specification, Data Link Layer chapter: sequence-number and LCRC rules, receiver and transmitter rules, Ack/Nak DLLP formats, replay and AckNak latency timer requirements, REPLAY_NUM rollover → retrain, Data Link Protocol Error conditions, and the error categories reported through AER. Replay-buffer size and Ack coalescing policy are implementation choices.

On the wire: TLPs carrying Seq fields in one direction, Ack/Nak DLLPs with AckNak_Seq_Num in the other, replayed TLPs with repeated sequence numbers, and — on rollover — TS1/TS2 ordered sets of Recovery.

In an analyzer: Ack latency per TLP, Nak positions, replay bursts, gaps where the transmitter stalls with a full replay buffer, Recovery entries, and possibly speed changes after Recovery.

Read AER on both ends, find which direction is failing (receiver-side bits vs transmitter-side bits), correlate with speed, slot, temperature and power-state transitions, and reserve "protocol-engine bug" for evidence a noisy channel cannot produce: impossible sequence numbers, replays with altered contents, or Acks that violate the latency rule on a clean link.

The sixteen questions, for the replay timer

Chapter 15 answered the sixteen questions for the Ack/Nak mechanism as a whole. Here they are for the piece that causes the most confusion in the field: the replay timer.

The replay timer through the sixteen questions
#QuestionAnswer
1What is it?A transmitter-side timer that forces a replay of all unacknowledged TLPs when no forward-progress Ack or Nak arrives in time.
2Why was it created?Acks and Naks are DLLPs; DLLPs are never retransmitted, so a lost Nak (or a TLP lost so badly the receiver never saw it) would otherwise leave the transmitter waiting forever.
3What problem does it solve?Guarantees liveness: every TLP is eventually delivered or the link is retrained.
4What existed before it?Conventional PCI had no link-level retransmission; a parity error was reported (PERR#/SERR#) and the transaction was not automatically repeated by the bus.
5Why is the current design better?It recovers from lost acknowledgements in hardware, in microseconds, with no software involvement, while escalating persistent failure through REPLAY_NUM.
6Who creates it?The transmitting port's Data Link Layer.
7Who consumes it?The same port's replay logic; its expiry is reported to AER as Replay Timer Timeout.
8Where does it live?In every transmitting port: Root Ports, switch ports, endpoint upstream ports.
9What memory/registers are involved?The replay buffer; REPLAY_NUM; AER Correctable Error Status bit 12 (Replay Timer Timeout) and bit 8 (REPLAY_NUM Rollover) at the transmitting port.
10What happens before it?A TLP is transmitted while the timer is idle (start), or a forward-progress Ack/Nak arrives (restart).
11What happens after it?On expiry: replay, REPLAY_NUM increment, possible rollover and Recovery. On progress: restart or stop.
12What happens if it is wrong?Too short (or a peer that Acks too slowly): spurious replays that waste bandwidth and set Replay Timer Timeout on a perfect channel. Too long: slow recovery from lost Naks, long tail latency.

16What would a senior engineer suspect when it fails?Lost DLLPs in the opposite direction (signal integrity of the other lane direction) if Bad DLLP is also logged at the same port; a slow-Acking peer (power management, coalescing bug) if it is not.

Specification discipline

Applicable specification. PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 (PCI-SIG; a members' document) — the Data Link Layer Specification chapter for the mechanism, the Error Signaling and Logging material for how its errors are classified, and the Advanced Error Reporting Extended Capability for the registers. PCI Express Base Specification — Data Link Layer Specification PCI Express Base Specification — Advanced Error Reporting Extended Capability. No NVM Express specification defines anything in this module; the NVMe over PCIe Transport Specification 1.4 assumes a reliable PCIe link and stops there.

Revision. Everything below the Flit Mode paragraph describes non-flit ("classic") operation, which every generation through 5.0 uses and which 6.x/7.0 links use when they do not operate in Flit Mode.

Normative 12-bit TLP sequence numbers; 32-bit LCRC over the sequence field and TLP; Ack (00h) and Nak (10h) DLLPs with a 12-bit AckNak_Seq_Num; cumulative acknowledgement; replay of all unacknowledged TLPs in original order on Nak or replay-timer expiry; REPLAY_NUM as a 2-bit counter whose rollover directs the Physical Layer to retrain; Bad TLP, Bad DLLP, Replay Timer Timeout and REPLAY_NUM Rollover reported as correctable errors; Data Link Protocol Error and Surprise Down as uncorrectable errors; DLLPs with a bad CRC discarded and not retransmitted.

Informative The specification's figures and implementation notes (for example on replay-buffer sizing and Ack scheduling) are explanatory. The throughput bounds, the replay-cost formula and the "Ack latency must sit well below the peer's replay limit" margin rule on this page are reasoned from the normative rules, not quoted.

Optional / Implementation-dependent Replay-buffer size; Ack coalescing policy within the latency limit; the use of nullification (mostly cut-through switches); DLLP scheduling among equal-priority choices; vendor counters of Naks or replays exposed through vendor-specific capabilities; whether a port ever implements the optional AER mask settings differently from defaults.

Version-dependent The presentation of the replay and AckNak latency limits (formula vs tables, tolerances, values at 16.0 GT/s and higher); additional DLLP types added over time (for example a Data Link Feature DLLP and a NOP DLLP); Flit Mode in 6.0 and later, mandatory at 64.0 GT/s. In Flit Mode, TLP bytes and Data Link Layer payload bytes share fixed 256-byte flits protected by a flit-level CRC and lightweight forward error correction, acknowledgement and retry operate on flits, and the per-TLP 12-bit sequence number and LCRC are not used. Commonly described details — such as a selective-Nak option that replays only the failed flit, and the byte budget of a flit — must be confirmed in the Flit Mode sections of the revision you use. Uncertain

The specification does not say how large a replay buffer must be for full throughput, how many Naks per second are acceptable, what correctable-error rate means "healthy", how analyzers should label replays, or which side of a link software should read first. It does not require a port to count replays. It does not define any NVMe-visible consequence of a replay. Check in your revision: the exact priority order between Nak, Ack, flow-control and other DLLPs and between replayed and new TLPs; the replay-timer hold and reset conditions during Recovery and L0s; the precise list of conditions that constitute a Data Link Protocol Error.

Never assume: that a correctable-error count of zero means no replays (errors may be masked, counted on the other port, or handled firmware-first); that the Ack you see came from the final destination; that a replayed TLP can legally differ from the original; that a Nak implicates the transmitter's silicon; that Flit Mode counters mean the same as classic-mode bits; or that a timer value quoted for one speed applies at another.

Two hardware facts dominate real debugging. First, the replay RAM in an SSD's PCIe core is sized by the IP integrator for the fastest speed and widest width they validated, often with assumptions about Ack latency taken from a reference Root Port. Put that SSD behind a switch and a retimer and the Ack round trip grows; if the buffer was sized tightly, throughput drops with zero errors. Second, the receive side checks the LCRC only after the whole TLP has arrived, so large TLPs carry more bits and are more likely to fail on a marginal channel: a channel with a bit error rate p corrupts a 280-byte TLP roughly 280 × 8 × p of the time but a 20-byte doorbell TLP fourteen times less often. Derived That is why replays cluster on data-carrying TLPs and why a "doorbells work, data transfers are slow" pattern does not by itself exonerate the link.

A retimer is transparent to the replay loop but not to its timing. Each retimer adds pipeline latency in both directions, so each one lengthens the Ack round trip and consumes margin between the peer's Ack latency and the replay timer. Retimers also participate in equalization, so a retimer with a poor equalization result produces Naks that look exactly like a bad slot. When a capture is taken on the endpoint side of a retimer, the Acks you see were generated by the Root Port beyond it.

Two protocol relationships are worth stating precisely because they generate most misreadings:

  • Link acknowledgement versus transaction completion. The Root Port's Ack for the SSD's 64-byte Memory Read (MRd) of a Submission Queue Entry (SQE) means only that the MRd crossed the link. The Completion with Data (CplD) that carries the SQE arrives later as a separate TLP with its own sequence number in the opposite direction, and the SSD acknowledges that. Four Data Link Layer events (TLP, Ack, TLP, Ack) frame one Transaction Layer exchange (request, completion).
  • Credits versus replay. A TLP consumes flow-control credits once, when the Transaction Layer first passes it down. A replay re-sends the stored copy and consumes no new credits; the receiver did not keep the discarded copies, so it has not consumed buffer space for them either. Derived This is why replay and flow control can be analysed independently — and why a transmitter can be idle for two unrelated reasons that look identical on the wire (see the ambiguous trace in Module 17).

Priority between DLLPs and TLPs matters for latency. In broad terms, a transmitter finishes the packet already in progress, then favours a pending Nak, then Acks whose deadline is near and flow-control updates that are required, then replayed TLPs, then new TLPs, then other DLLPs. Uncertain The exact ordering and its wording vary by revision; it is tabulated in the Data Link Layer chapter. What matters for analysis is the consequence: a long stream of large TLPs must not starve the Acks travelling in the same direction, so an Ack that appears only after a long TLP burst is a scheduling observation worth noting, not automatically a violation.
