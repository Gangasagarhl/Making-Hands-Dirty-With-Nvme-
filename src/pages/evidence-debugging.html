A debugging conclusion is only as good as the evidence under it. This page covers the course's evidence rules: confidence labels on every claim, the first-abnormal-event rule, the negative-evidence rule, and specification safety. Four worked cases show the rules applied to Non-Volatile Memory Express (NVMe) over PCI Express (PCIe) failures.

Why evidence discipline

Most wrong root causes are not caused by missing knowledge. They come from three habits:

  1. Stopping at the loudest error. A kernel message says "I/O timeout", so the timeout becomes the cause. It was the last thing to happen, not the first.
  2. Treating absence as proof (ignoring the negative-evidence rule). "There was no interrupt" when the analyzer was filtering interrupts out, or the capture started too late.
  3. Inventing authority. "The spec says the controller must…" when nobody opened the specification, or when the claim mixes a requirement with one vendor's behavior.

Each habit produces a confident, wrong report. Wrong reports are expensive: a "fix" ships, the bug comes back, and the team has lost both the time and the trust in the next report. The rules on this page cost a few extra sentences per report and prevent most of this.

What this page covers

  • How to separate observation, inference, hypothesis and conclusion.
  • The five confidence labels: Confirmed, Strong evidence, Likely, Possible, Unknown.
  • The rule that every root-cause conclusion names its evidence.
  • The first-abnormal-event rule: from symptom back to first deviation, then back to its preceding cause.
  • The negative-evidence rule and the trace-coverage statement that makes absence meaningful.
  • Specification safety: citing documents and revisions, separating normative text from interpretation, never fabricating quotations or section numbers.

This page builds on Debugging Methodology (timelines, hypotheses, elimination) and assumes you can read a synthetic trace (Chapter 39, Chapter 41) and an Advanced Error Reporting (AER) log (Chapter 46).

The story

The cookie jar is empty. Your little brother has crumbs on his shirt, so everyone says he ate the cookies. But the crumbs could be from his own snack. And the jar might have been empty since yesterday, because nobody checked it then. A good detective says: "Crumbs on the shirt: we saw that. He ate the cookies: we're guessing. Was the jar full this morning? We don't know, because nobody looked."

An air-accident investigation board never writes "the pilot crashed the plane." It writes a timeline from the flight recorder, marks the first departure from normal operation, separates findings (facts) from causes (conclusions) and contributing factors, and grades each conclusion. When the recorder stopped early, the report says so, and it does not conclude anything about the missing minutes.

Accident boards have months and a complete wreck. You often have one partial capture and a deadline. And unlike a flight recorder, a protocol analyzer only records what its trigger, filter and buffer allowed. Much of the evidence discipline here is about knowing what your recorder could not have seen. The analogy also suggests one cause per event; NVMe/PCIe failures often have a trigger and an enabling condition at different layers.

Observation, inference, hypothesis, conclusion

Every statement in a debugging report is one of four kinds. Labeling them, even just in your head, is the single most useful habit on this page.

Four kinds of statement
KindDefinitionExample
ObservationSomething recorded by an instrument or read from a register or log, with its source"Analyzer row 412: Host→Dev MWr to 0xFE60_1008, data 0x00000005, t = 10.004 312 s."
InferenceA meaning assigned to observations using known rules"Row 412 is the SQ1 tail doorbell (address = BAR0 + 0x1008, DSTRD = 0)."
HypothesisA proposed explanation that predicts further observations"The controller ignored the doorbell because CSTS.RDY was 0."
ConclusionA hypothesis that has survived tests, stated with a confidence label and its evidence"Likely: doorbell written before ready. Evidence: …"

Inferences are where most errors creep in, because they feel like observations. "Row 412 is a doorbell" depends on the BAR address, the stride, and on the row not being a misdecoded or filtered event. Usually that is safe. But when a conclusion rests on one inference, say so.

Each source has blind spots. A conclusion supported by two independent sources with different blind spots — say, an analyzer row and a driver tracepoint — is much stronger than one supported by two entries from the same source.

Confidence labels

Every root-cause conclusion in this course carries one of five labels. The labels tell the reader how much weight the conclusion can bear: whether to ship a fix, keep investigating, or both.

The five confidence labels
LabelMeaningMinimum evidenceTypical phrasing
ConfirmedThe mechanism has been demonstrated.Reproduced on demand; the fix removes it; reverting the fix brings it back. Or the mechanism is directly visible in complete evidence (for example a capture with stated full coverage showing the oversized TLP and the error it triggered)."Confirmed: …"
Strong evidenceThe mechanism explains every observation, and competing hypotheses have been eliminated by evidence.A disambiguating experiment or direct observation of the mechanism, but no on/off reproduction yet."Strong evidence that …; not yet reproduced."
LikelyThe mechanism explains the observations; at least one plausible alternative remains.Consistent observations; alternatives named but not excluded."Likely …; alternative X not excluded because …"
PossibleConsistent with some observations; little direct support.Plausibility, similarity to past bugs, a vendor hint."Possible: …; no direct evidence yet."
UnknownThe evidence does not favor any hypothesis.—"Unknown. The following evidence would decide it: …"

These map onto the scale in Debugging Methodology: Confirmed = Confirmed, Strong evidence ≈ High, Likely ≈ Medium, Possible ≈ Low. The fifth label, Unknown, is a legitimate conclusion. It is far better than a guess presented as "Low."

Moving up and down

  • Up when a prediction made before looking turns out right ("if this is CID reuse, there will be two outstanding SQEs with CID 0x0042"). Predictions are stronger than explanations made afterwards.
  • Up when an independent source agrees (analyzer and tracepoint).
  • Up to Confirmed only through a controlled on/off experiment, or through direct observation with stated complete coverage.
  • Down when an observation is unexplained. One unexplained observation is enough to block "Strong evidence."
  • Down when the key evidence is negative and coverage was not established (see the negative-evidence rule).
  • Down when a step relies on a specification claim nobody checked.

A senior engineer's hunch is still "Possible" until evidence arrives. Experience changes which hypotheses you think of first and which experiments you run. It does not change the label.

The ladder: one conclusion from story to debugging

A detective doesn't say "he did it" just because someone shouted. She says what she saw, what she thinks it means, and how sure she is.

A root-cause conclusion is a claim with three attached things: the evidence, the confidence label, and what would change it.

Evidence comes from hardware that records only some events: an analyzer on one link, AER registers in each component, counters in the controller. Each has known blind spots.

Normative specification text says what components shall do. A conclusion that a component violated the specification must cite the document, its revision, and the requirement by section name, and must separate the requirement from your interpretation.

At the protocol level, evidence is a set of transactions with direction, type, address, tag or CID, and timing, compared with the expected sequence.

In a trace, the first row that differs from the expected sequence is the first abnormal event, provided the capture covers the time and traffic in which an earlier deviation could have appeared.

The root cause is the preceding condition that produced that first deviation. It is labeled Confirmed only when switching it on and off switches the failure on and off.

Every conclusion names its evidence

A root-cause statement without evidence is an opinion. The course uses one template for every conclusion:

CLAIM:        <mechanism, stated so it could be wrong>
CONFIDENCE:   Confirmed | Strong evidence | Likely | Possible | Unknown
EVIDENCE:     <observations, each with source and location (capture row, log line, register, time)>
COVERAGE:     <what the evidence could and could not have shown>
ALTERNATIVES: <other hypotheses, and why each is excluded or still open>
SPEC BASIS:   <document, revision, section name; normative vs interpretation>   (if applicable)
WOULD CHANGE: <the observation or experiment that would raise or lower confidence>

Claim checklist

Before a report leaves your hands, check every conclusion against this list. Each "no" either gets fixed or lowers the confidence label.

  1. Is the claim falsifiable? Could some observation prove it wrong?
  2. Does each piece of evidence name its source and location?
  3. Are observations separated from inferences?
  4. Is the first abnormal event identified, and is the root cause something that precedes it?
  5. Does any key evidence rely on absence? If so, is coverage stated?
  6. Are alternatives listed with the reason each is excluded or still open?
  7. Is every specification statement cited by document, revision and section name, with interpretation labeled?
  8. Does the label match the evidence (see the table above)?
  9. Is there a regression test that checks the mechanism, not just the symptom?

The first-abnormal-event rule

The rule from the course's mastery method:

The first visible failure is not necessarily the root cause. The root cause may occur earlier. Always investigate backward: from the symptom, to the first deviation, to the preceding cause.

Three distinct things

Symptom
What someone noticed: an application error, a kernel message, a hung benchmark, a failed test. Usually late and usually at a high layer.
First deviation (first abnormal event)
The earliest event in the merged timeline that differs from the expected sequence. Finding it requires an expected timeline to compare against (see the timeline method in Debugging Methodology).
Preceding cause
The condition that produced the first deviation. It may not appear in the trace at all: a configuration value set at boot, a firmware change, a marginal channel, a driver code path. You find it by asking "what had to be true for this deviation to happen?" and checking that.

Procedure

  1. Pin the symptom. Exact time (with clock source), exact message, affected command or device.
  2. State coverage. For each evidence source: start, end, filters, directions, whether timestamps are complete. Without this you cannot say anything is "first."
  3. Build the expected timeline for the operation (T0 state, T1 first event, T2 request, T3 transport, T4 device action, T5 response, T6 completion, T7 software notification).
  4. Align and compare the observed timeline with the expected one, starting from the beginning of coverage, not from the symptom.
  5. Mark the first deviation. If it is at the very start of coverage, say so: the true first deviation may be earlier, before the capture.
  6. Ask what precedes it. List conditions that would produce this deviation. Check each with evidence outside the trace (config dumps, firmware versions, boot logs, change history).
  7. Test. Remove the preceding condition. If the deviation and the symptom disappear, and come back when the condition is restored, the cause is Confirmed.

If the first abnormal row is the first row of the capture, you have not found the first abnormal event. You have found the start of your evidence. Label it "earliest observed deviation" and widen the capture.

The negative-evidence rule

Absence of an event is evidence only if the event was expected, and the capture is known to be complete enough to observe it. Never say "the packet did not happen" unless the trace coverage supports that conclusion. Prefer: "No corresponding packet is visible in the captured interval."

Both conditions matter:

  • Expected. You must be able to say why the event should have occurred, from the specification or from the normal sequence. "No MSI-X after this CQE" is meaningful only if interrupts are enabled for that CQ, the vector is unmasked, and coalescing would not delay it.
  • Observable. The capture must have been able to record it: right link, right direction, inside the time window, not filtered, not dropped by a full buffer, and decoded by the analyzer.

The trace-coverage statement

Every trace used as evidence carries a coverage statement covering six items:

Trace coverage statement
ItemQuestionWhy it matters
Capture startWhen did recording begin, relative to the symptom and to the operation?Earlier deviations may be outside the window.
Capture endWhen did it stop, and why (trigger, buffer full, manual)?Later expected events may be missing because recording stopped.
Timestamps completeAre timestamps continuous? Any gaps, wraps or segment joins?Ordering across a gap is uncertain.
FilteringWere packets filtered (by type, address, requester, DLLPs, ordered sets)?Filtered events are invisible but did happen.
DecodingCould the analyzer's decoding omit or misclassify events (unknown vendor messages, protocol-mode mismatch, lane errors)?An event may be present in raw data but not shown as decoded.
DirectionsWere both directions captured?A one-direction capture cannot show requests or completions from the other side.

The probe point matters too. An analyzer on the SSD's link sees nothing that happens between the root complex and memory, or on other links behind a switch.

Phrasing

Never say / say instead
Never saySay instead
"The device never sent the completion.""No completion with tag 0x2C is visible in the captured interval (both directions, unfiltered, 0–4.2 s)."
"There were no errors on the link.""No NAK DLLPs or replayed sequence numbers are visible in the capture; DLLP capture was enabled. The device's correctable error status was 0 when read at 5.0 s."
"The host didn't ring the doorbell.""No host→device write to BAR0 + 0x1008 is visible between 1.0 and 3.0 s. Host-to-device memory writes were captured unfiltered in that interval."
"The firmware didn't log anything, so nothing happened.""The Error Information log contains no entry with this command's CID; the log holds N entries and may have wrapped."

If your hypothesis depends on something not happening, design the capture to prove absence: disable filters for that traffic class, trigger well before the operation, and include a positive control, such as a known event of the same type that is captured just before. If the analyzer shows earlier MSI-X writes from the same device, a missing later one is far more meaningful.

Code: a coverage-aware deviation finder

The script compares an observed event list with an expected sequence. It reports the first deviation, and it refuses to call a missing event "absent" unless the coverage description says that kind of event could have been seen. It uses only the Python standard library. The events are synthetic.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Find the first deviation from an expected sequence, respecting capture coverage."""

EXPECTED = ["doorbell", "sqe_fetch", "sqe_cpl", "data_mwr", "cqe_mwr", "msix"]

def first_deviation(observed, coverage):
    """observed: list of (time_us, kind). coverage: set of kinds the capture could record,
    plus a (start, end) window. Returns a list of findings in order."""
    start, end = coverage["window"]
    visible = coverage["kinds"]
    findings = []
    pos = 0
    for t, kind in observed:
        if not (start <= t <= end):
            continue
        if pos < len(EXPECTED) and kind == EXPECTED[pos]:
            pos += 1
            continue
        if kind in EXPECTED[pos:]:
            skipped = EXPECTED[pos:EXPECTED.index(kind, pos)]
            for s in skipped:
                findings.append(describe_missing(s, visible))
            pos = EXPECTED.index(kind, pos) + 1
        else:
            findings.append(f"UNEXPECTED at {t} us: {kind}")
    for s in EXPECTED[pos:]:
        findings.append(describe_missing(s, visible))
    return findings

def describe_missing(kind, visible):
    if kind in visible:
        return f"MISSING: no '{kind}' visible in the captured interval (capture could record it)"
    return f"NOT OBSERVABLE: '{kind}' could not be captured (filtered or out of coverage); no conclusion"

if __name__ == "__main__":
    observed = [(0.0, "doorbell"), (0.8, "sqe_fetch"), (1.4, "sqe_cpl"),
                (61.3, "data_mwr"), (63.0, "cqe_mwr")]
    full = {"window": (0.0, 5000.0), "kinds": set(EXPECTED)}
    filtered = {"window": (0.0, 5000.0), "kinds": set(EXPECTED) - {"msix"}}
    print("Full coverage:")
    for f in first_deviation(observed, full):
        print("  ", f)
    print("Interrupt writes filtered:")
    for f in first_deviation(observed, filtered):
        print("  ", f)

With full coverage, the script reports "MISSING: no 'msix' visible in the captured interval": a real finding, though phrased carefully. With interrupt writes filtered, the same observations give "NOT OBSERVABLE … no conclusion." Same events, different evidence, because the coverage differs.

Worked cases

Four cases, each written in the trace-lab format. All traces are synthetic examples for teaching, using the course's lab system: Gen4 x4, MPS 256, BAR0 at 0xFE60_0000, SSD at 01:00.0 (Requester ID 0x0100), root port 00:01.1, IOMMU enabled. Try each case before opening its solution.

Case 1 — "The SSD stopped sending interrupts"

Scenario

A test reports that, about once an hour, one I/O takes exactly 30 seconds and then succeeds after the driver resets the controller. A colleague captured a trace during a failure and wrote: "The device never sent the MSI-X. Device interrupt bug."

Coverage

Capture triggered on the driver's reset (a write to CC), with 2 s of pre-trigger buffer. Both directions. Filter: "exclude MSI-X writes to reduce buffer use" (the analyzer setting excludes host-bound memory writes to 0xFEE0_0000–0xFEEF_FFFF). DLLPs excluded.

#DirTime (s)TypeFieldsMeaning
Capture starts at 28.000 000 s (2 s pre-trigger). The I/O was submitted at about 0.0 s, outside the capture.
1Host→Dev28.104 220MWrAddr=0xFE60_1010 Len=1 Data=0x0000002ASQ2 tail doorbell (another queue)
2Dev→Host28.104 221MRdSQ2 slot 41Normal SQE fetch on SQ2
… normal SQ2 traffic …
40Host→Dev30.000 004MWrAddr=0xFE60_0014 Data=0x00460000CC.EN cleared: driver reset (trigger)
Analysis

Is the colleague's conclusion supported? No, for two separate reasons:

  1. The capture could not show MSI-X writes: they were filtered. "No MSI-X visible" carries no information.
  2. The capture starts 28 s after the I/O was submitted. Whatever happened to that command (its SQE fetch, data, CQE and interrupt) is outside the window. The first abnormal event, if it is in this capture at all, cannot be found here.

What the capture does show (Observation): SQ2 traffic was normal during the last 2 s, so the link and the device's fetch engine were working. The driver reset the controller at 30.000 s.

Correct conclusion: "Unknown. The interrupt for the timed-out command cannot be evaluated: interrupt writes were filtered and the command's lifetime is outside the capture window. Other queues functioned normally in the 2 s before reset (Observation)."

Next capture: trigger on the doorbell of the queue that times out (or use a long segmented buffer), remove the interrupt filter, and keep DLLPs. In parallel, read /proc/interrupts before and after, and log the CQ head and phase in the driver at timeout. Those three sources together can tell "CQE not written", "CQE written, no interrupt" and "interrupt sent, handler did not consume" apart.

Follow-up (synthetic): the second capture shows the CQE written to CQ3 slot 0 with phase 0, then MSI-X vector 3 written 90 ns later, and no CQ3 head doorbell for 30 s. The device did interrupt. The driver's log at timeout shows its expected phase for CQ3 was 1. The first abnormal event is now "host did not consume a valid CQE after wrap" (Strong evidence), and the colleague's device-bug theory is refuted. The preceding cause is in the driver's phase handling — compare the CQ simulator in the learning system.

Case 2 — "Controller is down" after hours of clean I/O

Scenario

After 6–10 hours of load, the kernel logs I/O timeouts on all queues, then the driver reports that CSTS reads as 0xFFFFFFFF and the controller is removed. The root port's AER status shows a Surprise Down error (the link went down unexpectedly). A first report says: "Surprise Down at the root port: bad slot or cable."

Coverage

Analyzer on the SSD's link, both directions, no filters, DLLPs and ordered sets captured, LTSSM state tracking on. Segmented buffer; the segment spans 1.2 s before the link-down to 0.5 s after. Timestamps continuous within the segment.

Analysis

Symptom: I/O timeouts and CSTS = 0xFFFFFFFF (driver log); Surprise Down (root port AER).

First deviation: row 6, the SSD's transmitter going to electrical idle at 1200.000 ms with no orderly idle sequence and without any link errors before it. The capture begins 1.2 s before this, so the deviation is not at the edge of coverage.

Hypotheses for the preceding cause:

  • H1, signal integrity (slot, cable, connector). It predicts rising NAKs and replays, or receiver errors, before link loss. The capture includes DLLPs unfiltered and shows none in 1.2 s. This is negative evidence, valid because DLLPs were captured and positive controls (normal ACKs, row 2) are visible. It argues against H1 but does not exclude an abrupt mechanical fault.
  • H2, the device lost power or reset internally (firmware assert, power-rail fault). It predicts a sudden stop of transmission from the device side with the host side still trying to train. That matches rows 6–7.
  • H3, platform-initiated reset or power removal. It predicts the host side going quiet or sending a hot-reset sequence first. The root port keeps sending TS1 after the event, which argues against it.

Additional evidence: after a power cycle, the SSD's persistent event log (or vendor telemetry) is read. If it shows an internal reset or assert near the failure time, H2 moves to Strong evidence. If the SMART unsafe-shutdown count increased by one, that is consistent with a power loss at the device, but it does not on its own separate a power fault from an internal reset. On NVMe 2.4 devices with voltage monitoring, the device's voltage history could separate "supply dipped" from "firmware fault"; check the Base 2.4 specification for how that information is reported.

Conclusion (before device logs): "Likely: the SSD stopped transmitting abruptly (device-side power loss or internal reset). Evidence: capture rows 6–7; no NAK/replay or receiver errors in the preceding 1.2 s with DLLP capture enabled; root port continued training attempts. Alternative H1 (abrupt mechanical fault) not excluded. The Surprise Down error and the timeouts are consequences, not causes."

Why the first report was wrong: it went from the symptom (Surprise Down at the root port) straight to a cause on the root port's side. Surprise Down says the link went down unexpectedly. It does not say why or which side caused it.

Corrective path and regression test: depends on the confirmed cause. In every case, keep the analyzer configuration (ordered sets, DLLPs, LTSSM tracking) as the standard capture for this failure, and add a soak test with device-log collection after each event.

Case 3 — Silent corruption with success status

Scenario

A database checksum fails on reads after heavy mixed I/O. Every NVMe completion has status 0 (success). No AER errors. The first report says: "Media corruption; replace the drive."

Coverage

Analyzer on the SSD's link, both directions, unfiltered, 0.8 s segment around one failing read identified by the application's offset. Driver tracepoints log each command's CID, PRP1, PRP2 and PRP list contents at submission.

Analysis

Symptom: application checksum failure on data that was read successfully.

First deviation: row 5. The PRP list contents the controller fetched differ from what the driver logged when it built the list. Everything before row 5 matches expectation; everything after it follows faithfully from the wrong list. The controller did exactly what the list said.

Preceding cause: something changed the PRP list page between submission (tracepoint) and fetch (row 4, 2.0 µs after the doorbell). Hypotheses: (a) the list page was freed and reused by another command before this command completed; (b) two commands were given the same list page; (c) a stray CPU write. A prediction for (a) or (b): another command's SQE in the same capture carries PRP2 = 0x1_5000_3000. Searching the capture finds CID 0x0188 on SQ2, submitted 1.1 µs earlier, with that PRP2 and a list containing 0x2_0088_7000 as its second entry. The prediction holds.

Why "media corruption" was wrong: the media returned correct data. The data went to the wrong host page, so the application read a page that was never filled for it. Success status is correct from the controller's point of view.

Conclusion: "Strong evidence: PRP list page 0x1_5000_3000 was shared by CID 0x0193 (SQ1) and CID 0x0188 (SQ2), so CID 0x0193's last 4 KiB was written to CID 0x0188's buffer. Evidence: capture rows 3–5 and 38–53; SQE for CID 0x0188 in the same capture; driver tracepoint for CID 0x0193 showing a different second entry. Not yet reproduced on demand." Raising it to Confirmed requires a stress test that forces list-page reuse (small list pool, high queue depth) and shows the corruption appearing with the defect and disappearing with the fix.

Corrective action: fix the list-pool allocation in the driver so that each in-flight command owns its list pages until completion. Regression test: a high-QD mixed test with a deliberately small list pool, checking every read against known data patterns, and asserting at submission time (debug build) that no list page is owned by two outstanding commands.

Analysis

What can be said: there is a 9.4 ms gap between two completions on that CQ in the analyzer's time base (Observation). Whether CID 0x0A08 is the slow command cannot be established: the host's submission time (doorbell, host→device) was not captured, and the clocks are not aligned. The gap could equally be a period with nothing submitted.

Candidate explanations (all Possible): media stall (garbage collection, read retry); power-state exit (APST or link L1 substates); host-side delay before submission; a link-level stall invisible in a device→host-only capture without DLLPs.

Conclusion: "Unknown. The capture cannot attribute the latency: host→device traffic was not captured (doorbell time unknown), DLLPs were excluded, and the analyzer clock is not aligned with the latency monitor. Next capture: both directions, DLLPs included, a host tracepoint on submission with a known marker write for clock alignment, and power-state logging." Then list the cheap experiments that work without a capture: rerun with APST disabled, with ASPM disabled, and after a sustained-write preconditioning phase, and see which changes the spike frequency.

Why this is a good answer: it resists the pressure to produce a cause and instead produces a plan that will produce one. "Unknown, and here is how we will find out" is a senior answer.

Specification safety

Debugging often ends with "the device (or driver) violates the specification." That is a strong claim and needs strong grounding. The rules:

  1. Never invent. Do not invent quotations, section numbers, field offsets, opcodes, status codes or log identifiers. If you cannot check it now, say so.
  2. Consult the official document when exact normative behavior matters. Summaries, blog posts, this course and AI assistants are pointers, not sources.
  3. Cite the document and revision. "NVM Express Base Specification, Revision 2.4" or "PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x"; plus the section by name, as a reader can find it. Section numbers change between revisions; names change less.
  4. Separate normative text from interpretation. Mark which sentence is the requirement (paraphrased, or quoted exactly if you have the text in front of you) and which is your reading of it.
  5. Know the requirement level. Specifications distinguish mandatory requirements ("shall"), recommendations ("should") and permissions ("may"). A device doing something a "should" discourages is not a violation.
  6. Check the revision context. A feature added in a later revision cannot be required of a device that claims an earlier one. The NVMe Version register (VS) and Identify data tell you what the device claims. Check the Revision Changes document for what changed.
  7. Do not pretend to have read what you did not read. Analyzer manuals, vendor datasheets and members-only documents are cited only if actually consulted. "I believe the analyzer's decoder does X; not checked against its manual" is honest and useful.

The baseline for this course is the NVMe 2.4 specification set (Base 2.4 ratified 2026-07-31 and published 2026-08-04; NVM Command Set 1.3; NVMe over PCIe Transport 1.4; and the other documents listed in Truth Policy, Sources and Decoding Safety). The PCI Express Base Specification is a PCI-SIG members' document; cite it by name and revision family.

Checking a specification claim, step by step

  1. Identify which document owns the topic: Base (architecture, registers, admin commands, queues), a command set specification (I/O commands), a transport specification (mapping to PCIe, RDMA or TCP), PCIe Base (TLPs, errors, link), Management Interface, Boot. See the reading plan.
  2. Identify the revision that applies: the one the device claims and the one your requirement document targets.
  3. Find the section by name and read the requirement in context, including any conditions ("if the controller supports…").
  4. Note the requirement level (shall, should, may).
  5. Write the citation and your interpretation separately.
  6. If the text is ambiguous, say so and record both readings. Ambiguities are worth raising with the specification body or the vendor; do not resolve them silently in your favor.

Lab: grade and rewrite a bug report

Objective
Apply the confidence labels, the first-abnormal-event rule, the negative-evidence rule and specification safety to a realistic bug report.
Skills

Safety

Input
The synthetic report below.
Expected output
A marked-up report (each sentence labeled O / I / H / C), a list of problems, and a rewritten conclusion using the claim template.
Background
This page; Engineering Bug Report Training.

Title: SSD firmware drops commands under load.

"During the overnight stress test the system logged 'nvme0: I/O 77 QID 6 timeout, aborting' at 03:12:44. I looked at the analyzer capture from 03:12:40 to 03:12:45 (filtered to NVMe traffic only to save buffer) and there is no SQE fetch for CID 77 on QID 6, so the firmware dropped the command. AER showed one Completion Timeout at the root port. Per section 4.6 of the NVMe spec, the controller shall fetch commands in order, which it clearly didn't. The fix is a firmware update. Confidence: high."

Step 1 — Label every sentence

Mark each statement as Observation, Inference, Hypothesis or Conclusion.

Step 2 — Find the problems

Use the claim checklist. Pay special attention to the capture window, the filter, the AER entry and the specification citation.

Step 3 — Rewrite

Write the conclusion using the claim template, with an honest label, and list the evidence you would collect next.

Observation questions

  1. When was CID 77 submitted, and is that time inside the capture?
  2. What did "NVMe traffic only" probably exclude?
  3. Which requester's non-posted request timed out in the AER entry, and how does that relate to the NVMe timeout?

Challenge

Design the capture and logging setup that would turn the next occurrence into a Strong-evidence or Confirmed conclusion.

Hints

The Linux NVMe driver's default I/O timeout is commonly 30 seconds (it is a configurable module parameter; check your system). An analyzer filter "NVMe only" usually means decoded NVMe transactions, which may exclude DLLPs, ordered sets and non-NVMe addresses. A root port completion timeout usually concerns a CPU-initiated non-posted request to the device.

Solution

Problems:

  1. Window. The timeout was logged at 03:12:44. With a 30 s timeout (if default), CID 77 was submitted around 03:12:14, so the capture from 03:12:40 cannot contain its doorbell or SQE fetch. "No SQE fetch for CID 77" is not evidence (negative-evidence rule).
  2. Filter. "NVMe traffic only" probably removed DLLPs and link events, which would hide link-level causes. The coverage statement is missing.
  3. Mechanism not established. "Firmware dropped the command" is a hypothesis presented as a conclusion. Alternatives: the host never rang the doorbell for slot 77's position; the SQE was fetched and executed and the CQE was not consumed; the link had a problem.
  4. AER misread. A completion timeout at the root port concerns a request the root port's side issued (for example the driver's MMIO read of CSTS during abort handling), not the device's command processing. It may be a consequence of the device being unresponsive at that moment, or the abort path itself. Its time relative to the NVMe timeout matters.
  5. Specification citation. "Section 4.6" is unchecked and the revision is missing. "Shall fetch commands in order" is not the real requirement: the controller fetches from the head of each SQ in order, but command execution and completion order are not required to follow submission order. And nothing shows an out-of-order fetch anyway.
  6. Label. "High" is unsupported. Nothing in the evidence bears on the claimed mechanism.

Rewrite:

CLAIM:        Unknown. One I/O on QID 6 (CID 77) did not complete within the driver timeout.
CONFIDENCE:   Unknown
EVIDENCE:     dmesg 03:12:44 "I/O 77 QID 6 timeout, aborting" (Observation).
              AER: one Completion Timeout logged at root port 00:01.1; time of detection
              relative to 03:12:44 not established (Observation).
COVERAGE:     Analyzer 03:12:40-03:12:45, NVMe-decoded transactions only (DLLPs, ordered
              sets, non-NVMe addresses excluded). CID 77 was submitted ~30 s before the
              timeout (assuming the default timeout; to confirm), outside the window.
ALTERNATIVES: (a) controller never fetched the SQE; (b) fetched and executed, CQE not
              consumed by host; (c) link or device unresponsive; (d) host did not publish
              the entry. None excluded.
SPEC BASIS:   None claimed yet.
WOULD CHANGE: Capture with pre-trigger covering submission (segmented buffer triggered on
              the doorbell for QID 6 or a 60 s rolling buffer), unfiltered including DLLPs;
              driver tracepoints for submission (slot, CID, doorbell value) and for CQ head
              and phase at timeout; AER and SMART read before and after.

What a senior engineer notices: the report's real value is the timestamp and the queue. Everything else needs to be redone. The quickest disambiguation is the driver-side state at timeout: if the CQ for QID 6 contains a valid CQE for CID 77 that the host had not consumed, the firmware theory is refuted without any new capture.

Failure variants

  • The capture covers submission, but the SQE fetch is visible and the CQE is also visible: the investigation moves to the host's completion handling.
  • The capture shows the doorbell but no fetch, with full coverage: now "controller did not fetch" is a valid observation, and the next question is controller state (CSTS, CFS) and Bus Master Enable.

Quiz

  1. Which label fits "mechanism explains all observations; one plausible alternative not yet excluded"?

    • Confirmed
    • Strong evidence
    • Likely
    • Possible
    Answer and reasoning

    Likely. Strong evidence requires competitors to be eliminated.

  2. When is absence of an event evidence?

    Answer

    When the event was expected and the capture is known to be complete enough to observe it (right link, direction, window, filters and decoding).

  3. The first abnormal row of a trace is its very first row. What do you conclude?

    • That row is the root cause
    • The real first deviation may be before the capture; widen it
    • The trace is corrupt
    • The symptom is the cause
    Answer and reasoning

    The edge-of-capture trap: you have found the start of the evidence, not necessarily the first deviation.

  4. In Case 3, why was "media corruption" wrong even though data was wrong and status was success?

    Answer

    The media returned the correct data; it was written to the wrong host page because the PRP list was shared. The first deviation is the PRP list contents, which precede any media action.

  5. Which is a safe specification citation?

    • "Section 5.2.3 requires…"
    • "The NVMe spec says…"
    • "As everybody knows, the spec requires…"
    • "NVM Express Base Specification, Revision 2.4, Controller Registers (CSTS description) — paraphrase: …; our interpretation: …"
    Answer and reasoning

    It names the document, revision and section by name, and separates paraphrase from interpretation.

  6. What upgrades a conclusion to Confirmed?

    Answer

    Reproduction on demand where the fix removes the failure and reverting it brings it back, or direct observation of the mechanism in evidence with stated complete coverage.

  7. Root port AER shows Surprise Down. Which statement is justified by that alone?

    • The link to the device went down unexpectedly, as detected by the root port
    • The slot is faulty
    • The SSD firmware crashed
    • The cable is loose
    Answer and reasoning

    AER says what was detected and by whom. Cause needs more evidence (Case 2).

  8. Name the six items of a trace coverage statement.

    Answer

    Capture start, capture end, whether timestamps are complete, whether packets may be filtered, whether analyzer decoding may omit events, and whether both directions were captured.

  9. Why is "should" in a specification different from "shall" when you claim a violation?

    Answer

    "Shall" is a mandatory requirement; "should" is a recommendation. Not following a recommendation is not a compliance violation, though it may be a quality concern worth raising.

Interview questions

  1. Beginner. What is the difference between an observation and an inference in a debugging report?

    Model answer

    An observation is recorded by an instrument or read from a log or register, with its source. An inference assigns meaning using rules ("this write is a doorbell because of its address"). Inferences can be wrong even when observations are right, so reports should keep them distinct.

  2. Intermediate. Your analyzer shows no completion for a Memory Read. What do you check before saying the completion was never sent?

    Model answer

    Capture window (did it end too early?), direction coverage, filters (completions or that requester excluded?), buffer overflow, decoding (could the completion be misdecoded, for example a different tag width?), and probe location (was the completion on another link?). Then check for a positive control: other completions to the same requester captured nearby.

  3. Advanced. Explain why the component logging an AER error is often not the cause. Give two examples.

    Model answer

    AER is detected by the receiver or requester that notices the problem. A malformed TLP is logged by the receiver, while the sender built it (for example an MPS mismatch). A completion timeout is logged by the requester, while the completer or the path failed to return the completion. The logger is the witness.

  4. Senior. A manager wants a root cause by tomorrow. You have Likely. What do you write?

    Model answer

    The Likely conclusion with its evidence and alternatives, the actions justified at that level (for example a low-risk mitigation, extra logging, or a hold on a risky deployment), the experiment that would move it to Strong evidence or Confirmed, and when that result will be available. Never upgrade the label to meet a deadline.

  5. Senior. How do you handle an ambiguity in the specification that decides whether a device or a driver is at fault?

    Model answer

    Cite the text and its revision, write down both readings and what each implies, check later revisions and errata or the Revision Changes document for clarification, and raise it with the vendor and, if needed, the specification body. Meanwhile, make the product robust to both readings where possible, and label conclusions that depend on one reading.

Summary, mastery gate and next steps

Evidence discipline means four habits: label every claim with its confidence and evidence; find the first deviation and then its preceding cause, rather than stopping at the loudest symptom; treat absence as evidence only with stated coverage; and ground every specification claim in a named document and revision, with interpretation kept separate.

Do not move on until you can:

  • define the five confidence labels and give the minimum evidence for each
  • write a root-cause conclusion with the claim template
  • state the first-abnormal-event rule and apply it to a merged timeline, avoiding the edge-of-capture trap
  • state the negative-evidence rule and write a six-item coverage statement
  • rewrite an unsafe specification claim into a safe one
  • solve the four worked cases without opening the solutions
