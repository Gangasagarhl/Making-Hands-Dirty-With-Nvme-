One large simulated investigation that covers the whole course. A server's NVMe SSD goes quiet now and then. You get the boot trace, the configuration space, the NVMe initialisation, normal I/O, power transitions, a failure, a recovery, a second failure, and every log a real investigation would collect. The root cause is hidden. Find the first abnormal event, write a bug report, and only then open the official solution.

How the capstone works

This is the course's final practical. Nothing on this page tells you the answer before the solution section. The evidence is complete enough to solve the case. It also holds distractors: real anomalies that have nothing to do with the root cause, as real incidents always do.

Learning objectives

  • Read evidence from every layer (PCIe physical and link, Transaction Layer Packets (TLPs), configuration space, NVM Express (NVMe) registers, queues, commands, interrupts, power management, Linux logs, NVMe logs) and correlate it on one timeline.
  • Tell apart the first abnormal event, its consequences, and unrelated anomalies.
  • Form hypotheses, rank them, and design experiments that would separate them.
  • Write a bug report that another engineer could act on without talking to you.

Prerequisites

Everything up to Chapter 71, especially link training (Chapter 9), enumeration and BARs (Chapter 17, Chapter 19), NVMe registers and initialisation (Chapter 24, Chapter 26), queues and doorbells (Chapter 25–Chapter 28), the end-to-end flow (Chapter 39), interrupts (Chapter 42, Chapter 43), errors (Chapter 44–Chapter 47) and power (Chapter 50). Read Engineering Bug Report Training before you write your report.

Rules

  1. Timebox: four hours for the investigation, one hour for the report.
  2. Work only from the evidence on this page and the course material. Do not open the solution until your report is written.
  3. Hints are graded from gentle to strong. Each hint you open costs points in the grading rubric; note which ones you used.
  4. Your deliverable is a bug report in the format taught in Engineering Bug Report Training, plus a one-page timeline.

This is a paper investigation (Safe lab). If you reproduce parts of it on real hardware, power-management and reset experiments are Caution lab and must run on a lab machine with no data you need.

Every trace, log, register value and counter on this page is synthetic, written for teaching, built on the course's reference lab system (see the environment table). Kernel log lines are paraphrased. Vendor names, model names and identifiers are fictional. Nothing here describes a real product.

31.050 s (expected): PS3. 32.950 s (expected): PS4. Confirmed later with Get Features (Power Management) only in the sense that the current state reads back as PS0 when queried, because the query itself wakes the controller.

Analyzers see the link, not the inside of the controller. Whether the controller entered PS3 or PS4, and what it did internally on entry or exit, is invisible here. You can only infer it from configuration (the APST table), timing (exit latency in later traces) and vendor telemetry. Say so in your report when you rely on it.

Your investigation

Answer these questions in writing as you go. They are the skeleton of your report. They do not give away the answer, but they make sure you do not skip a layer.

  1. Build a single timeline from boot to the second failure, with one line per significant event and its source (analyzer, kernel, harness, register read).
  2. Did link training, enumeration, BAR assignment and NVMe initialisation complete correctly? Name the evidence for each.
  3. For a normal I/O, list the transactions and check that each matches what the specification and the course predict.
  4. In the first failure, what is present and what is missing compared with a normal I/O? Is the controller processing commands?
  5. Where is each stalled command's completion at 104 s: not produced, produced but not delivered, or delivered but not consumed? Prove it.
  6. Explain the "completion polled" messages and the 30-second stall length.
  7. Are the AER Receiver Errors part of the causal chain? Use the counts.
  8. Are the Error Information log entries part of the causal chain?
  9. Why does the admin queue keep working when the I/O queues do not?
  10. Why does nvme reset fix it, and why only for a while?
  11. What conditions must all be true for a stall to happen? Use the harness statistics and the change history.
  12. What is the first abnormal event, and what is the root cause? What experiments would confirm it?
