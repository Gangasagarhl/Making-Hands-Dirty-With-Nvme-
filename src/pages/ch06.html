Each PCI Express generation roughly doubles the bandwidth of a lane, but the way it gets there changes: faster clocks for Gen1 to Gen5, a leaner encoding from Gen3, and from Gen6 four voltage levels per symbol with error correction and fixed-size flits. This chapter teaches you to turn a "GT/s" number into bytes per second, honestly, and to know what that number does and does not promise an NVMe SSD.

Why this chapter exists

Almost every performance conversation about NVMe starts with a number like "Gen4 x4" and an expectation like "so it should do 8 GB/s". Both the number and the expectation hide several layers of overhead. Engineers who cannot do this arithmetic misdiagnose healthy systems as broken and broken systems as healthy.

There is a second trap: version numbers. "PCIe 5.0", "Gen5", "32 GT/s", "NVMe 2.0", "NVMe 2.4" all appear in the same datasheets. This chapter separates them cleanly.

Learning objectives

  • State the transfer rate, encoding and modulation of every PCIe generation from Gen1 (2.5 GT/s) to Gen7 (128 GT/s).
  • Compute per-lane and per-link (x4, x8, x16) bandwidth from the transfer rate and encoding, showing the formula.
  • Explain 8b/10b, 128b/130b, NRZ, PAM4, Flit Mode and forward error correction (FEC), and why each was introduced.
  • Estimate packet-level efficiency (headers, framing, link-layer traffic) and so the realistic data throughput of an NVMe SSD link.
  • Explain what a faster generation does and does not do to latency.
  • Explain speed compatibility and distinguish a PCIe specification revision, a link speed and an NVMe specification revision.

The story

Imagine sending letters by a conveyor belt. In the first factory (Gen1), the belt moves at a certain speed, but for every 8 letters you send you must also put 2 empty envelopes on the belt so the machine at the other end can keep count. Two out of every ten places are "wasted".

Later factories make the belt faster and faster. In the third factory (Gen3), engineers find a cleverer counting trick: only 2 marker places for every 128 letters. Almost nothing is wasted.

Then the belt gets so fast the letters start to blur. So in the sixth factory (Gen6), instead of making the belt faster again, they make each box hold two letters by using four colours of box instead of two. But colours are easier to confuse than "box or no box", so they add a little "checking card" to every bundle of boxes, so mistakes can be fixed at the other end. And they send letters in fixed-size bundles so the checking cards always fit.

Think of a motorway's capacity. The speed limit is the transfer rate (GT/s). The number of lanes is the link width. But not every vehicle carries cargo: some are escort vehicles (encoding overhead), some are paperwork vans (packet headers), some are traffic police (acknowledgements and flow-control messages). The cargo delivered per hour is the throughput an application sees. Raising the speed limit increases cargo per hour, but does not make a single trip from your house shorter if most of the trip is spent at loading docks.

  • On a motorway, vehicles in different lanes are independent. On a PCIe link, one packet is striped across all lanes; lanes are not independent channels.
  • Escort vehicles in the analogy are separate vehicles; encoding overhead is bits inside every symbol or block, not separate packets.
  • Motorway traffic in both directions shares nothing; on PCIe, the two directions are independent in bandwidth too, but they are coupled by the protocol: reading data requires requests in one direction and acknowledgements and credits in the other.
  • PAM4 does not "carry two cars per slot" for free: it trades noise margin for bits, which is why error correction comes with it.

Beginner explanation

A PCIe generation is mainly a speed for each lane. The speed is written in GT/s, gigatransfers per second: how many symbols per second each lane puts on the wire in one direction.

Not all of those symbols are your data. A part of every stream is used so the receiver can stay in step (encoding), and a part is used to wrap your data in packets with addresses and checks. So:

  • the transfer rate (GT/s) is the raw speed;
  • the encoded bandwidth is what is left after encoding (for example, 16 GT/s with 128b/130b encoding gives about 15.75 Gb/s per lane, about 1.97 GB/s);
  • the data throughput is what is left after packet overhead, which depends on packet size and traffic pattern.

Multiply by the number of lanes for the link. A Gen4 x4 NVMe SSD link has about 7.9 GB/s of encoded bandwidth in each direction; real SSDs reading sequentially reach roughly 7 GB/s because of the remaining overheads.

Both ends must support a generation to use it. A Gen5 SSD in a Gen4 slot runs at Gen4.

Worked rows, step by step

Gen1 x4. 2.5 GT/s × 4 lanes = 10 Gb/s raw. 8b/10b keeps 8 of every 10 bits: 10 × 0.8 = 8 Gb/s. ÷ 8 = 1.0 GB/s per direction.

Gen2 x16. 5.0 × 16 = 80 Gb/s raw × 0.8 = 64 Gb/s ÷ 8 = 8.0 GB/s.

Gen3 x4. 8.0 × 4 = 32 Gb/s raw × 128/130 = 31.508 Gb/s ÷ 8 = ≈ 3.94 GB/s. Note that Gen3 almost doubled Gen2's usable bandwidth while raising the rate by only 1.6×: the switch from 20 % to about 1.5 % encoding overhead supplied the rest.

Gen4 x4 (the lab system). 16.0 × 4 = 64 Gb/s raw × 128/130 = 63.015 Gb/s ÷ 8 = ≈ 7.88 GB/s.

Gen5 x4. 32 × 4 = 128 Gb/s × 128/130 = 126.03 Gb/s ÷ 8 = ≈ 15.75 GB/s.

Gen6 x16. 64 × 16 = 1024 Gb/s raw ÷ 8 = 128 GB/s of flits. × 236/256 = ≈ 118 GB/s of TLP bytes per direction.

Gen7 x4. 128 × 4 = 512 Gb/s ÷ 8 = 64 GB/s of flits × 236/256 = ≈ 59 GB/s.

Gb/s versus GB/s: a factor of 8. "64 Gb/s" and "8 GB/s" describe the same raw Gen4 x4 rate. Many slide decks mix them. Always write the unit with a capital or lower-case B deliberately, and say whether a number is per direction or both directions.

Engineering explanation

Signalling rate

The transfer rate in GT/s counts symbols per second on one lane in one direction. For Gen1–Gen5, which use Non-Return-to-Zero (NRZ) two-level signalling, one transfer is one bit, so 16 GT/s is 16 × 109 raw bits per second per lane. GT/s is used instead of Gb/s precisely because not every bit transferred is a data bit.

Each generation's rate was chosen against the physics of copper. Insertion loss grows with frequency; the relevant frequency is the Nyquist frequency, half the NRZ bit rate: 1.25 GHz at Gen1, 4 GHz at Gen3, 8 GHz at Gen4, 16 GHz at Gen5. Gen3 deliberately chose 8 GT/s (not 10 GT/s) and recovered the difference with a better encoding, to limit loss on existing board materials.

Encoding: 8b/10b (Gen1, Gen2)

8b/10b maps every 8-bit byte to a 10-bit symbol chosen from a table so that:

  • the stream is DC-balanced (equal ones and zeros over time, tracked by "running disparity");
  • there are enough transitions for clock recovery (no more than five identical bits in a row);
  • special control symbols (K-codes such as COM, STP, SDP, END, SKP, IDL) exist outside the data set, so framing and ordered sets are unambiguous;
  • many bit errors produce invalid symbols or disparity errors, detected directly at the receiver.

The price is 20 % of the raw rate.

Encoding: 128b/130b (Gen3, Gen4, Gen5)

128b/130b sends 128-bit blocks (16 bytes per lane) preceded by a 2-bit sync header: 10b marks a data block, 01b an ordered-set block. Transition density and DC balance come from a scrambler (a pseudo-random sequence XORed with data) rather than from a code table. Framing of packets inside data blocks uses tokens (such as STP for the start of a TLP, SDP for a DLLP, EDS for end of data stream) instead of K-code symbols.

Overhead: 2/130 ≈ 1.54 %. The trade-offs: scrambling alone guarantees transitions only statistically; error detection relies more on the Data Link Layer's checks; and receivers need block alignment (achieved with the Electrical Idle Exit Ordered Set) as well as bit lock.

Modulation: NRZ and PAM4

At 64 GT/s, NRZ would need a 32 GHz Nyquist frequency, with channel loss far beyond practical boards. Gen6 instead adopted four-level Pulse Amplitude Modulation (PAM4): each symbol (unit interval) carries one of four voltage levels, encoding two bits. The symbol rate stays at 32 GBaud, the same Nyquist frequency (16 GHz) as Gen5, while the bit rate doubles.

The cost: three eyes stacked in the same voltage range, each about one third the height, so roughly 9.5 dB less signal-to-noise per decision. The raw error rate rises from the "almost never" of NRZ PCIe to something that needs correcting routinely. That is why Gen6 brought forward error correction and Flit Mode.

Flit Mode and FEC (Gen6, Gen7)

In Flit Mode, the link carries fixed-size 256-byte flits (flow-control units) instead of variable-length framed packets. Per the description published by PCI-SIG, each flit has:

Composition of a 256-byte Flit Mode flit (as described by PCI-SIG; confirm in the Base Specification)
BytesContentPurpose
236TLP bytesTransaction Layer Packets packed back to back, possibly spanning flits

  • FEC corrects most errors in place with very low added latency (PCI-SIG describes it as low single-digit nanoseconds; Uncertain confirm figures).
  • CRC detects what FEC cannot correct; such a flit is retried by the link layer, so retry moves from per-TLP to per-flit.
  • The per-TLP framing token and per-TLP Link CRC (LCRC) of non-flit mode are replaced by flit-level mechanisms, and Flit Mode uses a different TLP header format. Chapter 10 and Chapter 11 show both formats.
  • Flit Mode is required at 64 GT/s and above. When both ports support Flit Mode, it is also used at lower data rates, where the flits are carried using that rate's encoding. Version-dependent
  • Flit Mode adds a new low-power partial-width state, L0p, and does not support L0s (Chapter 8). Version-dependent

The exact flit layout (byte positions, how the 6 FEC bytes are interleaved across lanes, the CRC polynomial, how flit sequence numbers and replay work, and the "standard" versus other flit types) is defined in the Base Specification, Revision 6.0 and later. This course does not reproduce field positions; confirm them there.

Effective throughput: the second layer of overhead

Encoded bandwidth still has to carry packets with headers and framing, plus link-management traffic. For non-flit mode (Gen1–Gen5), each TLP costs, besides its data payload:

  • a TLP header: 12 bytes (3 DW) or 16 bytes (4 DW, for 64-bit addresses);
  • approximately 8 bytes of physical framing, sequence number and Link CRC (LCRC); Derived confirm framing details in Chapter 7 and the Base Specification;
  • optionally a 4-byte End-to-end CRC (ECRC).

Latency implications

A faster generation shortens serialisation time: the time to clock a packet onto the wire. For a 280-byte TLP (256-byte payload) on a x4 link:

Gen33.94 GB/s≈ 71 ns

Gen515.75 GB/s≈ 18 ns

But the latency of a 4 KiB NVMe read is dominated by other things: the NAND read itself (tens of microseconds for TLC), controller firmware, the round trip to fetch the command from host memory (hundreds of nanoseconds to a microsecond, much of it inside the Root Complex and memory system), and interrupt delivery. Doubling link speed shaves perhaps a few hundred nanoseconds from a 4 KiB read that takes tens of microseconds. Derived Generations transform bandwidth far more than latency.

Gen6 and Gen7 add a small FEC decode latency and change retry behaviour; Flit Mode also packs small TLPs more efficiently. Low-power states (L1, L0p) add exit latencies that can dwarf serialisation time; Chapter 50 covers power management.

Port-to-port latency through a SerDes, a Switch or a retimer is implementation-dependent. Switches commonly add on the order of a hundred nanoseconds or more per hop; measure on your platform rather than relying on datasheet averages.

Compatibility across generations

  • Every port supports 2.5 GT/s and every lower rate up to its maximum. Links train at 2.5 GT/s and move to the highest common rate (Chapter 9).
  • Gen3 introduced equalisation at 8 GT/s; Gen4, Gen5 require equalisation at each of their higher rates; Gen6 adds PAM4-specific equalisation. Rate changes beyond 5 GT/s are not just "clock faster".
  • Flit Mode is negotiated: two Gen6-capable ports use it, and a Gen6 port talking to a Gen5 port uses non-flit mode at up to 32 GT/s.
  • Connectors and boards are rated for a maximum rate. A Gen5 CPU and Gen5 SSD joined through a Gen4-grade riser may train at Gen4 or train at Gen5 and suffer errors.

A device can claim compliance with a newer PCIe revision while supporting a lower maximum speed (for example, implementing Revision 5.0 rules but only up to 16 GT/s). These axes are independent, and the NVMe over PCIe Transport Specification (Revision 1.4 in the August 2026 set) describes how NVMe is carried on PCIe without fixing a PCIe generation.

"PCIe 4.0 NVMe drive" means a drive whose PCIe port supports up to 16 GT/s. It says nothing about which NVMe revision the controller implements.

The central concept on the ladder: effective bandwidth

A faster conveyor belt delivers more letters, but some places on the belt hold markers and envelopes, not letters.

Link bandwidth = rate × lanes × encoding efficiency; data throughput = link bandwidth × packet efficiency × protocol efficiency.

The SerDes runs at the transfer rate; the encoder/scrambler adds sync headers or 8b/10b symbols; the link layer adds sequence numbers and LCRC; the transaction layer adds headers. MPS caps payload per TLP.

The Base Specification defines the data rates, encodings, framing and Flit Mode; Link Capabilities 2 lists supported speeds; Device Control sets MPS and MRRS.

On the wire: blocks with sync headers (Gen3–5) or flits (Gen6–7) containing TLPs and link-layer information; SKP ordered sets at intervals.

An analyzer shows TLP sizes, inter-TLP gaps, DLLP density and link utilisation; a throughput graph that plateaus well below the encoded bandwidth points to gaps or small payloads.

If throughput is half the expectation, check negotiated speed/width first, then MPS, then read-request size and outstanding-request limits, then the device itself.

A Gen5 NVMe SSD consumes noticeably more power in its PCIe PHY than a Gen3 one at the same width, and the controller must move twice the data per second through its internal buses, DRAM and flash channels. That is why the first SSDs of a new generation often do not saturate the link: the media and controller back-end, not PCIe, become the limit.

On the board, a Gen5 slot needs every element of the path (CPU package, board, connector, riser, retimer, drive) to be rated for 32 GT/s. A single Gen4-grade element typically causes the link to fall back to 16 GT/s during training, or to reach 32 GT/s with poor margin.

Specification view

Data rates, encodings and Flit Mode are defined in the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 — Physical Layer Logical Block; electrical requirements per rate in the — Electrical Sub-Block; the register fields that expose speeds in the — PCI Express Capability Structure. Earlier revisions (1.x through 5.0) define the lower rates; newer revisions incorporate them. NVMe documents never define link speeds; the NVMe over PCIe Transport Specification, Revision 1.4 is transport mapping only.

  • Defined rates: 2.5, 5.0, 8.0, 16.0, 32.0, 64.0 GT/s (Revision 6.0) and 128.0 GT/s (Revision 7.0).
  • 2.5 and 5.0 GT/s use 8b/10b; 8.0, 16.0 and 32.0 GT/s use 128b/130b in non-flit mode; 64.0 GT/s and 128.0 GT/s use PAM4 and require Flit Mode.
  • Link Capabilities 2 contains a Supported Link Speeds Vector; Current Link Speed and Max Link Speed are indexes into it.

Every bandwidth number on this page is derived with the formula given, not quoted. Packet-efficiency figures assume the stated header and framing sizes.

Whether a port steps through intermediate rates or jumps directly to the highest; how many retries at a failing rate; whether a device autonomously lowers its speed when idle; SerDes power at each rate; Switch and retimer latency.

Each revision adds a rate. Flit Mode, L0p and FEC exist from Revision 6.0. Some register fields (for example, extra bits in Link Status 2 or new capability structures for 32 GT/s and 64 GT/s equalisation) appear only in newer revisions. Release years quoted here are approximate.

  • The specification does not promise any application throughput; it defines rates and protocols.
  • It does not say which generation an NVMe revision requires (none is required).
  • Check in the Base Specification: exact flit layout and FEC structure; the framing token formats of 128b/130b; SKP scheduling intervals in each clocking mode.

Never assume bidirectional marketing numbers are per-direction; never assume an SSD's datasheet throughput is achievable on a link that negotiated a lower speed or uses smaller MPS; never assume Gen6 bandwidth equals 2 × Gen5 for small packets or for links running in non-flit mode.

Protocol view

A generation shows up in the protocol in three places:

  1. Advertisement. Every TS1/TS2 ordered set carries a Data Rate Identifier listing the rates the sender supports, plus a speed-change request indication (Chapter 9).
  2. Transition. Speed changes happen only through the Recovery state: both ports enter Electrical Idle, switch rate, and retrain at the new rate. At 8 GT/s and above, equalisation runs (once per rate, typically the first time that rate is reached after a fundamental reset).
  3. Framing. The same TLP looks different on the wire at different rates: 8b/10b symbols with K-code framing; 128b/130b blocks with tokens; or Flit Mode flits. Above the Physical Layer, nothing changes in non-flit mode; in Flit Mode the link layer and TLP header format also change.

Reasoning

  1. First abnormal event: the Nak at row 3. Everything after it is the link's recovery machinery doing its job.
  2. Repeated Naks on replay mean errors are persistent, not a single bit flip. That points at signal integrity at 16 GT/s.
  3. The link fell back instead of failing. Software sees no I/O error; the user sees throughput drop roughly eightfold (Gen4 x4 → Gen1 x4: 7.88 → 1.0 GB/s encoded).
  4. Linux evidence would include correctable AER errors (Bad TLP, Replay Timer Timeout or Receiver Error) at the Root Port, and LnkSta showing 2.5GT/s. Bandwidth notification (Link Bandwidth Management Status) may be set.
  5. Root cause candidates: marginal channel at 16 GT/s (connector, riser), temperature drift, retimer issue; the device is not necessarily at fault.

Illustrative shapes of output lines (exact text depends on pciutils and kernel versions):

  • LnkCap2: Supported Link Speeds: 2.5-16GT/s, Crosslink- Retimer- 2Retimers- DRS-
  • LnkCtl2: Target Link Speed: 16GT/s, EnterCompliance- SpeedDis-
  • DevCtl: … MaxPayload 256 bytes, MaxReadReq 512 bytes

The "available PCIe bandwidth" message uses the kernel's own per-generation calculation including encoding overhead, so its numbers (for example 63.012 Gb/s for Gen4 x4) are encoded bandwidth, not achievable data throughput. It cannot tell you about TLP overhead, MPS or errors. Version-dependent wording varies by kernel version.

Code / pseudocode

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""PCIe bandwidth calculator: encoded bandwidth and approximate TLP data throughput.
All results are derived estimates, per direction, GB/s = 1e9 bytes/s."""

GENS = {
    #       GT/s   encoding efficiency          label
    1: (2.5,   8 / 10,     "8b/10b"),
    2: (5.0,   8 / 10,     "8b/10b"),
    3: (8.0,   128 / 130,  "128b/130b"),
    4: (16.0,  128 / 130,  "128b/130b"),
    5: (32.0,  128 / 130,  "128b/130b"),
    6: (64.0,  236 / 256,  "PAM4, Flit Mode (TLP bytes)"),
    7: (128.0, 236 / 256,  "PAM4, Flit Mode (TLP bytes)"),
}

def encoded_gbps(gen: int, lanes: int) -> float:
    rate, eff, _ = GENS[gen]
    return rate * lanes * eff / 8          # GB/s per direction

def tlp_efficiency(payload: int, header: int = 16, framing: int = 8, ecrc: bool = False) -> float:
    """Fraction of TLP bytes that are payload (non-flit mode assumptions)."""
    return payload / (payload + header + framing + (4 if ecrc else 0))

def data_estimate(gen, lanes, payload=256, header=16, link_overhead=0.03):
    """Encoded bandwidth x TLP efficiency x (1 - DLLP/SKP allowance). Flit Mode already
    accounts for framing/CRC in its efficiency, so only the header is charged there."""
    bw = encoded_gbps(gen, lanes)
    framing = 0 if gen >= 6 else 8
    return bw * tlp_efficiency(payload, header, framing) * (1 - link_overhead)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"{'Gen':<4}{'enc':<30}{'x1':>8}{'x4':>8}{'x8':>8}{'x16':>9}   x4 data@MPS256")
    for g, (rate, eff, label) in GENS.items():
        row = "".join(f"{encoded_gbps(g, n):>8.2f}" for n in (1, 4, 8))
        print(f"{g:<4}{label:<30}{row}{encoded_gbps(g, 16):>9.2f}   ~{data_estimate(g, 4):.2f}")
    print()
    for mps in (128, 256, 512):
        print(f"Gen4 x4, payload {mps:3d} B: TLP efficiency {tlp_efficiency(mps):.3f}, "
              f"~{data_estimate(4, 4, mps):.2f} GB/s data")

Worked example

Scenario. A team buys Gen5 x4 NVMe SSDs rated at 14 GB/s sequential read and installs them in servers whose Root Ports support up to Gen4. They measure 6.9 GB/s. Is something wrong?

  1. Negotiated link: SSD LnkCap 32GT/s x4; Root Port LnkCap 16GT/s x4; LnkSta 16GT/s x4. Compatibility: the link runs at Gen4.
  2. Ceiling: Gen4 x4 encoded ≈ 7.88 GB/s; with MPS 256 and about 3 % link-layer allowance ≈ 7.88 × 0.914 × 0.97 ≈ 7.0 GB/s. Derived
  3. Compare: 6.9 GB/s is about 98 % of the estimated ceiling. Nothing is broken; the platform is the limit.
  4. What would change it? Only a Gen5-capable path. Increasing queue depth, changing I/O size or updating SSD firmware will not exceed the link ceiling.
  5. Counter-check: if the same drives measured 5.0 GB/s, the next suspects would be MPS 128 (ceiling ≈ 6.4 GB/s), a link at x2, or CPU/NUMA effects.

Second scenario. What x16 link is needed to feed four Gen5 x4 SSDs at full speed through a Switch? Each SSD needs ≈ 14 GB/s of data; four need ≈ 56 GB/s. Gen5 x16 encoded is ≈ 63 GB/s, ≈ 56–57 GB/s of data after overheads. It is just enough, with no headroom; a Gen4 x16 upstream (≈ 28 GB/s data) would halve the aggregate. Derived

Common mistakes

  • Treating GT/s as GB/s, or forgetting the factor of 8 between Gb and GB.
  • Quoting bidirectional totals as if one direction could use them.
  • Forgetting encoding overhead for Gen1/Gen2 (20 %), or overstating it for Gen3+ (it is about 1.5 %, not 20 %).
  • Ignoring TLP header overhead and MPS.
  • Assuming doubling the generation halves I/O latency.
  • Calling a drive "NVMe Gen4". PCIe Gen4 is the link; NVMe has revisions, not generations.
  • Assuming Gen6 always doubles Gen5 throughput: two Gen6 ports joined by a Gen5-grade channel may run at 32 GT/s, and non-flit/flit differences change efficiency.

Debugging connection

When measured throughput is below expectation, walk down this ladder, cheapest check first:

  1. Negotiated speed and width on both ends (LnkSta). A single generation down halves the ceiling; a width down halves it again.
  2. Path: is a Switch upstream link, or a Root Port shared by other busy devices, the real limit (Chapter 4)?
  3. MPS and MRRS (DevCtl). Small payloads cost efficiency; small read requests limit how much data each request can return.
  4. Errors and Recovery: AER correctable counters, link bandwidth notifications. A link that replays or retrains loses throughput silently.
  5. The device and host: queue depth, CPU, NUMA placement, SSD internal limits (media, thermal throttling).

A classic pattern: throughput is exactly half or exactly one eighth of expectation. Exact ratios point to link configuration (x2 instead of x4; Gen1 instead of Gen4), because physics rarely produces round numbers by accident.

Senior engineer insight

Seniors carry three numbers in their heads for any NVMe link: the encoded ceiling, the realistic data ceiling at the configured MPS, and the device's own rating. The smallest of those is the expectation. They phrase findings as "the link is at 98 % of its practical ceiling" rather than "the drive is slow", because that statement tells everyone where effort would and would not help.

They also know where generations stop mattering. For small random I/O, an SSD's IOPS are limited by its controller and media long before Gen4 x4 bandwidth; moving to Gen5 helps sequential throughput and high-queue-depth workloads, much less for 4 KiB latency. For Gen6 and beyond, they watch the interplay of FEC, retry and power states, because tail latency (not average bandwidth) is what changes.

Lab: Predict, then measure, an SSD link's throughput ceiling

Objective

Compute the expected sequential-read ceiling of an NVMe SSD from its link configuration, then compare with a read-only measurement.

Skills

Bandwidth arithmetic; reading LnkSta and DevCtl; interpreting benchmark results against a derived ceiling.

Safety

Safe lab for steps 1–2 (read-only inspection and calculation). Caution lab for step 3: the benchmark reads the raw block device; it is configured read-only, but run it only on a non-production machine and double-check the device name.

Hardware required

Linux machine with an NVMe SSD.

Software required

pciutils, Python 3 with pcie_bw.py, and optionally fio.

Input

The SSD's BDF and block device name (for example /dev/nvme0n1).

Expected output

A one-line statement: "Link GenN xM, MPS P: encoded ceiling X GB/s, practical ceiling ≈ Y GB/s; measured Z GB/s = W % of practical ceiling."

Background

Large sequential reads move data device→host as Memory Write TLPs of up to MPS bytes. The practical ceiling is encoded bandwidth × TLP efficiency × a small link-layer allowance.

Step 1 — Collect configuration

sudo lspci -vvv -s 01:00.0 | grep -E "LnkSta:|MaxPayload"

Step 2 — Compute

Edit the __main__ section of pcie_bw.py or call data_estimate(gen, lanes, payload) with your values.

Step 3 — Measure (optional)

# Read-only sequential read; --readonly makes fio refuse to write
sudo fio --name=seqread --filename=/dev/nvme0n1 --readonly --rw=read --bs=1M \
         --iodepth=32 --ioengine=libaio --direct=1 --runtime=20 --time_based --numjobs=1

Observation

Note the bandwidth fio reports (it reports in MiB/s and MB/s; use MB/s = 106 bytes/s to compare).

Questions

  1. What percentage of the practical ceiling did you reach?
  2. If below 85 %, is the limit the link or the device? How would you tell?
  3. What would the ceiling be with MPS 128? With x2?

Challenge

Run the same measurement with --bs=4k --rw=randread --iodepth=1. Compute the bandwidth that represents. Why is it so far below the link ceiling, and what does that tell you about generations and latency?

Hint

At queue depth 1, throughput = block size ÷ latency. A 4 KiB read taking 70 µs gives about 58 MB/s, no matter the link generation.

Solution

For the lab system: Gen4 x4, MPS 256 → encoded ≈ 7.88 GB/s, practical ≈ 7.0 GB/s. A healthy Gen4 SSD measuring 6.8–7.0 GB/s is at 97–100 %. At MPS 128 the practical ceiling falls to ≈ 6.4 GB/s; at x2 to ≈ 3.5 GB/s. If the device is rated well below the link (for example a 5 GB/s drive), the device is the limit, and your measurement compares against the device rating instead. The QD1 random-read challenge shows throughput limited by per-command latency, dominated by NAND and controller time; the link generation contributes only a few hundred nanoseconds.

What a senior engineer notices

  • Whether the benchmark is CPU-bound (a single fio job can saturate a core before the link).
  • Whether the SSD throttles thermally partway through a longer run.
  • Whether the measured number is suspiciously close to a round fraction of the ceiling.

Failure variants

  • Measured above the computed ceiling: you mixed MiB/s with MB/s, or the data came from a cache.
  • Measured exactly half: check width (x2) or generation (one lower).
  • Wildly varying run to run: check for link Recovery events and AER correctables during the run.

Extension

Repeat on a system with a Switch between Root Port and SSD and with several SSDs active at once. Compute the Switch upstream ceiling and compare.

Exercises

  1. Compute the encoded per-direction bandwidth of Gen2 x8, Gen3 x16 and Gen5 x8.
  2. Why did Gen3 raise the rate only from 5.0 to 8.0 GT/s yet nearly double usable bandwidth?
  3. For Gen6 x4, compute raw flit bandwidth and TLP-byte bandwidth per direction.
  4. A link vendor quotes "128 GB/s" for Gen5 x16. Reconcile this with the ≈ 63 GB/s in the table.
  5. Compute TLP efficiency for a 512-byte payload with a 3 DW header and ECRC enabled.
  6. An NVMe controller reports VS = 2.4 and the link reports 8GT/s. What can you say about each number?
Answers

1: Gen2 x8 = 5 × 8 × 0.8 ÷ 8 = 4.0 GB/s; Gen3 x16 = 8 × 16 × 128/130 ÷ 8 ≈ 15.75 GB/s; Gen5 x8 ≈ 31.5 GB/s. 2: Encoding overhead fell from 20 % to ≈ 1.5 %: 8.0 × 0.985 = 7.88 Gb/s versus 5.0 × 0.8 = 4.0 Gb/s per lane. 3: 64 × 4 ÷ 8 = 32 GB/s of flits; × 236/256 ≈ 29.5 GB/s of TLP bytes. 4: 32 GT/s × 16 ÷ 8 = 64 GB/s raw per direction, × 2 directions = 128 GB/s bidirectional raw; with encoding ≈ 63 GB/s per direction. 5: 512 ÷ (512 + 12 + 4 + 8) = 512/536 ≈ 95.5 %. 6: VS 2.4 is the NVMe Base Specification revision the controller claims; 8 GT/s is the negotiated PCIe link speed (Gen3). They are independent; you cannot infer one from the other.

Quiz

  1. Which generations use 8b/10b encoding?

    • Gen1 and Gen2
    • Gen1 only
    • Gen1 to Gen3
    • All generations
    Answer and reasoning

    Gen1 (2.5 GT/s) and Gen2 (5.0 GT/s). Gen3–Gen5 use 128b/130b; Gen6–Gen7 use Flit Mode with PAM4.

  2. What is the approximate encoded per-direction bandwidth of Gen4 x4?

    • 4 GB/s
    • 16 GB/s
    • 7.88 GB/s
    • 64 GB/s
    Answer and reasoning

    16 × 4 × 128/130 ÷ 8 ≈ 7.88 GB/s.

  3. Why did Gen6 adopt PAM4?

    • To remove the need for equalisation
    • To double bit rate without doubling the Nyquist frequency
    • To reduce power by half
    • To be compatible with Ethernet
    Answer and reasoning

    PAM4 carries 2 bits per symbol, so 64 GT/s needs 32 GBaud, the same Nyquist frequency as Gen5 NRZ.

  4. What size is a Flit Mode flit?

    • 64 bytes
    • 128 bytes
    • 130 bits
    • 256 bytes
    Answer and reasoning

    256 bytes, of which 236 carry TLP bytes, 6 Data Link Layer payload, 8 CRC and 6 FEC.

  5. Which register reports the speeds a port supports as a bit vector?

    • Link Status
    • Link Capabilities 2
    • Device Control
    • NVMe CAP
    Answer and reasoning

    Link Capabilities 2's Supported Link Speeds Vector.

  6. Doubling the generation of an NVMe SSD's link roughly halves:

    • 4 KiB read latency
    • NAND read time
    • TLP serialisation time
    • Interrupt latency
    Answer and reasoning

    Only serialisation time halves. Overall latency is dominated by media, controller and host effects.

  7. Why is MPS relevant to throughput?

    Model answer

    Each TLP carries fixed header and framing overhead; MPS caps payload per TLP, so smaller MPS means more TLPs, more overhead per byte and lower efficiency (≈ 84 % at 128 B versus ≈ 91 % at 256 B for 4 DW headers in non-flit mode).

  8. A Gen5 SSD in a Gen5 slot trains at 16 GT/s. List three possible reasons.

    Model answer

    A Gen4-rated element in the path (riser, cable, retimer) causing equalisation to fail at 32 GT/s; firmware limiting Target Link Speed; marginal channel causing fallback after errors; one side's firmware/BIOS configuration; device firmware issue. Check Link Control 2, Link Status 2 equalisation bits and error counters.

  9. Explain the difference between "PCIe 5.0 compliant" and "runs at 32 GT/s".

    Model answer

    "PCIe 5.0 compliant" claims conformance to the Revision 5.0 rule book, which permits but does not require 32 GT/s support; "runs at 32 GT/s" is a negotiated link speed. A device can be compliant with a revision while supporting a lower maximum rate.

Interview questions

  1. (Beginner) What is GT/s and why not just say Gb/s?

    Model answer

    Gigatransfers per second: symbols on the wire per second per lane. It includes encoding overhead, so it is not the same as data bits per second.

  2. (Beginner) What is the per-direction bandwidth of Gen3 x4?

    Model answer

    8 × 4 × 128/130 ÷ 8 ≈ 3.94 GB/s encoded; roughly 3.5 GB/s of data in practice.

  3. (Intermediate) Explain 128b/130b and its trade-offs versus 8b/10b.

    Model answer

    2-bit sync header per 128-bit block; scrambling supplies transitions and DC balance; tokens frame packets. Overhead drops from 20 % to ≈ 1.5 %, but error detection at the symbol level is weaker, block alignment is needed, and scrambler-related corner cases must be handled.

  4. (Intermediate) Why did Flit Mode arrive together with PAM4?

    Model answer

    PAM4's higher raw error rate requires FEC; FEC works on fixed-size codewords; fixed-size flits make FEC, CRC and retry efficient and predictable, and also allow more efficient packing of small TLPs and link-layer information.

  5. (Senior) A customer moves from Gen4 to Gen5 servers and sees no improvement in their database's p99 latency. Explain.

    Model answer

    Database p99 latency for small random I/O is dominated by media access, controller firmware, queueing and host software. Link serialisation is tens of nanoseconds of a tens-to-hundreds of microseconds request. Gen5 helps bandwidth-bound workloads. If anything, power-state exit latency or retimers in the Gen5 platform could add latency. Measure where time goes before attributing it.

  6. (Senior) How would you validate that a new Gen5 platform's NVMe slots are production-ready?

    Model answer

    Train-to-speed tests across thousands of power cycles and resets with multiple SSD vendors; lane margining across temperature and voltage; long soak with traffic and AER monitoring (zero or bounded correctable errors); hot-plug tests where applicable; fallback behaviour when a degraded component is inserted; compare throughput to the derived ceiling; capture and review training (equalisation) on samples.

  • Senior engineer: Explain the engineering reasons for 128b/130b at Gen3 and PAM4 plus FEC at Gen6, and what each did to latency and error behaviour.
Model explanation (junior engineer)

16 GT/s per lane × 4 lanes = 64 Gb/s raw. 128b/130b keeps 128 of every 130 bits: 63.0 Gb/s, which is 7.88 GB/s per direction. Each 256-byte payload TLP also carries a 16-byte header and about 8 bytes of framing, sequence number and LCRC, so 256/280 ≈ 91 % of that carries data: ≈ 7.2 GB/s. Acks, flow-control updates and SKP ordered sets take a few percent more, leaving ≈ 7.0 GB/s.

Chapter summary

  • Gen1 2.5 GT/s and Gen2 5.0 GT/s use 8b/10b (80 %); Gen3 8.0, Gen4 16.0 and Gen5 32.0 GT/s use 128b/130b (≈ 98.5 %); Gen6 64.0 and Gen7 128.0 GT/s use PAM4 with Flit Mode and FEC.
  • Encoded bandwidth = rate × lanes × efficiency ÷ 8, per direction. Gen4 x4 ≈ 7.88 GB/s; Gen5 x4 ≈ 15.75 GB/s.
  • Data throughput is further reduced by TLP headers, framing, DLLPs and SKPs; MPS matters.
  • Generations change bandwidth far more than latency.
  • PCIe revision, PCIe link speed and NVMe revision are three independent numbers.
