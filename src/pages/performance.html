Performance engineering for NVMe over PCI Express (PCIe) means turning vague complaints ("it's slow") into numbers you can predict, measure and explain. This page gives you the vocabulary and the equations (latency, IOPS, throughput, queue depth, Little's law), a full treatment of PCIe bandwidth calculations from link rate through encoding and packet overhead to NVMe IOPS ceilings, latency budgets, tail latency, CPU overhead, and a disciplined fio methodology.

Why this part exists

A drive that works correctly but delivers half its expected throughput, or whose 99.9th-percentile latency is ten times its average, is a failed product for many customers. Performance problems are harder than functional ones in one specific way: there is rarely an error message. Nothing is "abnormal" in the protocol sense; every packet is legal. You find the cause by knowing what the numbers should be and finding which ceiling the system hit first.

That requires three skills this page builds: computing theoretical ceilings (link, protocol, controller, media, host CPU) from first principles; measuring honestly (preconditioning, steady state, percentiles, open versus closed loop); and connecting measurements to mechanism using traces, exactly as in Debugging Methodology.

Learning objectives

  • Define latency, IOPS (input/output operations per second), throughput, queue depth, outstanding commands, CPU overhead, PCIe bandwidth, controller bandwidth, NAND bandwidth and tail latency, and relate them with equations.
  • Apply Little's law to predict the queue depth needed for a target IOPS, and the IOPS achievable at a given queue depth.
  • Compute theoretical and effective PCIe bandwidth for Gen3 x4, Gen4 x4, Gen5 x4, Gen5 x8 and Gen6 x4, accounting for encoding, Transaction Layer Packet (TLP) overhead, Data Link Layer Packets (DLLPs) and payload size.
  • Derive a link-limited IOPS ceiling for 4 KiB random reads and writes, including the non-data traffic each NVMe command generates.
  • Explain why sequential and random workloads differ, why queue depth matters, why high IOPS can coexist with bad latency, and why high bandwidth can hide latency problems.
  • Build a latency budget for a single command and locate where time goes using a trace.
  • Design a fio experiment that produces trustworthy numbers, and report them honestly.

The story

Imagine a supermarket. Latency is how long you wait from joining a queue to walking out with your bag. Throughput is how many bags leave the shop every minute. If there is one checkout and the cashier takes one minute per customer, the shop serves sixty customers an hour, and if only one person is in the shop, that person waits one minute. Open ten checkouts and the shop serves six hundred an hour, but only if there are at least ten customers ready. With only one customer, nine cashiers sit idle: more checkouts do not help a lonely shopper.

And if a hundred customers arrive at once for ten checkouts, the shop is busy and serves lots of people per hour, but some customers wait ten minutes. The shop looks great on the "bags per hour" chart, and terrible to the unlucky customer at the back.

A motorway toll plaza is a good adult model. The road (PCIe link) has a speed limit and a number of lanes; each vehicle (TLP) has a fixed-size cab (header and framing) and a variable trailer (payload). Short trailers waste road space on cabs; long trailers use it efficiently. The toll booths (controller and NAND) each take time per vehicle. The cars in the plaza at any moment (outstanding commands) equal the arrival rate times the time each spends inside: that is Little's law.

Both pictures hide important details. NVMe commands are not served first-come-first-served; the controller reorders internally, and a single command can wait behind background work (garbage collection) that no customer queued. The PCIe "road" is two independent one-way roads (each direction has its own bandwidth), so a read workload and a write workload load different directions. Vehicles also need space for traffic going the other way: acknowledgements and flow-control updates travel in the reverse direction and take a little capacity from it. And the "toll booths" are not identical: a NAND die busy erasing can block a read for milliseconds.

Throughput and IOPS

T = R × S
T
throughput in bytes per second
R
IOPS: commands completed per second
S

Example: 1 000 000 IOPS × 4 096 bytes = 4.096 GB/s. 7.0 GB/s at 128 KiB (131 072 bytes) per command is about 53 400 IOPS.

Little's law

L = λ × W
L
average number of commands outstanding (in flight) in the system being measured
λ
average completion rate (IOPS) of that system, in commands per second
W
average time each command spends in that system (latency), in seconds

Little's law holds for any stable system in steady state, whatever the distribution of arrivals and service times. Rearranged, it is the most useful equation in storage performance:

λ = L / W
λ
achievable IOPS
L
outstanding commands you actually keep in flight
W
average latency at that load

At QD 1 with 80 µs latency: λ = 1 / 80 µs = 12 500 IOPS (about 51 MB/s at 4 KiB). To reach 1 000 000 IOPS at 80 µs you need L = 1 000 000 × 0.000 080 = 80 commands in flight. If latency rises to 160 µs as the device gets busy, you need 160.

Utilisation and queueing

U = λ × St / c
U
utilisation of a resource, between 0 and 1
λ
arrival (and, in steady state, completion) rate at that resource, per second
St
average service time of one command at the resource, in seconds
c
number of identical servers that work in parallel (for example independent NAND dies)

As U approaches 1, waiting time grows sharply: in simple queueing models the average wait is proportional to U / (1 − U). At 50% utilisation waiting roughly equals service time; at 90% it is nine times service time. That non-linearity is why latency explodes near saturation even though IOPS barely rises. Real SSDs are not simple queues, but the shape survives Derived.

The central concept on the ladder: queue depth and Little's law

One cashier serves one customer at a time. Ten cashiers can serve ten, but only if ten customers are waiting. If you send customers one by one, the shop is slow however many cashiers it has.

An SSD contains many independent units (NAND dies, planes, channels, controller cores). It reaches its rated IOPS only when enough commands are outstanding to keep them busy. Little's law relates outstanding commands, IOPS and latency.

Each 4 KiB random read occupies one die for its read time (tens of microseconds) and one channel for its transfer. A drive with 128 dies can have up to about 128 reads progressing in parallel on its media; the controller needs commands in its internal queues to schedule them.

The NVMe Base Specification defines queues, queue sizes (up to CAP.MQES + 1 entries per I/O queue) and the number of queues (negotiated through the Number of Queues feature). It does not define how many commands a controller processes in parallel or in what order; that is Implementation-dependent.

On the link, outstanding commands appear as SQ tail doorbell values running ahead of the SQ head reported in CQEs; every command produces SQE fetches, data TLPs, a CQE and (unless coalesced) an MSI-X message.

In a trace, count doorbell-submitted commands minus completions over time to plot in-flight depth. If the in-flight depth is low while the application claims QD 128, the host is the bottleneck.

Performance debugging: compare measured IOPS with L / W. If they agree and IOPS is low, either L is too small (host not submitting enough) or W too large (device or path slow). Find which.

The ceiling model: host, link, controller, media

Every command passes through a chain of resources. Achieved performance is the minimum of their ceilings, and whichever is lowest is the binding ceiling. When you remove it, the next one binds.

Why queue depth matters

As you raise queue depth, IOPS rises almost linearly while the device has idle parallelism; then it flattens at the binding ceiling. Latency stays flat while IOPS rises, then grows linearly with QD once IOPS is flat, exactly as Little's law demands (W = L / λ with λ fixed).

The knee is the operating point most production systems want: near maximum IOPS without paying the latency of deep queues. Benchmarks that report only QD 256 numbers show the ceiling but not the price.

Where outstanding commands come from matters too. Thirty-two jobs at QD 4 and one job at QD 128 both offer 128 outstanding commands, but the first spreads them over 32 NVMe queues and CPUs, while the second puts them on one CPU, which may become the binding ceiling.

Why high IOPS can still have bad latency

  • Little's law: at a fixed IOPS ceiling, every extra outstanding command adds latency. 1 M IOPS at QD 256 means an average latency of 256 µs, whereas the device might deliver 80 µs at QD 64 with 800 k IOPS.
  • Averages hide tails: 1 M IOPS with p99.9 at 5 ms means 1 000 commands per second wait over 5 ms. A database transaction waiting for one of them stalls.
  • Background work: garbage collection, wear levelling, internal metadata flushes, thermal throttling: brief stalls that barely move average IOPS but dominate tails.
  • Read-behind-write and erase interference: a read to a die that is programming or erasing waits (unless the controller supports suspend/resume of those operations Implementation-dependent).
  • Batching: interrupt coalescing and doorbell batching increase IOPS per CPU but add latency to individual commands.

Why high bandwidth can hide latency problems

With large transfers and deep queues, a link can stay full even when individual commands are slow: Little's law again, with large S. A sequential read at 7 GB/s with 128 KiB commands and QD 32 means W = L / λ = 32 / 53 400 ≈ 600 µs per command. If one command in a hundred takes 10 ms because of an internal stall, the other 31 outstanding commands keep the link busy and the throughput graph barely moves. The application that needed that block waits 10 ms.

Bandwidth measurements also hide per-command overheads: a configuration with 64-byte read completions (see bandwidth calculations) may still saturate a Gen4 x4 link for writes if the drive cannot write faster than the link's reduced efficiency anyway. The problem appears only when the drive is upgraded, or when latency of small writes is measured.

Always report throughput together with the latency distribution of the commands that produced it. "7 GB/s" is not a result; "7 GB/s at 128 KiB, QD 32, one job, p50 590 µs, p99 1.1 ms, p99.9 9.8 ms, steady state after preconditioning" is.

Step 3: DLLP, ordered-set and Flit overhead

  • Ack DLLPs acknowledge received TLPs; they travel in the direction opposite to the data they acknowledge. A DLLP occupies 8 bytes on the wire (6-byte DLLP plus 2 bytes of framing). Receivers coalesce Acks; the frequency is implementation-dependent.
  • UpdateFC DLLPs return flow-control credits, also in the opposite direction to the traffic consuming them, also 8 bytes each.
  • SKP ordered sets are inserted periodically for clock compensation; on 128b/130b links their cost is a few tenths of a percent.

The data direction of a busy NVMe link therefore carries DLLPs for the reverse traffic (which is light), while the reverse direction carries Acks and credits for the data. A practical engineering allowance is about 2% of the data direction for DLLPs and ordered sets Implementation-dependent: the real figure depends on Ack coalescing and the mix of traffic.

Flit mode (Gen6): each 256-byte flit carries 236 bytes of TLP space, 6 bytes of data-link payload (which carries the acknowledgement and flow-control information that DLLPs carried before), 8 bytes of CRC and 6 bytes of forward error correction Uncertain (field sizes from published 6.0 descriptions; confirm in the specification). TLPs no longer carry their own framing token or LCRC. TLP headers in Flit mode use a different format; for a 64-bit memory write we assume about 16 bytes of header, which you should verify. Flit efficiency is 236 / 256 = 92.2% before TLP headers.

Step 5: the other direction, and why writes differ

For an NVMe Write, the controller reads host memory: it sends Memory Read requests (MRd, up to the Max Read Request Size, MRRS) and the root complex returns CplD TLPs. Data now flows host→device in completions with a 3 DW header (20 bytes overhead), but the completer chooses how to split them: by Read Completion Boundary (RCB) and up to MPS. A root complex that returns 256-byte completions gives 92.8% efficiency; one that returns 64-byte completions gives 76.2%, capping Gen4 x4 writes at roughly 7.88 × 0.762 × 0.98 ≈ 5.9 GB/s even if the NAND could go faster Implementation-dependent. The device also needs enough tags and completion buffer space to keep enough read requests outstanding to cover the round-trip latency (bandwidth-delay product).

Nreq ≥ Beff × trt / M
Nreq
number of outstanding read requests the device must keep in flight (bounded by its tags and completion buffer credits)
Beff
target effective bandwidth in bytes per second
trt
round-trip time from MRd to its last CplD, in seconds

Example: 7 GB/s × 1.5 µs / 512 B ≈ 21 outstanding 512-byte reads. With 2 µs round trips through a switch and IOMMU, ≈ 27. A device limited to fewer outstanding reads cannot reach link rate for writes, whatever the link speed (see Chapter 13 — Tags).

Tail latency

Percentiles

The p-th percentile is the latency below which p% of commands complete. Report at least p50, p99, p99.9 and max, with the number of samples (p99.99 from 10 000 samples is a single command).

Fan-out amplification

Pslow = 1 − (1 − q)N
Pslow
probability that a request touching N independent drives or commands waits for at least one slow command
q
probability that a single command is "slow" (for example 0.01 for the p99 tail)
N
number of commands the request must wait for (fan-out)

With q = 0.01 and N = 100, Pslow ≈ 63%: a drive's p99 becomes the system's typical case. This is why storage arrays and distributed databases care about p99.9 and beyond.

Causes of tails

  • Garbage collection and background media management (writes and mixed workloads).
  • Reads colliding with programs or erases on the same die.
  • Internal read retry and stronger error correction on worn or retention-stressed blocks.
  • Thermal throttling; power-state exits (NVMe power states, ASPM L1).
  • Link Recovery events and replays (see the Failure Library).
  • Host: CPU preemption, interrupt routing to busy CPUs, memory reclaim, lock contention.

Coordinated omission

A closed-loop benchmark (fixed QD: submit a new command only when one completes) slows its own submission rate when the device stalls, so it records few samples during the stall and the tail looks better than users would experience. For latency service levels, also test open-loop: submit at a fixed or Poisson arrival rate regardless of completions (fio's rate_iops with rate_process=poisson), and measure latency from the intended submission time where the tool supports it.

CPU overhead

IOPScpu = n × f / c
IOPScpu
maximum IOPS the host CPUs can drive
n
number of CPU cores doing I/O
f
core clock frequency in cycles per second
c
CPU cycles consumed per I/O across submission, interrupt and completion

Example (illustrative): at 3 GHz and 6 000 cycles per I/O, one core drives 500 k IOPS; a 3.45 M IOPS Gen5 ceiling needs about seven cores busy only with I/O. Techniques that reduce c:

  • Per-core queues: NVMe's many queues let each CPU own an SQ/CQ pair with no lock.
  • Interrupt coalescing (the Interrupt Coalescing feature, Feature Identifier 08h in the NVMe Base Specification): fewer interrupts per completion, at a latency cost.
  • Polling (io_uring polled I/O, Linux poll queues): no interrupt; burns CPU to cut latency. Worth it for very-low-latency devices.
  • Batching: one doorbell for many SQEs; reap many CQEs per interrupt.
  • Avoiding layers: no filesystem, no I/O scheduler (none), registered buffers and files in io_uring.
  • NUMA locality: buffers and threads on the socket attached to the device's root port.
  • IOMMU cost: mapping and unmapping per I/O costs cycles; strict versus lazy invalidation policies trade safety for speed.

fio methodology

Expert lab Any fio job that writes to a raw block device (--filename=/dev/nvme0n1 with a write workload) destroys all data on that namespace. Use a dedicated test drive. Read-only jobs (--rw=read or randread) are Safe lab for data, but still load the system.

The method

  1. Define the question. "What is p99 latency at 200 k IOPS 70/30 random read/write 4 KiB?" is testable; "how fast is the drive?" is not.
  2. Fix and record the configuration. Drive model, firmware, capacity, fill level; host CPU, NUMA node, kernel, fio version; link speed and width, MPS, MRRS; power settings; scheduler; number of queues.
  3. Precondition. SSD performance depends on history. Fresh-out-of-box results are unrepresentative for writes. Follow a recognised method such as the SNIA Solid State Storage Performance Test Specification (PTS): purge, fill the drive (for example twice its capacity with sequential writes), then run the target workload until steady state. Uncertain exact steady-state criteria: see the SNIA PTS.
  4. Reach steady state. Measure in rounds; accept results only when successive rounds agree within a stated tolerance.
  5. Sweep, don't spot-check. Queue depth 1…256 and jobs 1…N; block sizes; read/write mixes. Find the knee.
  6. Measure latency distributions, not just averages; log latency over time to see periodic stalls.
  7. Repeat runs and report variation.
  8. Explain each result with the ceiling model; an unexplained result is not finished.

Code: bandwidth and IOPS ceiling calculator

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Derived PCIe payload bandwidth and NVMe 4 KiB IOPS ceilings (teaching model).
Standard library only. Assumptions are explicit; change them to match your system."""

LINKS = [  # name, GT/s per lane, lanes, encoding efficiency, flit mode
    ("Gen3 x4", 8, 4, 128 / 130, False),
    ("Gen4 x4", 16, 4, 128 / 130, False),
    ("Gen5 x4", 32, 4, 128 / 130, False),
    ("Gen5 x8", 32, 8, 128 / 130, False),
    ("Gen6 x4", 64, 4, 1.0, True),
]
MPS = 256                 # Max Payload Size, bytes
OVH_MWR64 = 24            # non-flit: 4 framing/seq + 16 header + 4 LCRC
OVH_MWR32 = 20            # non-flit: 4 + 12 + 4
FLIT_TLP_FRACTION = 236 / 256   # assumption from published Flit descriptions
FLIT_HDR_MWR64 = 16       # assumption: verify in the specification
FLIT_HDR_MWR32 = 12       # assumption
DLLP_FRACTION = 0.02      # non-flit allowance for Ack/UpdateFC/SKP (implementation-dependent)

def read_cmd_bytes(flit, io=4096):
    """Device-to-host wire bytes for one NVMe Read of `io` bytes."""
    o64 = FLIT_HDR_MWR64 if flit else OVH_MWR64
    o32 = FLIT_HDR_MWR32 if flit else OVH_MWR32
    tlps = -(-io // MPS)                     # ceiling division
    data = io + tlps * o64                   # data MWr TLPs
    cqe = 16 + o64                           # 16-byte CQE
    msix = 4 + o32                           # MSI-X write below 4 GB
    sqe_fetch = o64                          # MRd64 has no payload
    return data + cqe + msix + sqe_fetch

print(f"{'link':8} {'after enc':>10} {'payload BW':>11} {'4K rd IOPS':>11}")
for name, gts, lanes, enc, flit in LINKS:
    b_link = gts * 1e9 * lanes * enc / 8          # bytes/s per direction
    frac = FLIT_TLP_FRACTION if flit else 1.0
    o64 = FLIT_HDR_MWR64 if flit else OVH_MWR64
    eta = MPS / (MPS + o64)
    loss = 0.005 if flit else DLLP_FRACTION
    payload = b_link * frac * eta * (1 - loss)
    iops = b_link * frac / read_cmd_bytes(flit)   # upper bound, no DLLP loss
    print(f"{name:8} {b_link/1e9:9.2f}G {payload/1e9:10.2f}G {iops/1e6:10.2f}M")

# Little's law helper
lat_us, target_iops = 80, 1_000_000
print(f"\nTo sustain {target_iops:,} IOPS at {lat_us} us you need "
      f"{target_iops * lat_us / 1e6:.0f} commands in flight.")

Expected output (rounded): Gen3 x4 3.94G / 3.53G / 0.86M; Gen4 x4 7.88G / 7.06G / 1.72M; Gen5 x4 15.75G / 14.12G / 3.45M; Gen5 x8 31.51G / 28.23G / 6.90M; Gen6 x4 32.00G / 27.63G / 6.68M; and 80 commands in flight. These are derived teaching numbers, not measurements.

Worked examples

Example 1: is this Gen4 drive link-bound?

Measured: 4 KiB random read, 16 jobs × QD 32, 1.15 M IOPS, mean latency 445 µs. Little's law: 16 × 32 = 512 offered; λ × W = 1.15 M × 445 µs ≈ 512 ✓ (the host kept the queues full). Link ceiling ≈ 1.72 M, so the link is at 67%: not binding. Lowering QD to 8 per job gives 1.12 M IOPS at 114 µs: the device ceiling is ≈ 1.15 M and deep queues only added latency. Conclusion: controller-bound; operate near the knee (QD 8 per job).

Example 2: a Gen5 x4 drive reads at 9.8 GB/s

Ceiling ≈ 14.1 GB/s. Check: LnkSta 32 GT/s x4 ✓; MPS 128 ✗. With MPS 128, ηTLP = 128/152 = 84.2%, ceiling ≈ 15.75 × 0.842 × 0.98 ≈ 13.0 GB/s: still above 9.8. Check jobs: one job at QD 64 using 100% of one CPU: CPU-bound submission. Four jobs: 12.6 GB/s. Then MPS 256 (platform setting): 13.9 GB/s. Two ceilings, removed in order.

Example 3: Gen6 x4 marketing claim

"Gen6 x4 gives 32 GB/s." Raw, per direction, before Flit overhead: yes. Payload: about 27.6–27.8 GB/s under our assumptions, and a drive will reach that only if its controller and NAND can. Correct statement: "up to about 27–28 GB/s of payload per direction, link-limited, derived".

Common mistakes

  • Adding both directions ("Gen4 x4 = 16 GB/s") and comparing with a one-direction workload.
  • Using GT/s as if it were GB/s, or forgetting encoding.
  • Mixing GB and GiB (a 7% error between 7.0 GB/s and 6.52 GiB/s).
  • Testing a fresh drive and publishing write performance that collapses after an hour.
  • Reporting average latency only.
  • Assuming iodepth × numjobs is achieved in-flight depth without checking with Little's law.
  • Running fio on a filesystem with buffered I/O and calling it device performance.
  • Ignoring NUMA: buffers on the remote socket can cost tens of percent at high IOPS.
  • Treating a closed-loop benchmark's tail as the tail users experience.

Senior engineer insight

Seniors start every performance investigation by writing down the ceilings before running anything: link (per direction, with real MPS and completion sizes), controller (datasheet and prior measurements), media (if known), CPU (cycles per I/O × cores). Then they run one sweep and see which ceiling the curve flattens against. When a measurement does not match any ceiling, they treat it as a bug in their model and go and find the missing term, often a per-command overhead nobody counted (PRP list fetches, small completions, interrupt storms) or a host effect (NUMA, IOMMU, power states).

Challenge

Predict the IOPS and mean latency at 1 024 outstanding commands without measuring.

Hint

Past the ceiling, λ is constant; W = L / λ.

Solution

1. Row by row: 52 000 × 77 µs = 4.0 ✓; 198 000 × 81 µs = 16.0 ✓; 690 000 × 93 µs = 64.2 ✓; 905 000 × 141 µs = 127.6 ✓; 935 000 × 274 µs = 256.2 ✓; 938 000 × 546 µs = 512.1 ✓. The host achieved the offered depth. If λ × W were much lower, the host would not be keeping commands in flight (CPU-bound submission, block-layer limits).

2. Between 64 and 128 outstanding: IOPS gains 31% for 52% more latency from 64→128, then nothing.

3. No: the 4 KiB link ceiling on Gen4 x4 is ≈ 1.72 M; 938 k is 55%. The controller (or media) binds at ≈ 940 k.

4. From 128 to 512 outstanding, the mean grows about 3.9× (141 → 546 µs) while p99.9 grows about 9× (520 → 4 800 µs). Tails grow faster than the mean because queueing inside the controller amplifies variability: every command queued behind a slow event (a busy die, a garbage-collection pause) inherits its delay, and deeper queues put more commands behind each such event.

Challenge: λ ≈ 938 k; W ≈ 1 024 / 938 000 ≈ 1.09 ms mean latency; IOPS unchanged.

What a senior engineer notices

That the "rated" IOPS of a drive is usually measured at a depth no latency-sensitive application would use, and that the right production setting here is about 64–128 outstanding commands, not 512.

Failure variants

  • λ × W far below offered depth: a host bottleneck (try more jobs, check CPU).
  • IOPS ceiling matches the link ceiling: check speed, width, MPS.
  • IOPS falls at high QD: possible controller overload behaviour or thermal throttling; log temperature.

Extension

Repeat with an open-loop job at 80% of the measured ceiling (rate_iops, rate_process=poisson) and compare p99.9 with the closed-loop result at similar IOPS.

    • 1 000

    • 100 000
    • 1 000 000
    Answer and reasoning

    λ = L / W = 1 / 0.000 1 s = 10 000 IOPS.

  2. What is the usable bandwidth per direction of Gen4 x4 after encoding but before TLP overhead?

    • 8.0 GB/s
    • 16.0 GB/s
    • about 7.88 GB/s
    • about 6.4 GB/s
    Answer and reasoning

    16 GT/s × 4 lanes × 128/130 / 8 ≈ 7.88 GB/s.

  3. For an NVMe Read, which TLP type carries the data and in which direction?

    • MWr, device→host
    • CplD, host→device
    • MRd, device→host
    • CplD, device→host
    Answer and reasoning

    The controller writes the read data into host memory with Memory Write requests. For an NVMe Write it reads host memory, and data returns host→device in CplD.

  4. Payload efficiency of a 64-bit MWr with 128-byte payload on a non-Flit link?

    • 91%
    • 84%
    • 76%
    • 95%
    Answer and reasoning

    128 / (128 + 24) = 84.2%.

  5. Why can 64-byte read completions from the root complex cap write throughput?

    Model answer

    Each CplD carries 20 bytes of overhead; at 64 bytes payload efficiency is ~76%, so the host→device direction carries less data per second (≈ 5.9 GB/s payload on Gen4 x4 after DLLP allowance).

  6. IOPS stays at 900 k while QD doubles from 128 to 256. What happens to mean latency?

    Model answer

    It roughly doubles (W = L / λ): from about 142 µs to about 284 µs.

  7. What is coordinated omission and how do you mitigate it?

    Model answer

    A closed-loop load generator submits less during stalls, under-sampling them, so measured tails look better than real users see. Use open-loop arrival (fixed or Poisson rate) and, where possible, measure from intended submission time.

  8. A request fans out to 50 drives, each with a 1% chance of a slow response. Probability the request is slow?

    • 1%
    • about 5%
    • about 39%
    • 50%
    Answer and reasoning

    1 − 0.9950 ≈ 0.395.

  9. Why should you precondition before measuring write performance?

    Model answer

    A fresh drive has free erased blocks and no garbage collection; performance falls once it must reclaim space. Steady-state numbers represent long-term behaviour.

  10. What two Gen6 changes make its overhead calculation different from Gen5?

    Model answer

    No 128b/130b line code (Flit mode with FEC and CRC inside 256-byte flits instead), and TLPs no longer carry per-TLP framing tokens and LCRC; acknowledgement and credit information rides in the flit rather than in separate DLLPs.

Interview questions

  1. Beginner: Explain the difference between IOPS and throughput.

    Model answer

    IOPS counts commands per second; throughput counts bytes per second. Throughput = IOPS × bytes per command. Small random I/O stresses IOPS; large sequential I/O stresses throughput.

  2. Beginner: What is Little's law?

    Model answer

    L = λW: average outstanding = completion rate × average time in system. It links queue depth, IOPS and latency in any stable system.

  3. Intermediate: Walk me through Gen5 x4 effective bandwidth.

    Model answer

    32 GT/s × 4 × 128/130 / 8 ≈ 15.75 GB/s per direction; MWr with 256 B payload and 24 B overhead ≈ 91.4% → 14.4 GB/s; minus ~2% DLLP/SKP ≈ 14.1 GB/s. Real drives top out around 14 GB/s.

  4. Intermediate: Why do random writes slow down over time?

    Model answer

    As free blocks run out, garbage collection must relocate valid data before erasing; write amplification rises and steals media bandwidth from host writes. Over-provisioning and workload pattern change the steady state.

  5. Advanced: A drive achieves rated sequential read but writes are 30% below rating on one platform only. Hypotheses?

    Model answer

    Host→device efficiency: small read completions from that root complex, smaller MRRS, higher round-trip latency (switch, IOMMU) limiting outstanding reads (bandwidth-delay product), MPS mismatch. Check completion sizes and gaps in a trace, and lspci MaxReadReq.

  6. Senior: How do you set a latency SLA for an NVMe-backed service?

    Model answer

    Characterise the latency distribution under open-loop load at the expected rate on preconditioned drives, including background activity and mixed traffic; account for fan-out; set percentile targets with headroom; monitor percentiles in production and validate every firmware/kernel change with the same test.

Summary

Throughput = IOPS × size; Little's law links outstanding commands, IOPS and latency; achieved performance is the minimum of host, link, controller and media ceilings for the specific workload. PCIe effective bandwidth follows from rate × width × encoding, then TLP overhead (24 bytes per 64-bit MWr, 20 per CplD on non-Flit links), then DLLPs and ordered sets; Gen6 Flit mode replaces line coding and per-TLP framing with 256-byte flits. Reads and writes stress different directions and different overheads. Report latency distributions, precondition, find the knee, and explain every number.
