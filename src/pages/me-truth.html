This page teaches the habit that keeps every other skill in this course honest: knowing, for each sentence you say about NVM Express (NVMe) or PCI Express (PCIe), where it comes from and how sure you are allowed to be. You will practise labelling claims, ranking sources, pinning revisions, reading "shall/should/may", writing source notes, mapping a topic across several specifications, separating what an analyzer shows from what crossed the wire, and decoding packets, commands, queues, addresses and DMA without guessing.

Why this part exists

A protocol engineer's most valuable product is not knowledge. It is trustworthy statements. When you write "the controller returned Invalid Field in Command because PRP2 was not page-aligned", three other people will act on that sentence: a firmware engineer may change code, a validation engineer may write a test, a manager may tell a customer. If the sentence was half memory, half guess, all three act on a fiction.

Most wrong conclusions in storage debugging do not come from missing knowledge. They come from four quiet slips:

  • Promotion: an assumption ("tags are probably reused in order") silently becomes a fact two meetings later.
  • Version drift: something true in an older revision, an older kernel or one vendor's firmware is described as if it were universal and current.
  • Layer collapse: the NVMe specification is quoted for a PCIe packet detail, or the analyzer's label is quoted as if it were the specification.
  • Over-reading the trace: "the packet never happened" when the capture simply did not see it.

The master prompt that defines this course calls the cure a source-grounded mastery engine. The rules in it were written for the people writing the course; on this page each rule becomes a method you practise, with examples, counterexamples, exercises and hidden answers. Every other page in the course was written under these same rules, so learning them also teaches you how to read the course itself: what a Verified badge promises, what a Derived badge does not, and why synthetic traces are always labelled.

Learning objectives

  • Tag any technical claim with one of six truth labels and defend the choice.
  • Resolve a conflict between two sources using an explicit source hierarchy, and recognise when the "conflict" is really two different questions.
  • Write a specification baseline block and a source-notes block without inventing section numbers.
  • Classify specification text as requirement, recommendation, permission, informative note, example, rationale, implementation note, reserved, ignored or vendor-specific.
  • Follow an eleven-step primary-source workflow before teaching or reporting a specification detail.
  • Map one operation (an NVMe Read) across the Base specification, a command set, the PCIe transport, the PCIe Base Specification and the operating system.
  • Separate raw event, protocol decoding, analyzer user interface (UI) and engineer interpretation.
  • Decode a Transaction Layer Packet (TLP), an NVMe command, a queue, an address and a Direct Memory Access (DMA) transfer while saying exactly which fields support the conclusion, and say "insufficient trace evidence" when they do not.
  • Write an analogy boundary for any analogy you use.

The story

In a natural history museum, every dinosaur skeleton has a small card next to it. A good museum's cards are very careful. One says: "Real bones, found in 1902." Another says: "Plaster copy of real bones." A third says: "The skin colour is our best guess; nobody has ever seen it." A fourth says: "This tail is borrowed from a cousin species, because the real tail was never found."

The skeletons all look equally impressive. Without the cards, a child would believe the skin colour as much as the bones. The cards do not make the museum less exciting. They make it trustworthy: you know which parts to believe completely, which parts are clever reasoning, and which parts are a guess.

An engineer's notes need the same little cards on every sentence.

A newspaper's fact-checking desk works like this. A reporter's draft says "the bridge carries 40,000 cars a day". The checker asks: says who? The city's traffic count (primary source), a press release quoting it (secondary), or a commenter online (not a source)? Which year? Was it weekdays only? If the checker cannot confirm it, the sentence changes to "the city estimated in its 2024 survey that…" or it goes. Nobody at the desk thinks this slows journalism down; it is what makes the newspaper worth reading.

What maps well: labelling the provenance of each claim; preferring the primary source; pinning a date or revision.

What does not map: a museum card is written once; in engineering the "exhibit" changes with every specification revision, firmware release and kernel version, so labels must be re-checked. A newspaper usually has one primary source per fact; a single NVMe-over-PCIe behaviour can be split across four documents plus an implementation.

What it hides: in engineering, much of what you need is derived (reasoned from several verified facts), and derivation can be done well or badly. Neither analogy teaches you to check a chain of reasoning; this page does.

The six truth labels

The course uses six labels. Learn them as a vocabulary you use in your own notes, bug reports and design reviews, not just as decorations on this website.

The rule behind the labels

If a statement cannot be established from an official specification, official standards documentation, official vendor documentation, official Linux kernel documentation or source, or another clearly identified primary source, it must not be presented as a normative fact. That does not mean you must stay silent. It means you must say what kind of statement it is.

Never silently turn an assumption into a fact. The failure is almost never the first sentence; it is the third retelling, where "probably" quietly disappeared. When you copy a claim forward into a new document, copy its label with it.

Every sentence wears a little card saying "real bone", "plaster copy", "best guess" or "nobody knows".

Each technical claim gets one label: verified (with citation), derived (with reasoning), synthetic, implementation-dependent, version-dependent, or unknown.

Hardware behaviour splits naturally: register layouts and packet formats are usually specification-defined; queue fetch policy, internal buffering, retries and timing are usually implementation-dependent. Labelling forces you to notice which side of that line a claim sits on.

Specifications separate normative text (requirements using defined keywords) from informative text (notes, examples, rationale). A "verified" label is only as strong as the kind of text it cites: a verified quote from an informative example is not a requirement.

On the wire, the same packet can be described with labels at each step: the bytes are observed (verified by the capture), the decode is derived from the format, the NVMe meaning is derived from correlation, the timing pattern is implementation-dependent.

In a trace report, every row's "meaning" column is an interpretation. Mark which meanings are proven by fields in the row and which are inferred from neighbouring rows.

In debugging, labels tell you where to dig. A root-cause chain whose weakest link is "assumed" is a hypothesis, not a root cause. The label shows the next experiment to run.

Worked example: labelling a paragraph

Here is a paragraph an engineer might write after looking at a synthetic capture from the course's lab system (Lab SSD at 0000:01:00.0, Gen4 x4). Read it, then read the labelled version.

"The host rang the SQ1 doorbell, the drive fetched the command 900 ns later, which is slow for this controller, and then it read 4 KiB from the NAND and wrote it back with sixteen 256-byte writes because MPS is 256. The drive didn't send an interrupt, so the MSI-X vector must be broken."

The same paragraph, claim by claim
ClaimLabelWhy
The host wrote the SQ1 tail doorbell.DerivedThe capture shows a Memory Write to BAR0 + 0x1008; mapping that offset to "SQ1 tail doorbell" uses the doorbell formula and CAP.DSTRD = 0. Correct, but derived.
The drive fetched the command 900 ns later.DerivedThe capture shows a Memory Read from the SQ1 region; that it is this command's SQE follows from the address (slot 5 of SQ1) and the timing.
"…slow for this controller".UnknownNo baseline is cited. Slow compared with what? Needs a golden trace from the same device and configuration.

"The MSI-X vector must be broken".HypothesisOne of several (vector masked, function masked, interrupt coalescing, wrong vector mapping, capture gap). "Must" is unjustified.

The rewritten paragraph is longer but far more useful: "The host wrote SQ1's tail doorbell (BAR0 + 0x1008, value 6). 910 ns later the device read 64 bytes from SQ1 slot 5. We have no golden timing for this device, so we cannot yet call that slow. The device wrote 4 KiB to the data buffer as sixteen 256-byte Memory Writes (MPS = 256 allows this size; the split is the device's choice) and then a CQE to CQ1 slot 5. No MSI-X Memory Write is visible in the 30 s after the CQE; the capture was unfiltered and bidirectional. Candidate explanations: vector masked, function masked, coalescing, wrong vector mapping. Next check: MSI-X table and Pending Bit Array state."

Exercise: label these ten claims

  1. "An NVMe Submission Queue Entry is 64 bytes."

    Model answer

    Verified (NVMe Base Specification, command format). Strictly, the specification defines the SQ entry size through controller-reported and host-configured values (CC.IOSQES is a power of two); 64 bytes is the size used by the NVM command set over PCIe. A careful note says "64 bytes for the commands taught in this course; entry size is configured through CC.IOSQES".

  2. "Our SSD services 4 KiB random reads in about 80 µs."

    Model answer

    Implementation-dependent and a measurement: it needs the workload, queue depth, temperature, fill state and tool version to be meaningful.

  3. "Since the doorbell stride is 4 bytes, CQ1's head doorbell is at BAR0 + 0x100C."

    Model answer

    Derived from the doorbell offset formula and CAP.DSTRD = 0. Correct derivation; label it as derived because a different DSTRD changes the answer.

  4. "NVMe 2.4 adds rate limiting controls."

    Model answer

    Version-dependent; it is supported by the NVM Express announcement of the 2.4 set and must be confirmed field-by-field in the Base Specification 2.4 and the Revision Changes document. Any statement about its opcodes or fields is unknown until you read them there.

  5. "The kernel waits 30 seconds before timing out an I/O command."

    Model answer

    Version-dependent and configurable: it is a Linux driver default exposed as a module parameter in common kernels, not an NVMe requirement. Cite the kernel version and parameter you checked.

  6. "The completion arrived with tag 0x21, so it belongs to the MRd with tag 0x21."

    Model answer

    Derived, and incomplete: a completion matches a request by Requester ID and tag (the Transaction ID), within the window where that tag is outstanding. With only the tag shown, say the match is probable, not proven.

  7. "SSDs use a Flash Translation Layer."

    Model answer

    General industry knowledge, implementation-dependent in every detail. NVMe does not define a Flash Translation Layer; do not cite the NVMe specification for it.

  8. "This trace shows the device never sent a completion."

    Model answer

    Only acceptable with a coverage statement. Default wording: "No completion for CID 0x0005 is visible in the captured interval" plus the interval, direction coverage and filters.

  9. "Opcode 02h is a Read."

    Model answer

    Incomplete. 02h is Read in the NVM Command Set on an I/O queue; on the Admin queue 02h is Get Log Page. The claim needs the queue type and command set before it can be verified.

  10. "Gen4 x4 delivers 8 GB/s."

    Model answer

    Derived raw rate only (16 GT/s × 4 lanes ÷ 8 bits per byte ≈ 8 GB/s of symbols per direction). After 128b/130b encoding and packet overhead the payload rate is lower, and an application will see the device's own limits on top. Never present it as guaranteed throughput.

The source hierarchy

When two sources disagree, you need a rule for which wins. The course uses this order, highest first.

Source hierarchy for NVMe and PCIe questions
RankSourceAuthoritative forNot authoritative for
1Current official NVM Express specifications (Base, command sets, transports, Management Interface, Boot)What an NVMe host and controller are required, recommended or permitted to doPCIe packet rules; what a particular device does; what Linux does
2Current official PCI-SIG specifications available to you (PCI Express Base Specification and related ECNs)TLP/DLLP formats, ordering, flow control, link training, configuration space, Advanced Error Reporting (AER)NVMe semantics carried inside PCIe transactions
3Official Linux kernel documentation and sourceWhat a given kernel version actually does (queue allocation, timeouts, sysfs attributes)What the specifications require
4Official device, SSD or controller vendor documentationThat product's implementation choices and limitsOther products; the standard itself
5Official protocol-analyzer vendor documentationHow the tool captures, decodes, filters and displaysProtocol truth (the tool's view is a presentation)
6Peer-reviewed papers and standards publicationsMeasured behaviour under stated conditions; analysisNormative requirements
7High-quality technical booksExplanation and structure (often of an older revision)Current revision details
8Community material (blogs, forums, videos)Intuition, pointers to where to lookAnything you will put in a report without confirming

Community sources must never override a normative specification. If a popular blog says "the controller must post completions in order" and the specification does not require it, the blog is wrong for the standard, even if it is right for the device the blogger tested.

Most conflicts are two different questions

Before applying the ranking, check whether the sources actually answer the same question. Three common false conflicts:

  • Specification vs Linux source. The NVMe specification says what a controller may do; the Linux driver shows what Linux chooses to do. "The specification allows up to 65,535 I/O queues" and "Linux created 16 I/O queues on this machine" do not conflict. Rank 3 is the authority for the second question.
  • Specification vs vendor datasheet. "Max Data Transfer Size is reported by the controller" (specification) and "our MDTS is 512 KiB" (datasheet) are different levels. Conflict exists only if the datasheet contradicts what the device reports in Identify Controller; then the device's own report wins for that device, and the datasheet gets a bug.
  • Old book vs current specification. A good book written for NVMe 1.3 describing "the NVMe specification" as one document is not wrong about 1.3; it is version-dependent. The 2.x family split the material into a Base specification, separate command-set specifications and separate transport specifications.

Worked conflict

A colleague's wiki page says: "MSI-X vector 0 is always the admin queue." The Linux driver on your machine maps vector 0 to the admin queue. A vendor application note for a different operating system shows admin completions on a shared vector.

  1. Same question? Partly. Two questions are hiding in the claim: "which vector does the Admin Completion Queue use?" and "is vector 0 used only by the admin queue?". The first is answered by the NVMe specification's interrupt rules, which tie the Admin Completion Queue to vector 0 (confirm the wording in the Base Specification and the PCIe Transport's interrupt material). The second is a host choice: an I/O Completion Queue is given its vector when the host creates it with Create I/O Completion Queue, and nothing forbids a host from sharing vector 0.
  2. What does each source establish? The specification establishes the admin CQ's vector; the Linux source establishes that Linux gives vector 0 to the admin queue alone on your machine; the application note establishes that another host shares it; the wiki establishes nothing on its own.
  3. Conclusion: "The Admin Completion Queue uses vector 0" Verified once you have read the clause. "On this system, vector 0 serves only the admin queue" Verified for this kernel (driver source plus interrupt counters). "Vector 0 is always and only the admin queue" is unsupported.

Exercise

A forum answer says "PCIe Memory Writes are never acknowledged, so they can be lost silently". A colleague answers "Memory Writes are acknowledged by ACK DLLPs". Who is right, and at which layer?

Model answer

Both describe different layers, and the forum's conclusion is wrong. At the Transaction Layer a Memory Write is posted: no completion TLP returns to the requester. At the Data Link Layer every TLP crossing a link is protected by a sequence number and LCRC and is acknowledged with ACK/NAK DLLPs, so it is retried link-by-link if corrupted. "No completion" (transaction layer) does not mean "no acknowledgement" (data link layer). Source: PCI Express Base Specification, Transaction Layer and Data Link Layer chapters. The remaining truth: an end-to-end delivery confirmation for posted writes does not exist at the transaction layer; errors are reported through error signalling (for example AER), not through a completion.

Version control: the baseline block

Every lesson, note or report that depends on specification behaviour starts by pinning what it was checked against. The course uses this block:

A filled example

For PCIe

The PCI Express Base Specification is a PCI-SIG members' document. If you do not have access to a given revision, say so in the block ("REVISION: concepts common to the 4.0–6.x family; revision-specific details not checked"). Write "Flit mode" claims only against a revision that defines Flit mode (6.0 and later), and "non-Flit TLP header" claims for the traditional header format.

Two rules that prevent version drift

  • Never describe a modern feature as if it existed in an older revision. Example: a learner reading a 2.4-era tutorial about Exported NVM Subsystems should not assume a drive certified to an older revision implements them. The feature's revision context is part of the claim.
  • Never use an old layout as though it were necessarily current. Example: an old blog post's table of Identify Controller bytes may still be correct for many fields, but reserved bytes in an old revision may be defined in a new one. Use the current table, and when you must use an old one, label it with its revision.

Version sensitivity is not only in specifications. The Linux nvme command-line tool, the kernel driver, analyzer decode packages and firmware all have versions. A report that says "nvme-cli shows X" without the nvme-cli version can become unreproducible after a tool upgrade changes its output format.

Counterexample: a silent revision mix

A test plan says: "Per the NVMe specification, the controller shall support SGLs for I/O commands." The plan was written from a summary of a later revision and an optional feature was read as mandatory. The correct practice: identify the document and revision, read the controller-reported capability field for SGL support (in Identify Controller), and treat SGL support as Optional unless the normative text you are reading says otherwise for your configuration. The test plan then tests "if SGL support is reported, SGLs work; if not, SGL commands are rejected as the specification requires".

The zero-hallucination template

Every technical chapter in this course ends its specification view with six boxes. Use the same six boxes in your own study notes and design reviews. Here they are filled for one concept you already know: the SQ tail doorbell.

  • The host signals new submissions by writing the new tail index to the queue's SQ Tail Doorbell register (NVMe Base Specification, doorbell registers; NVMe over PCIe Transport for the memory-mapped placement).
  • Doorbell registers start at offset 1000h from the start of the controller's register space, with spacing set by CAP.DSTRD.
  • The controller consumes entries from its current head up to (but not including) the written tail.
  • On PCIe, a doorbell write appears as a Memory Write TLP from the root complex to an address inside the controller's BAR (it is an MMIO store).
  • With CAP.DSTRD = 0 on the lab system, SQ1's tail doorbell is at BAR0 + 0x1008.
  • Because Memory Writes are posted, the host receives no completion for the doorbell write itself.
  • How soon the controller fetches after the doorbell, and how many SQEs it fetches per Memory Read.
  • Whether the host batches several commands per doorbell write.
  • Whether the platform merges or delays the MMIO write (write-combining is not normally used for doorbells, but platform behaviour varies).
  • Shadow doorbell buffers (a later optional feature used mainly in virtualised controllers) change how often the real doorbell must be written; confirm the feature's revision context.
  • Where exactly doorbell rules are written down moved when the 2.x family split the Base and PCIe Transport documents.
  • What the controller does on a write of an invalid tail value or a write to a doorbell for a queue that does not exist (the error reporting mechanism and its asynchronous event information).
  • Whether any ordering is required between the SQE write to memory and the doorbell (and which side must enforce it).
  • Exact access size requirements for doorbell writes.
  • That one doorbell write equals one command.
  • That the doorbell's TLP carries the command (it carries only the index).
  • That a doorbell write visible on the analyzer was accepted by the controller.
  • That the doorbell offset is the same on every device (it depends on DSTRD).

Exercise

Fill the six boxes for "Phase Tag in a Completion Queue Entry". Keep each box to three bullets.

Model answer

Verified: the CQE contains a Phase Tag bit; the controller inverts the value it writes each time it wraps the CQ; the host uses it to tell new entries from old ones (NVMe Base Specification, completion queue entry and queue mechanics).

Derived: after creating a zero-filled CQ, the first pass of new entries has phase 1; the host must track the expected phase and flip it when its head wraps; reading a stale entry looks like "no new completion".

Implementation-dependent: whether the host polls or waits for interrupts; how many CQEs the controller writes per Memory Write; interrupt coalescing.

Version-dependent: little for the basic mechanism; check whether the CQE layout for your command set or transport adds fields.

Must check: the exact bit position of the Phase Tag in the CQE's status DWORD; the initial-content requirement for host-allocated CQ memory.

Never assume: that a CQE with the old phase is "empty" because its other fields are zero; that phase flips per entry; that an interrupt implies a phase-valid entry exists (it may already have been consumed by polling).

Specification reading discipline

A specification is not uniformly authoritative. Some sentences are requirements, some are advice, some are explanations written to help you, and some are pictures. Treating an example as a requirement is as dangerous as ignoring a requirement.

Kinds of specification text and how to treat them
KindHow you recognise itWeight in a report
RequirementA defined keyword such as "shall" (and its negation) in normative textA device or host that violates it is non-compliant
Recommendation"should"Deviation is permitted; a good report explains the consequence and whether the deviation is deliberate
Permission / option"may"; features marked optional; capability bitsBoth behaviours are compliant; you must check which one the device reports or does
Informative noteLabelled "Note", "informative", or placed in an informative annexExplains intent; never by itself a requirement
Example"For example", worked figures, sample valuesIllustrates one valid case; other values may be equally valid
RationaleText explaining why a rule existsHelps interpretation; not testable on its own
Implementation noteText describing a possible way to build somethingOne option, not the required design
Reserved field"Reserved" in a field tableHas defined handling rules in the document's conventions (commonly: writers clear to zero, readers do not depend on the value); may become defined in a later revision
Ignored fieldA field the receiver is told to ignore in a given contextIts value carries no meaning in that context, even if non-zero
Vendor-specific field"Vendor specific" in a field table or opcode rangeMeaning is defined only by the vendor; the standard says nothing about its content

"Shall", "should" and "may"

NVMe and PCIe specifications each contain a conventions section that defines their keywords. Read that section once per document family. In normative text:

  • shall states a requirement. "The controller shall…" means a compliant controller does it.
  • should states a recommendation: the preferred behaviour, from which deviation is allowed when there is reason.
  • may states permission: the behaviour is allowed but not required. A "may" is also a warning to the other side: the host must cope with both choices.

Do not claim a precise legal interpretation beyond what the document's conventions define. "The specification says the controller shall X" is a quotation; "therefore any controller that does Y is defective in every configuration" is an interpretation that may ignore a capability bit, an optional feature or a different revision. Keep them visibly separate.

Exercise: classify invented sentences

The sentences below are invented for practice in specification style; they are not quotations from any specification.

  1. "The host should allocate the Completion Queue in memory that is not shared with other functions."

    Model answer

    Recommendation. A host that shares memory is not non-compliant; it accepts whatever risk the recommendation was guarding against.

  2. "Note: Implementations typically fetch several entries at once to reduce latency."

    Model answer

    Informative note describing typical implementation behaviour. Not a requirement; a controller that fetches one entry at a time is still compliant.

  3. "Bits 31:16 are reserved."

    Model answer

    Reserved field. Apply the document's reserved-field convention. Do not attach meaning to observed non-zero values without checking whether a newer revision defines them.

  4. "The controller may complete commands in any order."

    Model answer

    Permission. The important consequence is for the other side: the host must match completions by Command Identifier and queue, not by arrival order.

  5. "For example, a host might use 4 KiB pages."

    Model answer

    Example. Other page sizes allowed by the capability and configuration fields are equally valid.

  6. "This field is ignored when the Enable bit is cleared to 0."

    Model answer

    Ignored field (contextual). A non-zero value in that state is not an error and carries no meaning.

Source notes: how to cite

Every specification-sensitive section of a report, lesson or design document carries a source notes block. It forces you to write down not only what the source establishes but also what it does not establish, which is where most over-claiming hides.

Do not fabricate page or section numbers

Section numbers move between revisions; a wrong number sends a reader to the wrong rule and makes the whole note look careless. Cite by document name, revision and section title ("NVM Express Base Specification 2.4 — Completion Queue Entry"). Add a section number only after you have read it in the document you cite, and then include the revision next to it.

  11. 11 only then explain

Worked walk-through: "What happens if the host creates an I/O CQ that is too large?"

  1. Document: queue creation is an admin command in the NVM Express Base Specification. Queue size limits come from a controller capability (CAP.MQES).
  2. Revision: 2.4, ratified 2026-07-31.
  3. Locate: the Create I/O Completion Queue command description, and the Controller Capabilities property.
  4. Definitions: read how queue size is encoded. It is a zero-based value (a field value of N means N + 1 entries). Missing this produces off-by-one errors in every later step.
  5. Normative requirements: what the controller shall do when the requested size exceeds the supported maximum.
  6. Tables: the command's status values table, which names the specific status for an invalid queue size.
  7. State diagrams: none for this command; note that, and move on.
  8. Error behaviour: confirm the status code type (command-specific vs generic) and whether the queue is partially created (it is not; confirm).
  9. Referenced sections: the queue-size rules for physically contiguous queues, and CAP.CQR (Contiguous Queues Required).
  10. Version dependencies: any change in the 2.x family to queue creation fields (check Revision Changes).
  11. Explain: "The size field is zero-based. If the host requests more entries than CAP.MQES + 1 allows, the controller fails the command with the command-specific status for an invalid queue size and creates no queue. Name the status from the table; do not guess its value."

Step 4 is the one experienced people skip and regret. Most NVMe field confusions are not about the rule; they are about the encoding: zero-based counts, units of 4 bytes vs bytes vs logical blocks, powers of two stored as exponents. Read the definition before the requirement.

Analyzer presentation versus protocol truth

A protocol analyzer shows you a decoded, grouped, coloured, sometimes summarised view of what its probe saw. That view is extremely useful and is not the normative wire-level definition. Four layers sit between the wire and your conclusion:

Each layer can be wrong in its own way. The raw event can be missing (probe placement, capture buffer full, a lane not captured). The decoding can be wrong (wrong decoder version, Flit vs non-Flit setting, a link with Extended Tags the tool did not expect). The UI can mislead (a "transaction" row that pairs a request with the wrong completion, an NVMe overlay that guessed a command from an address pattern). Counterexample: the helpful merge

NVMe command decoding safety

An opcode is a number whose meaning depends on context. Never infer a command's semantics from the opcode alone. Check:

  • Queue type: admin or I/O. The same opcode value names different commands on each.
  • Command set: which I/O command set the namespace uses (identified by the Command Set Identifier, CSI). The NVM, Zoned Namespace and Key Value command sets assign their own opcodes.
  • Controller capabilities: whether the controller supports the command at all (Identify Controller fields and the Commands Supported and Effects log).
  • Namespace context: the namespace's format (LBA size, metadata, protection information) changes what the fields mean.
  • Specification revision: fields once reserved may now be defined.
  • Field validity: some fields are valid only when another field or a feature enables them.

An analyzer row shows an SQE with CDW0 = 0x0003_0001 fetched from address 0x1_0000_00C0. A junior writes "Write command, CID 3". Exercise

A capture shows SQ1 tail doorbell writes of 5, 6, 7 and CQE writes at CQ1 offsets 0x50 and 0x60, but no CQ1 head doorbell writes at all over 10 seconds. Which ledger fields can you fill, and what must you write about the rest?

Tail of SQ1 = 7 after the last write (verified by payload). CQ1 slots 5 and 6 written (derived from offset / 16). SQHD and CID and phase: only if the CQE payload was captured. DMA safety

"The SSD directly reads RAM" is a useful first sentence and a dangerous last one. A correct description of a DMA read (device reading host memory, as for an SQE fetch or the data of an NVMe Write) answers six questions:

  1. Who initiates? The SSD's function, as PCIe requester (Requester ID 0x0100).
  2. Which address? A DMA address from a queue base or PRP entry (an IOVA on the lab system).
  3. What translation? The IOMMU at the root complex translates IOVA to a physical address, and may reject it.
  4. What PCIe transaction? A non-posted Memory Read request TLP (MRd), at most MRRS bytes.
  5. How does data return? As one or more Completion with Data TLPs (CplD) from the root complex, split on Read Completion Boundary multiples, matched by Requester ID and tag.
  6. What happens inside? Implementation-dependent: buffering, reordering, and what the controller does with the data are not visible.

A DMA write (device writing host memory, as for NVMe Read data, CQEs and MSI-X messages) is a posted Memory Write: no completion returns, and an IOMMU rejection is reported by the platform, not by a completion to the device.

  2. Which claim generalises from one device?

A full Completion Queue is a host-side resource problem: the controller cannot post into a full CQ. Who is responsible for emptying it?

(5) "Linux timeout fired after 30 s as per the spec" — wrong attribution: the 30 s default is a Linux driver setting (version-dependent, configurable), not an NVMe requirement. (6) "Firmware must be fixed" — conclusion without root cause. (7) "all Gen4 drives from this vendor" — over-generalisation from one device (implementation-dependent).

The word "full" without a queue name. The report's whole conclusion flips depending on whether it is the SQ (host cannot submit; harmless) or the CQ (controller cannot post; caused by whoever stopped consuming).

Failure variants

  • The capture was filtered to "NVMe transactions only", hiding the MSI-X writes.
  • The reporter's trace was a merged analyzer summary, so per-TLP coverage is unknown.

The map: opcode 00h on an I/O queue and its semantics — NVM Command Set (verified once read); CID/CQE/status — Base; doorbell and interrupt mapping — PCIe Transport; MWr/MRd/CplD formats — PCIe Base; whether Linux issues Flush for fsync on this device — kernel (depends on the reported volatile write cache). Answers: (1) with no volatile write cache, there is nothing volatile to commit; read what the specification says Flush does in that case and quote it. (2) No: an analyzer shows only the command and its completion. Persistence is a claim about the device's internal behaviour, verified only through the specification's guarantee plus device testing (for example power-loss testing), never through the trace.

What a senior engineer notices

The learner who writes "the trace proves the data is durable" has confused protocol completion with media state.

Failure variants

  • Citing an NVMe 1.x combined specification for the command-set details.
  • Inventing a section number from memory.

Extension

Repeat for Dataset Management (deallocate) and record which behaviours the specification leaves to the implementation.

Lab T3 — Decoding-safety drill with the evidence checker

Safe lab Runs a standard-library Python program on synthetic events.

Objective

Practise refusing to classify packets without evidence.

Skills

Fmt/Type decoding, field completeness, label-vs-header conflicts.

Hardware required

None.

Software required

Python 3.8 or later.

Input

evidence_check.py from the TLP decoding safety section.

Expected output

For each event, a decode, a list of missing fields, and a verdict.

Background

Chapter 11 — TLP Header Anatomy.

Step 1

Run the program unchanged; compare with the expected output described above.

Step 2

Add an event: a 1-DW MWr to BAR0 + 0x1008 from the root complex with payload 0x00000006. Add an entry to NEEDED if necessary.

Step 3

Add a 4DW MWr whose address is below 4 GiB (for example 0xFEE0_0000). Should the checker warn?

Observation

Which conflicts are about the trace and which are about the decoder's configuration?

Questions

  1. Why can a completion not be classified as "the SQE fetch completion" without the Requester ID?
  2. Why does event 3 need its payload?

Challenge

Extend the program with a rule: a CplD must have a preceding outstanding MRd with the same Requester ID and tag.

Hint

Keep a dictionary keyed by (requester, tag) of outstanding reads; remove the entry when byte count equals the remaining length.

Solution

Step 2: the event passes; the checker can classify it as a 3DW MWr. Its NVMe meaning ("SQ1 tail doorbell = 6") is a further derived step using the BAR and DSTRD. Step 3: yes. The PCI Express Base Specification requires the 32-bit (3DW) address format for addresses below 4 GiB, so a 4DW header with a sub-4 GiB address is a protocol issue worth flagging (confirm the exact rule wording in the Transaction Layer chapter). Questions: (1) tags are unique only per requester; the root complex and other devices reuse the same tag values. (2) The MSI-X message data identifies the interrupt; without it you cannot say which vector fired.

What a senior engineer notices

Whether "missing" fields are missing in the capture or only in the export. Re-export before re-capturing.

Failure variants

  • The analyzer was set to non-Flit decoding on a Flit-mode link.
  • Extended tags enabled on the device but the decoder assumes 8-bit tags.

Quiz

  1. A claim reasoned from two verified facts, with the reasoning shown, should be labelled:

    • Verified
    • Derived
    • Implementation-dependent
    • Unknown
    Answer and reasoning

    Derived. "Verified" is reserved for what a cited source directly states. Derived claims are often just as reliable, but the label tells the reader to check the reasoning.

  2. A Linux kernel source file and the NVMe Base Specification appear to disagree. What is the first thing to check?

    • Which document is newer
    • Which is higher in the hierarchy
    • Whether they answer the same question
    • Which one a colleague trusts
    Answer and reasoning

    Most conflicts between a specification and an implementation are not conflicts: one states what is allowed, the other what Linux does. Apply the hierarchy only when both claim to answer the same question.

  3. In normative specification text, "may" indicates:

    • Permission: allowed, not required
    • A recommendation
    • A requirement with exceptions
    • Informative text
    Answer and reasoning

    Permission. The other side must cope with both behaviours.

  4. Which layer of the analyzer stack produced the text "NVMe SQ Fetch, Entry 5"?

    • Raw event
    • Protocol decoding
    • Analyzer UI
    • Normative specification
    Answer and reasoning

    The PCIe decode yields "MRd, Length 16, Addr …". Naming it an NVMe SQ fetch requires a queue map, which is an overlay in the user interface and must itself be verified.

  5. Opcode 01h is seen in an SQE fetched from the Admin SQ. The command is:

    • Write
    • Create I/O Submission Queue
    • Flush
    • Cannot be determined
    Answer and reasoning

    On the admin queue, 01h is Create I/O Submission Queue. On an I/O queue in the NVM command set it would be Write. The queue type was the missing context.

  6. Why can an analyzer on the SSD's link not show the host writing an SQE?

    Model answer

    The host writes the SQE into host memory with CPU stores that go to DRAM through the memory controller; they do not cross the PCIe link to the SSD. The SQE becomes visible only when the device reads it (MRd + CplD with payload).

  7. Rewrite "the device never sent an MSI-X" as a statement that is safe without a coverage statement.

    Model answer

    "No MSI-X Memory Write from the device is visible in the captured interval." Then add coverage: start/end, directions, filters, whether decoding could omit events.

  8. Name four of the six questions a correct DMA description answers.

    Model answer

    Who initiates; which address is used; what address translation may exist; what PCIe transaction represents it; how data returns; what the controller may do internally.

  9. Why is citing "NVMe spec §5.x" without a revision unsafe?

    Model answer

    Section numbers change between revisions and between the 1.x combined document and the 2.x split documents. Without document name and revision, the reader may land on a different rule. Cite by document, revision and section title.

  10. A TLP's header says Fmt = 001b, Type = 00000b, but the analyzer labels it "CplD". What do you conclude?

    Model answer

    The header decodes as a 4DW Memory Read request. Label and header disagree; trust neither until the raw bytes and the analyzer's decode configuration (link speed, Flit mode, tag size settings) are checked. Report it as a decode conflict, not as a device error.

Interview questions

  1. (Beginner) What is the difference between "verified" and "derived"?

    Model answer

    Verified means a cited source directly says it. Derived means it follows from verified facts by reasoning that I can show. Both can be correct; the label tells the reader what to check: the citation or the reasoning.

  2. (Beginner) Why do we separate the NVMe specification from the PCIe specification when explaining a Read?

    Model answer

    Because they define different things. NVMe defines the command, queues and completion; PCIe defines the packets that carry the doorbell, the SQE fetch, the data and the interrupt. The NVMe over PCIe Transport specification joins them. Quoting NVMe for a TLP rule (or PCIe for a status code) produces wrong citations and wrong debugging.

  3. (Intermediate) An analyzer shows a "transaction" with status OK, but the application saw corrupted data. What do you check first?

    Model answer

    Expand the transaction into its raw TLPs: every completion's status, byte count, lower address and payload; whether any completion was poisoned (EP bit); whether the grouping paired the right completions. Then check addresses (IOVA vs expected buffer) and the PRP entries in the SQE. The OK is a UI summary; the evidence is in the rows.

  4. (Intermediate) How do you report the absence of a completion?

    Model answer

    "No completion for CID X on CQ Y is visible in the captured interval [start, end]", with direction coverage, filters, buffer wrap status and whether the analyzer could have dropped or hidden events. Only if coverage supports it do I treat it as strong negative evidence.

  5. (Advanced) A vendor datasheet states a capability that the device's Identify Controller data does not report. Which do you trust, and what do you do?

    Model answer

    For that device's behaviour, the device's own report is what hosts act on, so it governs what the host may use. I would record the discrepancy, check firmware version and configuration (some capabilities depend on settings), and file it with the vendor. The datasheet does not override the device or the specification.

  6. (Advanced) Why must addresses be normalised before comparing two traces?

    Model answer

    With an IOMMU, buffers get different IOVAs each run; even without one, allocation differs. Raw addresses differ everywhere and the real divergence is buried. Normalising to "region + offset" (SQ1+0x140, DATA+0x1000) preserves meaning and removes noise.

  7. (Senior) A junior's root-cause document quotes an informative note as a requirement. How do you handle it in review?

    Model answer

    Point to the conventions section and the note's informative status; ask what normative text, if any, supports the claim. If none does, the finding becomes "device behaviour differs from the informative description", which may still be a valid product concern but not a compliance failure. Coach on the habit, not just the instance: add the text-kind classification to their template.

  8. (Senior) How would you structure a team's knowledge base so assumptions do not become facts over time?

    Model answer

    Every entry carries a truth label, a baseline block (document, revision, date checked), source notes with "does not establish", and an owner. Entries derived from a single device are tagged implementation-dependent with the device/firmware. A periodic re-verification pass is triggered by new specification releases and kernel upgrades. Unlabelled claims are rejected in review.

Teach-back challenge

Explain "why every sentence needs a truth label" three times.

  • To a child: use the museum cards.
  • To a junior engineer: use the labelled paragraph from the worked example.
  • To a senior engineer: argue the cost: one unlabelled assumption in a root-cause chain turns a hypothesis into a fix that does not fix anything.
Model explanations

Child: "In the museum, cards tell you which bones are real and which are guesses. When I write about computers, I put an invisible card on each sentence so people know whether I read it in the rule book, worked it out, or am guessing."

Junior: "Look at this paragraph about a trace. Half of it is observed, some is derived from the doorbell formula, and two sentences are guesses about NAND and a broken vector. Once we label them, we know exactly which experiment to run next and which sentences we can put in the bug report."

Senior: "Our last escalation spent two weeks on a firmware change because 'the drive drops completions' was treated as established. It was a hypothesis built on negative evidence from a filtered capture. Labelling makes the weakest link visible at review time, when it is cheap."

Summary and mastery gate

Truth discipline is a set of small habits: label every claim; rank sources and check whether they answer the same question; pin revisions; read the kind of text you cite; write source notes with what they do not establish; follow the eleven-step workflow; map topics across documents; keep PCIe-normative, NVMe, implementation and analyzer views apart; decode from fields, not labels; keep a queue ledger; name the kind of address; describe DMA completely; and bound every analogy.

Do not move on until you can:

  • label any claim with one of the six labels and defend it
  • resolve a source conflict, first by checking whether it is one question
  • write a baseline block and a source-notes block without a section number you did not read
  • classify specification text as requirement, recommendation, permission, note, example, rationale, implementation note, reserved, ignored or vendor-specific
  • map an NVMe Read across five documents and owners
  • separate raw event, decode, analyzer UI and interpretation for a trace row
  • say "insufficient trace evidence" and name the missing field
  • tell the six kinds of address apart and describe DMA with all six questions
  • write an analogy boundary
