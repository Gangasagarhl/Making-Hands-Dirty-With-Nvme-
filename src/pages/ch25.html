NVM Express (NVMe) is, at its heart, a pair of circular queues in host memory and two tiny registers on the device. This chapter teaches you exactly who writes which index, how the host knows a completion is new without reading a single device register, when a queue is full or empty, and how one queue pair per CPU core lets an SSD scale to millions of operations per second.

Why this chapter exists

Every NVMe command you will ever trace — an Identify during boot, a 4 KiB random read under a database, a Flush before a power cut — travels through a queue. If you misunderstand queues, you will misread every trace that follows. If you understand them precisely, a protocol analyzer capture stops being a wall of packets and becomes a readable story: "the host published tail 8, the controller fetched three entries, posted three completions with phase 1, the host consumed them and published head 40."

Queues are also where most driver and firmware bugs that look "random" actually live: a stale tail, an off-by-one on wrap, a phase tag that was not inverted, a Completion Queue (CQ) that overflowed because it was sized smaller than the commands it had to absorb. Those bugs produce hangs and timeouts far away from their cause. A senior engineer can find them because they hold the ownership rules in their head without effort. That is the goal of this chapter.

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

  • Define a Submission Queue (SQ), a Completion Queue (CQ), the Admin queue pair and I/O queue pairs, and say where each lives.
  • State, without hesitation, which agent owns each of the four indices: SQ tail, SQ head, CQ tail, CQ head — and how each is communicated to the other side.
  • Explain the phase tag and walk a completion queue through a wrap, step by step, predicting exactly which entries the host will treat as new.
  • Apply the full and empty rules for a circular queue and explain why a queue of N slots holds at most N − 1 entries.
  • Decode MQES (Maximum Queue Entries Supported) as a 0's based value and explain CAP.CQR (Contiguous Queues Required) and its consequence for queue memory.
  • Explain queue depth, queue pairs per CPU, and why several SQs may map to a single CQ while the reverse is not allowed.
  • Describe the arbitration mechanisms — round robin and weighted round robin with an urgent priority class — and how the controller picks which SQ to fetch from next.
  • Recognise the queue machinery in a synthetic PCI Express (PCIe) trace and in Linux.

The story

Imagine a toy shop with one very fast shopkeeper behind a counter. Children don't shout their orders. Each child writes an order on a card and puts it into a round tray that spins: there are eight slots in the tray. The children put cards in the next empty slot, and then ring a little bell and hold up a number: "the next empty slot is now number 5!"

The shopkeeper hears the bell, takes cards out in order from where she last stopped, and fetches the toys. When a toy is ready, she puts a little answer card into a second spinning tray — the answer tray. Every answer card has a coloured sticker: on the first trip round the tray she uses green stickers; when she comes back round to slot 0 she switches to red stickers, then back to green on the next trip.

The children never have to ask "is there a new answer?" They just look at the next answer slot. If its sticker is the colour they are expecting this time round, it's a new answer. If it's the old colour, it's last round's answer — nothing new yet.

A supermarket has a row of checkout lanes. Each lane has a conveyor belt (the Submission Queue): shoppers place items at the back end (the tail), and the cashier takes items from the front end (the head). Each lane prints receipts onto a shared receipt spool (the Completion Queue). The store manager (the arbitration logic) decides which lane's cashier gets the next scanning slot: plain turn-taking (round robin), or by priority — an express lane for urgent customers that is always served first, then high, medium and low lanes in proportion to their weights (weighted round robin).

Two express lanes can print receipts onto one spool (several SQs share one CQ). A shopper standing at the belt does not walk round to the cashier to ask "have you scanned my stuff?" — they watch the receipt spool. And the conveyor belt is a loop: when the belt reaches the end, it comes back round to the start.

What the analogies hide. In a real supermarket, the shopper and the cashier share the belt in the same room. In NVMe, the SQ and CQ live in host memory, while the "cashier" (the controller) is on the far side of a PCIe link and can only see host memory through DMA reads and writes. The "bell" is a 4-byte write to a device register, and the number held up with it is the new tail — not "one more item".

Where it misleads. (1) Receipts in NVMe do not come out in the order items went in: a controller may complete commands out of order, so the CQ order need not match SQ order. (2) The cashier never tells the shopper "I'm ready for more items" by ringing a bell; instead the controller reports how far it has consumed the SQ inside each completion (the SQ Head Pointer field). (3) Nobody watches the belt continuously: the controller only looks after the bell rings, and the host may learn about receipts by interrupt or by polling. (4) The coloured sticker is a single bit, not a colour; it flips on every wrap of the completion queue only — submission queues have no phase tag.

Beginner explanation

The host (the CPU running the NVMe driver) and the NVMe controller (the logic inside the SSD) need to exchange two kinds of message: requests ("read these blocks into this buffer") and results ("done, it worked" or "failed, here is why"). NVMe uses two lists in host memory for this:

  • A Submission Queue: an array of fixed-size slots. Each slot holds one 64-byte command, called a Submission Queue Entry (SQE). The host writes commands here; the controller reads them.
  • A Completion Queue: an array of fixed-size slots. Each holds one 16-byte result, a Completion Queue Entry (CQE). The controller writes results here; the host reads them.

Both arrays are circular: after the last slot comes slot 0 again. Each queue has two positions that move around the circle:

  • The tail is where the producer will write the next entry.
  • The head is where the consumer will read the next entry.

For the SQ, the host is the producer and the controller is the consumer. For the CQ it is the other way round: the controller produces, the host consumes. That symmetry is the single most important idea in this chapter.

In the ring above, slots 2, 3 and 4 hold commands the controller has not fetched yet. The host's next command goes into slot 5. When the host has written it, it moves the tail to 6 and tells the controller by writing the number 6 into a device register called the doorbell.

Two kinds of queue pair

Every NVMe controller has exactly one Admin queue pair (queue identifier 0). It carries management commands: identify the device, create other queues, read logs, change features. Data commands — Read, Write, Flush and so on — travel on I/O queue pairs (queue identifiers 1 and up), which the driver creates by sending commands on the Admin queue. A modern SSD may support dozens or hundreds of I/O queues.

Why not just a register?

Older storage interfaces made the CPU talk to the disk controller through registers: write a command register, poll a status register. Each register access crosses the bus and stalls the CPU for a round trip. NVMe deliberately moves almost everything into host memory, where the CPU can write at memory speed, and leaves only one tiny register write — the doorbell — per batch of commands. The controller then pulls the commands itself with DMA. Completions arrive the same way: the controller pushes them into host memory, so the host can discover them by reading its own RAM, never by reading a device register.

How does the host know a completion is new?

Here is the clever part. The host never reads the CQ tail from the device (there is no register for it). Instead, each CQE contains a single bit called the phase tag (P). The controller writes P = 1 into every entry on its first trip around the CQ, P = 0 on its second trip, P = 1 on the third, and so on. The host remembers what value it expects. If the entry at its head position has the expected P, it is new. If not, it is left over from the previous trip, and nothing new has arrived. We will walk through this slowly with a table in the engineering section.

Engineering explanation

Each queue pair has four indices. Ownership means "only this agent ever advances it". The other agent learns its value through a defined channel.

Host owns: SQ tail and CQ head. Controller owns: SQ head and CQ tail. Each side owns the index of the queue it produces into for SQ (host) and the queue it consumes from for CQ (host). Say it as: "the host pushes commands and pulls completions; it owns the two ends it touches."

Producer and consumer

A circular queue with one producer and one consumer needs no lock, because each index has exactly one writer. The producer writes an entry, then advances the tail. The consumer reads the entry at the head, then advances the head. The only requirement is that the producer's entry is visible before the consumer learns the new tail. In NVMe over PCIe this becomes a memory-ordering requirement on the host (the SQE must reach memory before the doorbell write reaches the device) and an ordering guarantee on the device side (the CQE must not become visible before the data it describes). We deal with both in Chapter 28.

Full and empty rules

With a queue of N slots, head H and tail T, both in the range 0 to N − 1:

empty ⇔ H = T      full ⇔ (T + 1) mod N = H      entries in use = (T − H + N) mod N
N
queue size in entries (the 0's based size field in the creating command or AQA, plus one)
H
head index: the next slot the consumer will read
T
tail index: the next slot the producer will write

Because "empty" is H = T, the queue cannot be allowed to fill every slot — a completely full queue would also have H = T and be indistinguishable from empty. So one slot is always left unused: a queue of N slots holds at most N − 1 entries. This is Verified as the NVMe definition of a full queue: the queue is full when the head equals one more than the tail, with wrap.

Consequence: a queue created with size 2 (the minimum) can hold one outstanding entry. An Admin queue of 32 entries (as in the course's reference lab system) can hold 31 commands at once. An I/O queue of 1024 entries can hold 1023.

The phase tag, slowly

The CQE is 16 bytes, four Dwords (DW, 32-bit words). Dword 3 holds the Command Identifier (bits 15:00), the Phase Tag (bit 16) and the Status field (bits 31:17). Dword 2 holds the SQ Head Pointer (bits 15:00) and the SQ Identifier (bits 31:16). Dwords 0 and 1 are command specific.

Completion Queue Entry, 16 bytes (common layout)
DW31…2423…1615…87…0
DW0Command Specific (for example, Number of Queues Allocated)
DW1Command Specific (reserved for most commands in older revisions)
DW2SQ Identifier (SQID)SQ Head Pointer (SQHD)
DW3Status (SCT, SC, CRD, More, DNR)PCommand Identifier (CID)

The rules, all Normative in the NVMe Base Specification (queueing model):

  1. When the host creates a CQ, the memory should be initialised so that every entry's phase tag is 0. (In practice drivers zero the whole CQ.)
  2. On the first pass through the CQ, the controller writes P = 1 into each entry it posts.
  3. Each time the controller's CQ tail wraps from the last slot to slot 0, it inverts the value it writes.
  4. The host keeps an "expected phase", initially 1. It reads the entry at its CQ head; if P equals the expected phase, the entry is new: process it and advance the head. When the host's head wraps to 0, it inverts its expected phase.
  5. The first entry whose P does not equal the expected phase marks the end of the new entries. The host then writes the new head to the CQ head doorbell to free those slots for the controller.

Step 13 is the whole point. Slot 1 still contains a perfectly valid-looking CQE for CID 0x11 from the first pass. Without the phase tag, the host would have no way to tell it is old, short of reading a device register. With the phase tag, the host only needs one bit from memory it already owns.

The SQ's consumer is the controller, and the controller is told the tail explicitly by the doorbell. The CQ's consumer is the host, and the host is never told the CQ tail. The phase tag is what replaces the missing "CQ tail register". That asymmetry exists because reading a device register costs a full non-posted PCIe round trip (often around a microsecond), while reading host memory is a cache access.

Queue depth and queue size

Queue size is the number of slots the queue was created with. Queue depth is used two ways and you should always ask which one is meant:

  • the configured maximum number of commands that can be outstanding on a queue (at most size − 1), or
  • the number actually in flight at a given moment (what tools like fio call iodepth and what Little's Law calls L).

Depth matters because an SSD is internally parallel: tens of NAND dies, each able to work on a different request. A single outstanding command keeps only one die busy. Deeper queues let the controller keep more of the media busy — up to the point where latency grows without any gain in throughput.

MQES: how large a queue may be

The controller advertises its limit in the CAP (Controller Capabilities) register at offset 00h, field MQES, bits 15:00. It is a 0's based value: the number stored is one less than the real count. MQES = 03FFh means 1024 entries per queue. The minimum value the specification allows is 1h, meaning 2 entries. Because the field is 16 bits, the largest possible I/O queue is 65,536 entries.

0's based values are a classic source of off-by-one bugs. MQES, the queue size fields in Create I/O Completion Queue and Create I/O Submission Queue, and the AQA sizes are all 0's based. A driver that writes 1024 into a 0's based size field asks for 1025 entries — and the controller should reject it if MQES is 03FFh.

Physically contiguous queues and PRP-list queues

A queue is a block of host memory. It can be described to the controller in two ways:

  • Physically contiguous: one base address; the queue occupies consecutive bytes. The Admin queues are always contiguous: ASQ and ACQ registers hold a single base address each.
  • Non-contiguous: for I/O queues, the creating command's PRP Entry 1 can point to a Physical Region Page (PRP) list — a page of pointers, each naming one memory page of the queue. This lets a driver build a large queue out of scattered pages.

The CAP.CQR bit (Contiguous Queues Required, bit 16) tells the host whether the controller supports the second form. If CQR = 1, every I/O queue must be physically contiguous, and the host must set the Physically Contiguous (PC) bit in Create I/O SQ and Create I/O CQ. Most controllers built for servers set CQR = 1, because walking a PRP list on every SQE fetch or CQE post costs extra DMA reads.

The Implementation-dependent question is whether a given controller supports non-contiguous queues at all; that is exactly what CQR reports. A 1024-entry I/O SQ is 64 KiB — sixteen 4 KiB pages — which Linux can usually allocate contiguously through the DMA API; an IOMMU (Input/Output Memory Management Unit) can also make physically scattered pages look contiguous in I/O virtual address space.

Queue pairs per CPU

The design target for NVMe was many cores issuing I/O at once without locks. The standard approach, used by the Linux driver and others, is one I/O SQ (and usually one CQ) per CPU core, or per group of cores when the controller has fewer queues than the system has cores:

Each core writes only its own SQ tail and its own CQ head, so there is no cross-core contention. The completion interrupt — a Message Signaled Interrupt eXtended (MSI-X) vector — is steered to the same core, so the completing code finds its data warm in cache.

SQ-to-CQ mapping: many to one, never one to many

When the driver creates an I/O SQ, it names the CQ that the SQ's completions go to (the CQ Identifier field). Several SQs may name the same CQ. One SQ cannot spread its completions over several CQs. The Admin SQ always completes into the Admin CQ.

Why would you share? To give different priority classes to different SQs without multiplying interrupt vectors; or because the controller supports more SQs than CQs. Because the CQE carries SQID and SQHD, the host can always tell which SQ, and how far that SQ has been consumed.

Since a CQ shared by several SQs receives completions for all of them, it must be large enough to absorb the worst-case total of outstanding commands across all those SQs, or the controller will find the CQ full and stall. With a 1:1 pair and a driver that never allows more than size − 1 outstanding commands, the CQ can never overflow.

Arbitration: which SQ does the controller serve next?

With many SQs holding commands, the controller must choose which to fetch from. NVMe defines the arbitration mechanisms; the controller advertises them in CAP.AMS (Arbitration Mechanism Supported) and the host selects one in CC.AMS (Arbitration Mechanism Selected) before enabling the controller.

  • Round robin Mandatory: every SQ, including the Admin SQ, is treated as equal priority. The controller takes up to an Arbitration Burst of commands from one SQ, then moves to the next.
  • Weighted round robin with urgent priority class Optional: three strict priority levels. The Admin SQ is highest; then the Urgent class; then a weighted round robin among the High, Medium and Low classes, with weights set by the host through the Arbitration feature (Set Features). An SQ's class is set when it is created (the Queue Priority field in Create I/O SQ).
  • Vendor specific Optional: defined by the vendor.

Arbitration decides the order in which the controller fetches commands. The specification does not promise that commands complete in fetch order, nor that a high-priority command overtakes a low-priority command already being executed inside the device. Back-end scheduling is implementation-dependent. Linux, by default, does not enable weighted round robin.

The ladder for the central concept: the queue pair

Children put order cards in a spinning tray and ring a bell; the shopkeeper puts answer cards with coloured stickers into another spinning tray; the children read answers whose sticker is this round's colour.

An SQ and a CQ are circular arrays in host memory. The host produces SQEs and advances the SQ tail; the controller consumes them and advances the SQ head. The controller produces CQEs and advances the CQ tail; the host consumes them and advances the CQ head. The phase tag marks fresh CQEs.

The queues are DMA-able pages in DRAM. The controller holds per-queue state: base address, size, head, tail, phase, the associated CQ and interrupt vector. The doorbell registers are flops inside the controller's BAR0 region starting at offset 1000h; a write to one wakes the fetch engine.

NVMe Base Specification: the queueing model defines SQ/CQ, full/empty, the phase tag and SQHD; the controller registers define CAP.MQES, CAP.CQR, CAP.AMS, CAP.DSTRD and the doorbells; the Admin command set defines Create/Delete I/O SQ and CQ. The NVMe over PCIe Transport Specification defines how these map onto PCIe memory transactions.

On the link: host-to-device Memory Write TLPs (Transaction Layer Packets) to doorbells; device-to-host Memory Read TLPs to fetch SQEs, answered by Completion-with-Data TLPs from the root complex; device-to-host Memory Write TLPs to post CQEs; a device-to-host Memory Write to the MSI-X address for the interrupt.

In an analyzer: MWr to BAR0+0x1008 Data=8 (SQ1 tail = 8); MRd from 01:00.0 to the SQ1 base + 0x140, 192 bytes; CplD back; data movement; MWr 16 bytes to CQ1 base + 0x250 with P = 1; MWr to 0xFEE0_xxxx; MWr to BAR0+0x100C Data=40 (CQ1 head = 40).

Commands time out but the device is healthy? Check: was the SQ tail doorbell written at all, and with the right value? Did the controller ever fetch from the SQ base? Did a CQE arrive with a phase the host did not expect (host phase bug)? Did the controller stop posting because the CQ head doorbell was never written (CQ "full")?

The sixteen questions: the NVMe queue pair

1. What is it?
A Submission Queue and the Completion Queue it maps to: two circular arrays in host memory plus two doorbell registers in the controller.
2. Why was it created?
To let many CPU cores submit storage commands in parallel at memory speed, with at most one register write per batch and no register reads on the fast path.
3. What problem does it solve?
Register-based interfaces serialised work and stalled the CPU on uncached reads; a single shared command queue (as in AHCI, with 32 slots on one queue per port) created lock contention and limited parallelism.
4. What existed before it?
AHCI (Advanced Host Controller Interface) for SATA: one command list of 32 slots per port, with command issue and status registers; SCSI host adapters with vendor-specific queue designs.
5. Why is the current design better?
Up to 65,535 I/O queues of up to 65,536 entries each; per-core queues with no shared lock; the host discovers completions from memory; doorbells are posted writes that do not stall the CPU.
6. Who creates it?
The host driver allocates the memory. The Admin pair is created by writing AQA/ASQ/ACQ before enabling the controller; I/O pairs by Create I/O CQ and Create I/O SQ commands on the Admin queue.
7. Who consumes it?
The controller consumes SQEs; the host consumes CQEs.
8. Where does it live?
In host memory for NVMe over PCIe (with the optional exception of queues placed in a Controller Memory Buffer, a version- and implementation-dependent feature). Doorbells live in the controller's BAR0 register space.
9. What memory/registers are involved?
SQ and CQ memory; CAP (MQES, CQR, AMS, DSTRD); CC (IOSQES, IOCQES, AMS, MPS); AQA, ASQ, ACQ; SQyTDBL and CQyHDBL.
10. What happens before it?
PCIe enumeration and BAR assignment, enabling bus mastering, controller reset and enable, Identify, and the Number of Queues negotiation.
11. What happens after it?
Commands flow: SQE write, doorbell, fetch, execution with DMA, CQE post, interrupt, host reaps the CQE and writes the CQ head doorbell.
12. What happens if it is wrong?
Hangs (no fetch), duplicated or lost completions, corrupted commands, timeouts and controller resets; possibly asynchronous error events for invalid doorbell writes.
13. What does the protocol analyzer show?
Doorbell MWr TLPs, SQE-fetch MRd/CplD pairs, CQE MWr TLPs, MSI-X MWr TLPs. Never the SQE write by the CPU itself (that stays inside the host).
14. What does Linux show?
Queue count in /proc/interrupts (nvme0q0, nvme0q1 …), blk-mq hardware contexts under /sys/block/nvme0n1/mq/, depth in nr_tags, and the Number of Queues feature through nvme get-feature.
15. What would a validation engineer test?
Minimum and maximum queue sizes against MQES; wrap at every queue size; full CQ behaviour; phase inversion over many wraps; many-to-one mapping; arbitration fairness; invalid doorbell values; queue deletion with commands outstanding.
16. What would a senior engineer suspect when it fails?
Ownership violations (host writing an index it does not own), 0's based off-by-one, missing memory barrier before the doorbell, phase not inverted on wrap, CQ undersized for a shared mapping, doorbell stride miscalculated, stale cached CQE reads.

Hardware view

Inside the controller, each queue is a small record in on-chip memory or registers. A plausible (implementation-dependent) set of per-queue state is:

Doorbell writes land in a small register block. The controller hardware typically compares the new SQ tail with its internal SQ head and, if they differ, queues work for a fetch engine. The fetch engine issues PCIe Memory Read requests for one or several SQEs at a time — a controller may read four SQEs (256 bytes) in one request if they are contiguous and within the Max Read Request Size. CQEs are posted by a separate engine that writes 16 bytes per entry, sometimes several entries in one write when they are consecutive.

On the host side there is no special hardware: the queues are ordinary memory pages, made coherent with CPU caches by the platform. The device's reads of SQEs are snooped, so the device sees what the CPU wrote, provided the CPU's write has left the store buffer — which is why the driver needs a write memory barrier before the doorbell. The device's CQE writes invalidate cached copies, so the host's next read of the CQE sees new data.

Each SQE fetch costs a full PCIe read round trip: the request goes up to the root complex, the root complex reads memory, and the completion comes back — often several hundred nanoseconds to around a microsecond on a real platform (Implementation-dependent). That is why controllers prefetch several SQEs at once and why an optional feature allows SQs to live in device memory (the Controller Memory Buffer), turning the host's SQE writes into posted writes across the link.

Specification view

Queue architecture is defined across two documents of the NVMe 2.4 specification set:

  • NVM Express Base Specification, Revision 2.4 — Theory of Operation / queueing model, Controller Registers, Admin Command Set: SQ/CQ definitions, full/empty, the phase tag, CQE layout, arbitration, CAP/CC fields, Create/Delete I/O queue commands.
  • NVM Express over PCIe Transport Specification, Revision 1.4: the PCIe-specific mapping, including doorbell registers, queue memory in host memory, PRP-based queue description and interrupts.

The queueing model is Normative. Descriptions of one queue per core, and figures showing it, are Informative: the specification allows it but does not require it.

  • SQ entries are 64 bytes and CQ entries 16 bytes for the NVM Command Set (CC.IOSQES = 6, CC.IOCQES = 4 select 26 and 24 bytes).
  • A queue is empty when head equals tail and full when head equals tail + 1 with wrap.
  • CAP.MQES (bits 15:00) is 0's based, minimum 1h.
  • CAP.CQR (bit 16) set to 1 means I/O queues must be physically contiguous.
  • The CQE carries SQ Head Pointer, SQ Identifier, Command Identifier, Phase Tag and Status.
  • Round robin arbitration is mandatory; weighted round robin with urgent priority class and vendor specific are optional and advertised in CAP.AMS.
  • The Admin queue has identifier 0.
  • A queue of N entries holds at most N − 1 outstanding entries (follows from the full rule).
  • A host that never allows more outstanding commands than the CQ can hold can never be blocked by a full CQ.
  • Commands submitted to different SQs have no ordering relationship at all; even within one SQ, completion order is not submission order.
  • The number of I/O queues a controller supports (granted through the Number of Queues feature).
  • Whether the controller supports non-contiguous queues (CQR = 0) and weighted round robin.
  • How many SQEs the controller fetches per read, and when it decides to fetch.
  • How quickly the controller notices a doorbell and the internal scheduling of commands after fetch.
  • The host's choice of queue count, depth and CPU mapping (a driver policy, not a spec requirement).

The basic queue model has been stable since NVMe 1.0. Later revisions added features around it: the Controller Memory Buffer for queues (NVMe 1.2), the Doorbell Buffer Config command for shadow doorbells (NVMe 1.3), NVM Sets with an NVM Set Identifier in Create I/O SQ (NVMe 1.4), and the reorganisation into the Base plus Transport specifications (NVMe 2.0). In the 2.x documents the PCIe-specific queue details sit in the NVMe over PCIe Transport specification. Check the revision you are working against.

  • The specification does not say how many queues a host should create or how they map to CPUs.
  • It does not guarantee completion order relative to submission order.
  • It does not define how long a controller may take to notice a doorbell write.
  • Check in the specification: the exact maximum Admin queue size and its interaction with MQES for your revision; whether the CQ memory must be zeroed by the host or merely have phase tags of 0.
  • Never assume completions arrive in submission order.
  • Never assume an SQ slot is reusable just because you sent the command; it is reusable when an SQHD value has moved past it.
  • Never assume MQES is the count; add one.
  • Never assume a controller supports weighted round robin or non-contiguous queues; read CAP.

Code / pseudocode

The driver-side queue logic, reduced to essentials. This is not a real driver: it ignores locking, error handling and timeouts.

struct nvme_cqe { uint32_t dw0, dw1, dw2, dw3; };   /* 16 bytes */

struct queue_pair {
    uint8_t          *sq;          /* SQ base, size * 64 bytes   */
    struct nvme_cqe  *cq;          /* CQ base, size * 16 bytes   */
    uint16_t size;                 /* entries (NOT 0's based)    */
    uint16_t sq_tail;              /* host-owned                 */
    uint16_t sq_head;              /* host's copy, from SQHD     */
    uint16_t cq_head;              /* host-owned                 */
    uint8_t  cq_phase;             /* expected phase, starts 1   */
    volatile uint32_t *sq_db, *cq_db;  /* doorbell MMIO pointers   */
};

bool sq_full(struct queue_pair *q) {
    return (uint16_t)((q->sq_tail + 1) % q->size) == q->sq_head;
}

int submit(struct queue_pair *q, const void *sqe64) {
    if (sq_full(q)) return -EBUSY;
    memcpy(q->sq + q->sq_tail * 64, sqe64, 64);
    q->sq_tail = (q->sq_tail + 1) % q->size;
    wmb();                          /* SQE must be in memory before the doorbell */
    writel(q->sq_tail, q->sq_db);   /* posted MWr to SQyTDBL */
    return 0;
}

int reap(struct queue_pair *q) {
    int n = 0;
    for (;;) {
        struct nvme_cqe *e = &q->cq[q->cq_head];
        uint32_t dw3 = READ_ONCE(e->dw3);
        if (((dw3 >> 16) & 1) != q->cq_phase) break;   /* not new */
        rmb();                      /* read the rest only after seeing P */
        q->sq_head = e->dw2 & 0xFFFF;                  /* SQHD frees SQ slots */
        complete_command(dw3 & 0xFFFF, dw3 >> 17, e->dw0);
        if (++q->cq_head == q->size) { q->cq_head = 0; q->cq_phase ^= 1; }
        n++;
    }
    if (n) writel(q->cq_head, q->cq_db);              /* posted MWr to CQyHDBL */
    return n;
}

Note the rmb() after checking the phase tag: on weakly ordered CPUs the host must not read the CQE's other Dwords before confirming the phase, or it could combine a fresh phase bit with stale contents. On x86 this is a compiler barrier only; on Arm it is a real barrier.

Worked example

Problem. A variant of the Lab SSD (not the canonical CAP from Chapter 24) reports CAP = 0x0000_0020_2803_03FF. The driver creates I/O CQ1 and SQ1 with Queue Size = 3FFh, maps SQ1 to CQ1 with interrupt vector 1, and submits commands. At some moment the host's view is: SQ1 tail = 1020, last SQHD seen = 1018; CQ1 head = 1022, expected phase = 1. The controller then posts four CQEs. Answer: (a) how many entries do the queues have; (b) is SQ1 full; (c) where do the four CQEs land and with which phase; (d) what will the host write to CQ1HDBL after reaping all four?

(a) CAP.MQES = bits 15:00 = 03FFh, 0's based → 1024 entries maximum. Queue Size 3FFh → 1024 entries each. CAP bit 16 (CQR) = 1 → the queues must be contiguous, so PC = 1 in both create commands. CAP bits 18:17 (AMS) = 01b → weighted round robin with urgent priority class is supported. CAP bits 31:24 (TO) = 28h. DSTRD (bits 35:32) = 0.

(b) Entries in use from the host's view = (1020 − 1018 + 1024) mod 1024 = 2. Full would need (1020 + 1) mod 1024 = 1018 — false. The SQ has 1021 free slots (1023 usable − 2).

(c) The controller's tail matches the host's head at 1022 (the host had consumed everything). The four CQEs go to slots 1022, 1023, 0, 1. Slots 1022 and 1023 are written with the current phase; the tail then wraps and the phase inverts. What is the current phase? The host expects 1 at 1022, so the controller writes P = 1 into 1022 and 1023, then P = 0 into slots 0 and 1. Addresses: 0x1_2341_0000 + 1022 × 16 = 0x1_2341_3FE0, then 0x3FF0, then 0x0000, then 0x0010.

(d) The host consumes 1022 (P = 1 ✓), 1023 (P = 1 ✓, head wraps to 0, expected phase becomes 0), slot 0 (P = 0 ✓), slot 1 (P = 0 ✓), then slot 2 has P = 1 from the previous pass — stop. Head = 2, so the host writes 0x0000_0002 to CQ1HDBL at BAR0 + 0x100C.

Debugging connection

Most queue bugs reduce to one question: which side's view of which index is wrong? A disciplined approach:

  1. Reconstruct each index from evidence. SQ tail: last value written to SQyTDBL. SQ head: highest SQE address fetched, or the latest SQHD in a CQE. CQ tail: address of the latest CQE written. CQ head: last value written to CQyHDBL.
  2. Check them against the full/empty rules. Is the controller's CQ full given the last published head? Has the SQ tail moved past the head by more than size − 1?
  3. Check the phase. Decode bit 16 of DW3 in each CQE write. Does it invert exactly when the CQE address wraps back to the CQ base?
  4. Check ownership. Any host write to a doorbell offset belonging to a queue that does not exist? Any CQE written outside the CQ's memory?

The "stuck after exactly one queue's worth of I/O" symptom almost always points to a wrap bug: phase not inverted, head not published, or modulo arithmetic done with the 0's based size. Count the completions before the hang; if the count equals the queue size or size − 1, look at wrap handling first.

Senior engineer insight

  • The queue is a contract about ownership, not a data structure. When reviewing a driver or firmware change, ask for every write: "does this agent own this index?" Most queue bugs violate that rule.
  • The CQ is the backpressure point. A controller can always fetch; it cannot always post. If a design shares CQs, compute the worst case explicitly.
  • SQHD is the only truth about SQ space. Drivers that infer free space from their own bookkeeping work until a controller fetches ahead of execution — which good controllers do.
  • Arbitration is about fetch, not service. Customers who enable weighted round robin and expect strict latency isolation are often disappointed: once commands are fetched, the back end decides.
  • Per-core queues are a scalability tool with a cost. Each queue consumes controller resources and an interrupt vector; on a 256-core host with a device offering 64 queues, the driver shares queues among cores, and that mapping affects tail latency.
  • The phase tag is also a coherency test. If your platform does not keep device writes coherent with CPU caches (some embedded systems), the host may read a stale P forever. That looks like a device hang.

Step 1 — Run the simulator

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Synthetic NVMe queue-pair simulator for teaching (not a driver).

Models one SQ and one CQ with N entries each, the four indices
(SQ tail/CQ head owned by the host, SQ head/CQ tail owned by the
controller), and the CQE phase tag.
"""

class QueuePair:
    def __init__(self, entries):
        self.n = entries
        self.sq = [None] * entries          # SQ slots (command ids)
        self.cq = [(0, None)] * entries      # CQ slots: (phase, cid); memory zeroed
        self.sq_tail = 0      # host-owned, published through the SQ tail doorbell
        self.sq_head = 0      # controller-owned, reported back in CQE SQHD
        self.cq_tail = 0      # controller-owned, never visible as a register
        self.cq_head = 0      # host-owned, published through the CQ head doorbell
        self.ctrl_phase = 1   # phase the controller writes on this pass
        self.host_phase = 1   # phase the host expects for a new entry
        self.host_sq_head = 0 # host's latest knowledge of SQ head (from SQHD)
        self.doorbell_sq = 0  # last value written to SQ tail doorbell
        self.doorbell_cq = 0  # last value written to CQ head doorbell

    # ---------------- host side
    def sq_full(self):
        return (self.sq_tail + 1) % self.n == self.host_sq_head

    def submit(self, cid):
        if self.sq_full():
            return False
        self.sq[self.sq_tail] = cid
        self.sq_tail = (self.sq_tail + 1) % self.n
        return True

    def ring_sq_doorbell(self):
        self.doorbell_sq = self.sq_tail

    def reap(self):
        """Consume every new CQE; return list of (slot, cid, sqhd)."""
        done = []
        while True:
            phase, entry = self.cq[self.cq_head]
            if phase != self.host_phase:
                break
            cid, sqhd = entry
            done.append((self.cq_head, cid, sqhd))
            self.host_sq_head = sqhd
            self.cq_head = (self.cq_head + 1) % self.n
            if self.cq_head == 0:
                self.host_phase ^= 1
        if done:
            self.doorbell_cq = self.cq_head
        return done

    # ---------------- controller side
    def fetch_and_complete(self, max_cmds):
        posted = 0
        while self.sq_head != self.doorbell_sq and posted < max_cmds:
            if (self.cq_tail + 1) % self.n == self.doorbell_cq:
                break                         # CQ full: must not post
            cid = self.sq[self.sq_head]
            self.sq_head = (self.sq_head + 1) % self.n
            self.cq[self.cq_tail] = (self.ctrl_phase, (cid, self.sq_head))
            self.cq_tail = (self.cq_tail + 1) % self.n
            if self.cq_tail == 0:
                self.ctrl_phase ^= 1
            posted += 1
        return posted

    def show(self, note):
        bits = "".join(str(p) for p, _ in self.cq)
        print(f"{note:<34} SQ h/t={self.sq_head}/{self.sq_tail} "
              f"CQ h/t={self.cq_head}/{self.cq_tail} CQ P-bits={bits} "
              f"host expects P={self.host_phase}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    qp = QueuePair(4)
    qp.show("start (memory zeroed)")
    cid = 0x10
    for rnd in range(1, 5):

        while qp.submit(cid):
            cid += 1
            sent += 1

In reap(), delete the line self.host_phase ^= 1. Run again. Record the round in which the host stops reaping and what the P bits look like at that moment.

Observation

Write down, for each broken variant, the index values at the moment progress stops, and which side "looks guilty" from the outside.

Questions

  1. Why does round 1 submit only 3 commands into a 4-entry SQ?
  2. In round 2, why can the host submit 2 more commands even though only 2 of the first 3 have completed?
  3. In Step 2, which CIDs are sitting in the CQ unconsumed when progress stops?
  4. In Step 3, how many completions are posted in total, and why that number?

Challenge

Change the controller to post completions in reverse order of the fetched batch (out-of-order completion). Does anything in the host logic break? What must the host use, instead of slot position, to find the command each CQE belongs to?

Hints

For question 2, look at what host_sq_head is set to from SQHD. For Step 3, compute the CQ full condition with the doorbell value stuck at 0.

Solution

Q1. The full rule leaves one slot empty: with head 0, the host can fill slots 0, 1, 2; writing slot 3 would make tail 0 = head, indistinguishable from empty.

Q2. The controller fetched up to its head 2 but posted only two CQEs, each carrying SQHD = 1 then 2. The host sets its SQ-head copy to 2, so slots 0 and 1 are free — even though the third command (slot 2) has not completed. SQ space is freed by fetch (SQHD), not by completion. In this simulation fetch and completion happen together, so SQHD reflects that; in a real controller SQHD can run ahead of completions.

Step 2. The host reaps 0x10–0x13 in rounds 1–2. In round 3 the controller posts 0x14 and 0x15 into slots 0 and 1 with P = 0, but the host still expects P = 1, sees 0, and stops. P bits become 0011, host expects 1 — nothing is ever reaped again. From outside, the device "looks guilty" (commands never complete), but a trace would show the CQE writes. CIDs 0x14 and 0x15 sit unconsumed.

Step 3. The controller's view of the CQ head stays 0. It posts slots 0, 1, 2 (three CQEs); posting slot 3 would make (3 + 1) mod 4 = 0 = head — full. Three completions in total, then the controller correctly refuses to post. The SQ also stalls, because the host never sees SQHD advance past 3.

Challenge. Nothing in the phase logic breaks: the phase depends on CQ slot position, not on which command completed. The host must use the CID (and SQID for shared CQs) to find the command, never the slot number.

What a senior engineer notices

Both bugs produce the same outward symptom — a hang after about one queue's worth of I/O — but opposite evidence: the phase bug shows CQE writes with no CQ head doorbell; the doorbell bug shows the controller going silent after exactly size − 1 CQEs. One analyzer filter (CQ base range plus doorbell range) separates them in seconds.

Failure variants

  • Do modulo arithmetic with the 0's based size (e.g. % 3 for a 4-entry queue): the last slot is never used and the phase flips one slot early.
  • Initialise host_phase = 0: the host immediately reaps every zeroed slot as a completion.
  • Skip zeroing of CQ memory (initialise slots with P = 1): phantom completions on the first pass.

Extension

Add a second SQ that maps to the same CQ. Give the CQ 4 entries and each SQ 4 entries. Find a submission pattern that fills the CQ and stalls the controller even though neither SQ is full. Then size the CQ so that it can never happen.

Exercises

  1. A CQ has 8 entries. The host's head is 6 and it expects P = 0. Slots 6, 7, 0, 1 contain P = 0, 0, 1, 1. How many new entries are there, and what is the host's expected phase after consuming them?
    Answer

    Slots 6 and 7 are new (P = 0). Head wraps to 0 and the expected phase becomes 1. Slots 0 and 1 have P = 1 — they are also new (the controller has wrapped too). Slot 2 must be checked: if it has P = 0, stop. So at least 4 new entries, and after consuming them the expected phase is 1 with head at 2 (or further).

  2. CAP.MQES = 003Fh. What is the largest Queue Size value the host may put in Create I/O SQ, and how many commands can that queue hold at once?
    Answer

    MQES 3Fh → 64 entries. Queue Size field 3Fh (0's based, 64 entries). It holds at most 63 commands simultaneously.

  3. Explain in two sentences why there is no "CQ tail doorbell".
    Answer

    The CQ tail is owned by the controller, and the host learns about new entries from the phase tag in memory. A doorbell is how the host tells the controller about indices the host owns, so only the SQ tail and CQ head have doorbells.

  4. Three SQs (each 256 entries) share one CQ. What minimum CQ size guarantees that the controller can never find the CQ full, assuming the host never has more commands outstanding than the SQ full rule permits?
    Answer

    If the driver caps each SQ at 255 outstanding commands (the most a 256-slot SQ can hold), up to 765 completions can be pending at once. A CQ holds N − 1 entries, so it needs at least 766 slots. Careful: because SQHD frees SQ slots at fetch time, a driver could in principle have more commands outstanding than its SQ holds; the real bound is the driver's own limit on outstanding commands (its tag count), and the CQ must be sized from that.

  5. A driver uses weighted round robin and puts its latency-sensitive queue in the Urgent class. List two reasons why latency might still be poor.
    Answer

    Arbitration governs fetch only — commands already inside the controller from other queues still compete for media; and the Arbitration Burst may let a low-priority queue fetch several commands in a burst once selected. Also the controller may not prioritise internally, and host-side queuing (blk-mq) happens before the SQ.

Quiz

  1. Which agent owns the SQ head?

    • The host driver
    • The NVMe controller
    • The root complex
    • Whichever wrote the last doorbell
    Answer and reasoning

    The controller consumes the SQ, so it advances the SQ head, and reports it in the SQHD field of each CQE.

  2. A queue has 16 slots, head 9, tail 8. It is:

    • Empty
    • Holding 1 entry
    • Full
    • Invalid
    Answer and reasoning

    (8 + 1) mod 16 = 9 = head → full, with 15 entries in use.

  3. CAP.MQES = 07FFh means each I/O queue may have at most:

    • 2047 entries
    • 2048 entries
    • 2049 entries
    • 4096 entries
    Answer and reasoning

    0's based: 7FFh + 1 = 2048.

  4. The host finds the CQE at its head has P = 0 but it expects P = 1. What does this mean?

    • No new completion is there yet
    • The command failed
    • The controller reset
    • The CQ is full
    Answer and reasoning

    A mismatching phase means the entry is from the previous pass (or zeroed memory). Status is a separate field.

  5. Which mapping is permitted?

    • SQ1 and SQ2 both complete into CQ1
    • SQ1 completes into both CQ1 and CQ2
    • The Admin SQ completes into CQ1
    • An I/O SQ completes into the Admin CQ
    Answer and reasoning

    Many SQs may share one CQ. One SQ has exactly one CQ. The Admin SQ and Admin CQ are a fixed pair.

  6. CAP.CQR = 1. What must the host do?

    • Use PRP lists for every queue
    • Use 32-entry queues
    • Create I/O queues as physically contiguous (PC = 1)
    • Disable weighted round robin
    Answer and reasoning

    Contiguous Queues Required means the controller does not accept PRP-list-described I/O queues.

  7. Which arbitration mechanism must every controller support?

    • Round robin
    • Weighted round robin with urgent priority class
    • Vendor specific
    • Strict priority by queue identifier
    Answer and reasoning

    Round robin is mandatory; the others are optional and reported in CAP.AMS.

  8. Why does a CQE carry SQHD?

    Model answer

    Because the SQ head is owned by the controller and there is no register the host can cheaply read for it. Reporting it in every CQE lets the host know which SQ slots the controller has fetched and may be reused, without any MMIO read.

  9. On the PCIe link, which queue-related actions are visible, and which are not?

    Model answer

    Visible: doorbell writes (host→device MWr), SQE fetches (device MRd plus root-complex CplD), CQE posts (device MWr), MSI-X interrupts (device MWr). Not visible: the CPU writing SQEs into host memory, the host reading CQEs, and internal head/tail state.

  10. Explain why a 4-entry CQ that has wrapped twice can be read correctly by a host that never zeroes it again.

    Model answer

    After the first wrap, every slot contains P = 1 from pass 1; the controller writes P = 0 on pass 2 and the host expects 0. After the second wrap, slots contain 0 and the controller writes 1 while the host expects 1. In every pass, the stale value differs from the expected value, so zeroing is only needed once, at creation.

Interview questions

  1. Beginner: What are the SQ and CQ, and where do they live?
    Model answer

    Circular arrays in host memory. The SQ holds 64-byte commands written by the host and fetched by the controller; the CQ holds 16-byte completions written by the controller and read by the host. Only the doorbells live in the device.

  2. Beginner: Why is one slot always empty?
    Model answer

    Because empty is defined as head = tail; a completely full queue would also have head = tail. Leaving one slot unused makes full distinguishable: (tail + 1) mod N = head.

  3. Intermediate: Walk me through the phase tag.
    Model answer

    CQ memory starts with phase 0. The controller writes P = 1 on the first pass and inverts on every wrap. The host expects 1 initially and inverts its expectation when its head wraps. An entry whose P matches the expectation is new; the first mismatch ends the batch. It replaces a CQ tail register the host would otherwise have to read over PCIe.

  4. Intermediate: How does the host know it may reuse an SQ slot?
    Model answer

    From the SQHD field of CQEs: the controller reports how far it has fetched. Slots before SQHD are free. Using completion counts instead is wrong when the controller fetches ahead.

  5. Advanced: Why might you map several SQs to one CQ, and what is the risk?
    Model answer

    To use separate priority classes or more SQs than interrupt vectors/CQs while keeping a single completion path. The risk is CQ overflow pressure: the CQ must absorb the combined outstanding commands of every SQ, or the controller stalls posting and all SQs suffer.

  6. Advanced: A customer says enabling WRR with an urgent queue did not reduce p99 latency. What do you tell them?
    Model answer

    Arbitration selects which SQ to fetch from; it does not preempt work already fetched or scheduled to media. If background queues are deep, the device back end is saturated and fetch priority helps little. Measure fetch-to-completion versus doorbell-to-fetch latency in a trace to see where time goes; consider limiting background queue depth.

  7. Senior: Design a test plan for queue wrap correctness on a new controller.
    Model answer

    For queue sizes 2, 3, a power of two, a non-power of two, and MQES+1: run many wraps with mixed batch sizes; vary CQ head doorbell timing (immediate, lazy, near-full); verify phase inversion exactly at slot 0; verify SQHD monotonic modulo size; test shared CQ near full; inject invalid doorbell values and confirm the controller's response; include delete/recreate between runs with non-zeroed memory to catch phase initialisation errors; cross-check every CQE with an analyzer.

  8. Senior: The host hangs after exactly 1023 completions on a 1024-entry queue. Give your top three hypotheses and the one trace filter that separates them.
    Model answer

    (1) Host does not invert phase on wrap; (2) host never writes CQ head doorbell, so the controller sees a full CQ; (3) host SQ-full logic never learns SQHD and stops submitting. Filter: host→device MWr to the doorbell range plus device→host MWr into the CQ address range. Hypothesis 1 shows CQEs after the wrap with flipped P; 2 shows no CQ head doorbell and CQE posts stop at 1023; 3 shows no new SQ tail doorbells.

Teach-back challenge

Explain the queue pair three times, out loud or in writing:

  • To a child: the spinning trays, the bell, the coloured stickers that change every time round.
  • To a junior engineer: the four indices and their owners, the full/empty rule, the phase tag, SQHD — in under two minutes, with a ring diagram.
  • To a senior engineer: why the design needs no register reads on the fast path, where backpressure lives (the CQ), and what a trace can and cannot prove about queue state.
Model explanation for the senior engineer

NVMe's queue pair is a single-producer/single-consumer ring in each direction, with ownership split so that each side only writes indices it owns. The host publishes its two indices with posted MMIO writes; the controller publishes its SQ head in-band in CQEs and its CQ tail implicitly through the phase tag. No non-posted MMIO read is needed on the I/O path. Backpressure lives in the CQ: the controller can always fetch but must not post into a full CQ, so CQ sizing and timely head doorbells bound throughput. A trace shows published indices (doorbell values) and controller actions (fetch and post addresses) but never host memory accesses; you reconstruct state, you don't observe it.

Chapter summary

NVMe communicates through circular Submission and Completion Queues in host memory. The host owns the SQ tail and CQ head and publishes them through doorbell writes; the controller owns the SQ head (reported in CQE SQHD) and CQ tail (implied by the phase tag). A queue is empty when head = tail and full when tail + 1 = head with wrap, so N slots hold N − 1 entries. MQES gives the maximum queue size as a 0's based value; CQR says whether I/O queues must be contiguous. Queues are usually created per CPU; several SQs may share one CQ; arbitration (round robin, optionally weighted round robin with an urgent class) chooses which SQ to fetch from. On the link, queues appear only as doorbell writes, SQE fetches, CQE posts and interrupts.
