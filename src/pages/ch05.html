A PCIe link is made of lanes, and a lane is made of two differential pairs: one transmitting, one receiving. This chapter opens the lane up: how bits become voltages, why there is no separate clock wire, how a x4 SSD and a x16 slot agree on a width, how two ports agree on a speed, and why an old card still works in a new slot.

Why this chapter exists

In Chapter 4 a link was a line on a diagram. In the lab, a link is copper: traces on a motherboard, a connector, perhaps a riser, a cable, a retimer, and the pins of an SSD controller. Most "my NVMe drive is slow" or "my drive disappeared" cases that are not software turn out to be physical: the link trained at x2 instead of x4, or at Gen3 instead of Gen4, or it keeps dropping into recovery. To reason about those cases you need a working model of the physical lane, and of how width and speed are negotiated rather than fixed.

This chapter is also the vocabulary foundation for Chapter 6 (generations and bandwidth arithmetic), Chapter 7 (the Physical Layer's place in the layer model), and Chapters 8–9 (the training state machine).

The story

Two friends live on opposite sides of a noisy playground. They want to send messages to each other at the same time, so they stretch two pairs of strings between their windows: one pair for Ana to send to Ben, and one pair for Ben to send to Ana. That is one lane.

Why a pair of strings for each direction? Because the playground is noisy. When the wind shakes the strings, it shakes both strings in a pair the same way. So Ana sends a message by pulling one string up while letting the other go down. Ben does not care how high either string is; he only looks at which one is higher. Wind moves both together, so it does not change the answer.

If they need to send faster, they stretch more lanes side by side: two, four, eight, sixteen. Before they start, they check how many lanes actually reach the other window (some might be broken) and how fast they can both pull strings. They start slowly and speed up only if both can keep up.

Professional audio uses balanced cables (the three-pin XLR microphone cable). The signal is sent as two copies, one inverted. At the mixing desk, the receiver subtracts one from the other. Hum picked up along a 30-metre cable appears on both wires equally and cancels out, while the wanted signal doubles. A PCIe differential pair is the same trick at gigahertz frequencies.

For negotiation, think of a video call: both laptops start at a low, safe resolution, measure the connection, then step up to the best quality both cameras, both screens and the network can sustain. If the connection degrades, they drop back.

  • Audio cables carry analog signals at kilohertz; PCIe carries digital symbols at tens of gigatransfers per second, where the copper itself smears pulses into each other (inter-symbol interference). Noise cancellation is only part of the battle; equalisation is the other part.
  • A video call adapts continuously. A PCIe link negotiates speed and width during training and retraining, not on every packet. While a speed change happens, no data flows on the link.
  • The strings story implies each direction can be broken independently. That is true electrically, but a lane is only usable if both directions work: training requires a reply.
  • "Which string is higher" suggests two levels. From Gen6 onward PCIe uses four voltage levels per symbol (PAM4), not two (Chapter 6).

Beginner explanation

A PCIe lane is four wires:

  • two wires forming a differential pair that carries bits from port A to port B, driven by A's transmitter (TX) into B's receiver (RX);
  • two wires forming another differential pair from B's TX to A's RX.

Both directions run at the same time. This is called dual-simplex: two one-way roads, not one two-way road that takes turns.

There is no separate wire carrying a clock alongside the data. The receiver works out the timing by watching the edges in the data itself. To make sure there are always enough edges, PCIe encodes and scrambles the data so long runs of identical bits do not happen.

A link with more lanes moves more bytes at once. Bytes of a packet are dealt across lanes like cards dealt to players: byte 0 to lane 0, byte 1 to lane 1, and so on. The receiver collects them back in order.

When the system powers on, the two ports find out how many lanes are connected and working, and agree on a width. They always start at the slowest speed (2.5 GT/s) and then move up to the fastest speed both support. If something is wrong with the wiring, the link may settle for fewer lanes or a lower speed, and still work. That is a feature for availability, and a trap for performance.

Engineering explanation

The four-lane link

The master diagram of this chapter: a x4 link between our lab system's Root Port and NVMe SSD.

Differential pair

A differential pair is two conductors routed tightly together, carrying complementary signals: when one leg (D+) goes up, the other (D−) goes down by the same amount. The receiver measures only the difference D+ − D−.

  • Noise immunity: interference that couples equally into both legs (common-mode noise) disappears in the subtraction.
  • Lower emissions: the opposite currents in the two legs produce fields that largely cancel, so the pair radiates less.
  • Small swings: because the receiver looks at a difference, each leg can swing by only a few hundred millivolts, which saves power and permits high frequencies.
  • Controlled impedance: the pair is designed as a transmission line with a defined differential impedance (on the order of 85–100 Ω depending on generation and form factor; check the relevant electrical and form-factor specifications) so the signal travels without large reflections.

A "1" is represented by D+ above D− and a "0" by the reverse (for the two-level signalling of Gen1 to Gen5). Swapping the legs of a pair on a board (D+ to D−) inverts every bit; PCIe receivers handle that automatically (polarity inversion, below).

Transmitter (TX)

A port's transmitter takes bytes from the Physical Layer's logic and puts voltages on the wire. Its main blocks:

  • Encoder/scrambler (logical): turns bytes into a DC-balanced, transition-rich bit stream (8b/10b at Gen1–2; scrambling plus 128b/130b at Gen3–5; Flit Mode framing with PAM4 at Gen6–7; Chapter 6).
  • Serialiser: shifts the parallel data out one unit interval (UI) at a time. At 16 GT/s, one UI is 62.5 picoseconds.
  • Driver with transmit equalisation: a finite impulse response filter (feed-forward equaliser) that pre-distorts the signal, for example de-emphasising repeated bits, so that after the lossy channel the eye opens at the receiver. Coefficients are set during equalization (Chapter 9, Chapter 54).
  • Receiver detection circuit: used in the Detect state to sense whether a receiver termination is present on the other end of each lane.
  • Electrical Idle capability: the transmitter can hold both legs at the same common-mode voltage (no differential signal) to save power or to signal state changes.

An AC coupling capacitor sits in series with each leg of every transmit pair (on the transmitter's side of the channel, often on the add-in card or motherboard near the TX pins). It blocks DC so that the two ports can use different common-mode voltages. It is also why receiver detection works the way it does: the transmitter sees a different charging time when a terminated receiver is present behind the capacitor.

Receiver (RX)

  • Termination: a defined impedance at the receiver input that absorbs the arriving wave and that the far transmitter can detect.
  • Continuous-time linear equaliser (CTLE): boosts high frequencies to undo the channel's low-pass behaviour.
  • Decision feedback equaliser (DFE): subtracts the predicted trailing influence of previous bits (post-cursor interference). Used from Gen3 upward in practice; more taps at higher rates.
  • Clock and data recovery (CDR): a loop that locks a local sampling clock to the transitions in the incoming data and decides the best sampling instant in each UI.
  • Deserialiser and decoder/descrambler: rebuild bytes, find symbol or block boundaries, and remove encoding.
  • Electrical Idle detector: notices when the far transmitter stops driving.
  • Elastic buffer: absorbs the tiny frequency difference between the recovered clock and the local clock, using SKP ordered sets that the transmitter inserts periodically (Chapter 7).

The transmitter and receiver pair together is usually called a SerDes (serialiser/deserialiser). In an SSD controller ASIC, the PCIe SerDes is a hard analog block, typically licensed IP, sitting next to the digital PCIe controller (the logic that implements LTSSM, data link and transaction layers). Many "PCIe problems" are really interactions between SerDes tuning, firmware settings and the board channel.

Clocking: embedded clock and reference clock

PCIe sends no clock alongside data on each lane. The receiver's CDR recovers timing from the data transitions. That is why encoding and scrambling must guarantee transitions.

Both ports still need a reference clock (REFCLK) to generate their transmit bit rate. The standard reference is 100 MHz. Three clocking architectures exist:

A receiver tolerates it, provided the architecture is set up consistently. A mismatch (one side expects common clock, the board actually gives separate clocks with SSC) is a classic source of links that train but then accumulate errors. Implementation-dependent

Lane

Formally, a lane is the pair of differential pairs described above, plus the per-lane logic that runs on them: its own CDR, its own symbol/block lock, its own scrambler state, and a lane number assigned during training. A lane is never used alone for data unless the link is x1; otherwise it is one stripe of a link.

Link and deskew

The link aggregates lanes. Because each lane's traces have slightly different lengths and each lane's CDR locks independently, symbols that were transmitted together arrive at slightly different times. During training, the receiver measures this lane-to-lane skew using ordered sets that are sent simultaneously on all lanes, and inserts per-lane delay so the lanes line up. The specification bounds how much skew a receiver must tolerate, and the bound is expressed in time and scaled by generation. Sideband signals

Besides the data lanes and REFCLK, a slot or connector carries a few low-speed signals that matter enormously in debugging:

  • PERST# (PCI Express Reset): the host holds it asserted until power and REFCLK are stable; de-assertion starts the device's link training. A PERST# released too early or glitching is a top cause of devices missing at boot.
  • CLKREQ#: lets the device request REFCLK, and (with L1 PM Substates) lets the clock be gated in low-power states.
  • WAKE#: lets a device request power to be restored from a deep low-power state (alternatively an in-band Beacon).
  • PRSNT# pins / presence detect: tell the system a card or drive is present (used by hot-plug controllers).
  • SMBus: a management bus used by NVMe Management Interface out-of-band management (Chapter 63).

Link width negotiation

Width is negotiated by the Link Training and Status State Machine (LTSSM, Chapter 8) in three steps:

  1. Receiver detection (Detect state). Each port's transmitter tests each of its lanes for a receiver termination at the far end. Lanes with no receiver are not used. This is how a x16 Root Port finds that only four lanes of the slot are connected to an x4 SSD.
  2. Bit lock and symbol/block lock (Polling state). On the detected lanes, both sides send training sequences (TS1, then TS2). Each receiver locks onto the bit stream, finds symbol or block boundaries, and corrects polarity if needed.
  3. Link and lane numbering (Configuration state). The downstream port proposes a link number and lane numbers in TS1 ordered sets; the upstream port echoes them back on the lanes it can use; both settle on the largest valid width that both support and that is working. Lanes are numbered starting at 0; the link must be formed from a contiguous set starting at lane 0 (or, with lane reversal, at the opposite end).

If a lane fails part-way (it detected a receiver but cannot lock), the link can still form at a smaller valid width. A x4 SSD with a damaged lane 2 can train at x2 (lanes 0–1). It cannot train as "x3".

The specification defines which widths a port must be able to down-configure to, and the precise rules for forming a link on a subset of lanes. Support for widths other than the maximum and x1 has been required or optional differently across revisions. Uncertain Confirm the current rule in the Configuration substates and the Link Capabilities description of the Base Specification for your revision.

Lane reversal and polarity inversion

Lane reversal: on some boards it is easier to route lane 0 of the Root Port to lane 3 of the device, lane 1 to lane 2, and so on. During Configuration, the ports detect that lane numbers arrive reversed and one side remaps its lanes internally. Software sees an ordinary x4 link. Whether a given port supports lane reversal is implementation-dependent; the specification describes the mechanism and requires that at least one side can handle it for certain configurations. Confirm details in the Configuration substates. Implementation-dependent

Polarity inversion: if D+ and D− of a pair are swapped on the board, the receiver sees every bit inverted. During Polling, the receiver recognises inverted training symbols and inverts its input. Receivers are required to support this. Normative

Speed negotiation

Every link begins life at 2.5 GT/s with 8b/10b encoding, whatever its eventual speed. This guarantees that any two PCIe ports, from any generation, can talk at all. During training, each port advertises the data rates it supports in the training sets it sends. Once the link reaches L0 (normal operation) at 2.5 GT/s, a port that has seen a higher common rate (normally the downstream port, under hardware or software direction) initiates a change by entering the Recovery state, both sides switch rates, and Recovery completes at the new rate. For 8.0 GT/s and above, equalisation must be performed at that rate before it is reliable. If the faster rate fails, the link falls back to a lower one. Chapter 9 traces this step by step.

Compatibility

PCIe promises compatibility on three axes:

  • Speed: every port supports 2.5 GT/s, so a Gen5 SSD in a Gen3 slot runs at Gen3, and a Gen3 SSD in a Gen5 slot runs at Gen3. The link runs at the highest rate both support and the channel can sustain.
  • Width: a link trains at the widest width both ports and the wiring support. Up-plugging (x4 card into x16 slot) and down-plugging (x16 card into a slot wired as x4, using an open-ended or larger slot) both work electrically, at the smaller width.
  • Mechanical: a shorter card fits in a longer CEM slot. A longer card needs an open-ended slot. Form factors such as M.2 and U.2 have their own keying.

Compatibility means "it will work", not "it will perform". A Gen4 x4 SSD in a slot wired x1 is compatible and roughly sixteen times slower than intended. The link reports this honestly (LnkSta), but nothing will raise an error.

Channel loss, retimers and redrivers

Each generation doubles the signalling rate (or, for Gen6, keeps the Nyquist frequency of Gen5 by using PAM4), and copper loss rises with frequency. The specification defines a channel loss budget for each rate; commonly cited end-to-end figures are in the low-to-mid 20s of decibels at Gen3, high 20s at Gen4 and mid-30s at Gen5, measured at the Nyquist frequency. Uncertain Treat those numbers as orientation; confirm them in the electrical sub-block chapter for your revision.

When a board route exceeds the budget, designers add a retimer, which participates in the protocol (it has its own LTSSM-like behaviour and takes part in equalisation) and effectively splits the channel in two, or a redriver, a purely analog amplifier/equaliser that is invisible to the protocol and can make things worse if mistuned. The Base Specification defines retimer requirements and limits how many may be in one link (commonly cited as two). Uncertain

The central concept on the ladder: negotiated width and speed

The friends count how many string pairs really reach the other window and how fast both can pull, then use only those strings at that speed.

Two PCIe ports detect which lanes are connected, agree on the widest working width, start at 2.5 GT/s, and step up to the fastest common rate that works.

Each port's SerDes performs receiver detection per lane, CDR lock, polarity check and lane deskew; the LTSSM logic sequences these steps and programs the SerDes rate and equalisation settings.

The Base Specification's Physical Layer Logical Block defines the LTSSM states, the TS1/TS2 contents (link number, lane number, supported data rates, training control) and the rules for width and rate selection. Link Capabilities reports maximum speed/width; Link Status reports the negotiated ones.

Across the link: TS1 and TS2 ordered sets on every lane, Electrical Idle ordered sets before rate changes, equalisation exchanges at 8 GT/s and above.

An analyzer shows per-lane training sets, the lane numbers each side proposes, the rates advertised, and the time spent in each LTSSM state. A degraded link shows fewer lanes with numbers assigned or a missing Recovery.Speed transition.

"Capable x4, negotiated x2" means look at the physical lanes 2–3 (connector, damaged trace, bent pin, retimer lane). "Capable 16 GT/s, negotiated 8 GT/s" means look at equalisation results, channel loss and the Target Link Speed setting before blaming the device.

The sixteen questions for "a lane"

1. What is it?
One transmit differential pair and one receive differential pair between two ports, plus the per-lane logic that drives them.
2. Why was it created?
To replace wide, skew-limited parallel buses with a narrow, fast, self-clocked serial path that can be replicated for bandwidth.
3. What problem does it solve?
Clock skew, noise and pin count at high data rates.
4. What existed before it?
Parallel buses with a shared clock (PCI, PCI-X, AGP).
5. Why is the current design better?
Each lane carries its own timing; lanes are deskewed independently; bandwidth scales by adding lanes.
6. Who creates it?
The board/form-factor designer (wiring) and the two ports' SerDes (bringing it to life).
7. Who consumes it?
The Physical Layer logic of both ports, which builds a link out of lanes.
8. Where does it live?
On the PCB, connector and device package between two SerDes lanes.
9. What memory/registers are involved?
Link Capabilities (Maximum Link Width), Link Status (Negotiated Link Width); lane-level status in optional extended capabilities such as Lane Error Status and Lane Margining at the Receiver.
10. What happens before it?
Power, REFCLK, PERST# de-assertion, receiver detection.
11. What happens after it?
It joins a link during Configuration and carries striped symbols in L0.
12. What happens if it is wrong?
Degraded width, bit errors (Receiver Errors), repeated Recovery, or no link at all if lane 0 is broken and reversal is not possible.
13. What does the protocol analyzer show?
Per-lane TS ordered sets, lane numbers, per-lane errors (disparity/symbol or sync header errors), Electrical Idle.
14. What does Linux show?
LnkCap/LnkSta width in lspci -vvv; current_link_width in sysfs; AER correctable Receiver Error counts; optionally Lane Error Status.
15. What would a validation engineer test?
Width with each lane deliberately disabled, lane reversal, polarity inversion, margining, temperature/voltage corners, many power cycles.
16. What would a senior engineer suspect when it fails?
Connector or interposer damage, routing errors, AC-capacitor population mistakes, retimer lane faults, crosstalk; then SerDes configuration.

Hardware view

Follow one lane of the lab system physically, from the CPU to the NAND controller:

  1. CPU package: the Root Port's SerDes lane drives a differential pair out of the package through BGA balls into the motherboard.
  2. Motherboard: the pair runs as a tightly coupled stripline in an inner layer, through vias (each via is a small impedance discontinuity), past the AC coupling capacitors, to the connector.
  3. Connector: an M.2 socket, a U.2 backplane connector or a CEM slot. Each mating contact adds loss and reflection.
  4. Optional retimer: on a riser or backplane, re-clocking the signal.
  5. Drive PCB: a short route into the SSD controller package and its SerDes receiver.

Specification view

The relevant normative material lives in the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 — Physical Layer Logical Block (encoding, ordered sets, LTSSM, lane and link formation) and PCI Express Base Specification — Electrical Sub-Block (transmitter and receiver requirements, reference clock, channel). Form-factor documents such as the PCI Express Card Electromechanical Specification, the PCI Express M.2 Specification and SNIA SFF-TA documents for EDSFF define connectors, sideband pins and power. The register view lives in PCI Express Base Specification — PCI Express Capability Structure (Link Capabilities, Link Control, Link Status and their "2" versions).

  • A lane is a set of differential signal pairs, one pair for transmission and one for reception.
  • All ports must support 2.5 GT/s; links train initially at 2.5 GT/s.
  • Receivers support polarity inversion; the downstream port drives link and lane numbering during Configuration.
  • Link Capabilities reports Maximum Link Speed (bits 3:0) and Maximum Link Width (bits 9:4); Link Status reports Current Link Speed (bits 3:0) and Negotiated Link Width (bits 9:4), plus Link Training (bit 11) and Data Link Layer Link Active (bit 13).
  • The speed fields are encoded as an index into the Supported Link Speeds Vector in Link Capabilities 2, not as a direct GT/s code.

Because the link forms on contiguous lanes starting at lane 0 (or the reversed equivalent), a fault on lane 0 is more damaging than a fault on lane 3: it can collapse the link to x1 on the far lane or prevent it from forming. A fault on the highest lane of a x4 link typically yields x2.

Implementation-dependent: SerDes equaliser design (CTLE/DFE taps), lane-reversal support, whether a port retries a failed higher rate, how many times it retries before settling, firmware policies for target speed, whether autonomous width changes are used, clocking architecture on a given board.

Version-dependent: available rates (each Base Specification revision adds one), encoding (8b/10b, 128b/130b, Flit Mode with PAM4), channel and jitter budgets, defined widths (x12 and x32 history), retimer requirements and the Lane Margining at the Receiver capability (introduced with Revision 4.0).

  • The Base Specification does not say how a particular CPU's lanes map to slots, or how firmware sets bifurcation.
  • It does not define M.2/U.2/EDSFF pinouts; those are in form-factor specifications.
  • Confirm in the specification: the exact receiver detection procedure and termination ranges; exact lane-to-lane skew limits; exact width down-configuration requirements for your revision.

Never assume: that the slot's label matches its electrical width; that a link at capability speed has margin to spare; that LnkSta stays constant over time; that the Root Port and the device report the same thing at every instant (one may be mid-retrain).

Protocol view

At the physical level, three kinds of things are transmitted:

  • Packets from upper layers (TLPs and DLLPs) with physical framing added, striped across lanes.
  • Ordered sets, sent on all lanes simultaneously (not striped): TS1 and TS2 for training, SKP for clock compensation, Electrical Idle Ordered Set (EIOS) before going electrically idle, Electrical Idle Exit Ordered Set (EIEOS) for exit and block alignment at 8 GT/s and higher, Fast Training Sequence (FTS) for leaving L0s, and Start of Data Stream (SDS) to begin data at 128b/130b rates.
  • Electrical Idle: no differential signal; used in low-power states and during speed changes.

Because ordered sets are per-lane and identical in timing across lanes, they carry per-lane information (each lane's TS1 contains that lane's lane number) and they let receivers measure skew. That is why width negotiation is done with ordered sets and not with packets: packets need a formed link first.

Reasoning

  1. Receiver detection succeeded on lane 2, so the termination and AC coupling path exist. The failure is in signal quality or in one direction of the lane, not a missing connection.
  2. The first abnormal event is the absence of lock on lane 2 in Polling. Everything after (x2) is a consequence.
  3. Lane 3 is healthy but unused: the link must be contiguous. Do not report "lanes 2 and 3 broken".
  4. Next steps: inspect the connector and the lane 2 pair in both directions; try another slot; check Lane Error Status if the port implements it; use lane margining where supported.

Interpreting the output (illustrative shapes; your text will differ by pciutils version):

  • LnkCap: Port #0, Speed 16GT/s, Width x4, ASPM L1, … is what this port can do.
  • LnkSta: Speed 16GT/s, Width x4 is what the link negotiated. Newer pciutils append (downgraded) when the value is below capability, or (ok).
  • A kernel message of the form "… Gb/s available PCIe bandwidth, limited by 8.0 GT/s PCIe x4 link at 0000:00:01.1 (capable of … Gb/s with 16.0 GT/s PCIe x4 link)" is printed when the kernel detects that a device's available bandwidth is limited by a link somewhere on its path. It names the limiting port.

The setpci commands above only read (no =value). Never add = to experiment on a production system: writing Link Control can retrain or disable the link under a mounted filesystem.

What it cannot prove: Linux sees the result of training, not the process. A link reporting x4 now may have trained at x2 last boot; a link at full speed may be retraining hundreds of times per hour (look at AER correctable counters and Link Bandwidth Management Status, Chapter 9).

Code / pseudocode

Decoding the raw link registers yourself makes the meaning of each field concrete. This script is standard-library Python and runs anywhere; give it values you read with setpci or use the built-in examples.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Decode PCIe Link Capabilities (32-bit) and Link Status (16-bit) words."""
import sys

# Typical mapping when the Supported Link Speeds Vector is fully populated from 2.5 GT/s up.
# Strictly, the value is an index into Link Capabilities 2's vector (see text).
SPEED = {1: "2.5 GT/s", 2: "5.0 GT/s", 3: "8.0 GT/s", 4: "16.0 GT/s",
         5: "32.0 GT/s", 6: "64.0 GT/s", 7: "128.0 GT/s"}

def lnkcap(v):
    return {
        "max_speed": SPEED.get(v & 0xF, f"index {v & 0xF}"),
        "max_width": f"x{(v >> 4) & 0x3F}",
        "aspm_support": (v >> 10) & 0x3,
        "port_number": (v >> 24) & 0xFF,
    }

def lnksta(v):
    return {
        "speed": SPEED.get(v & 0xF, f"index {v & 0xF}"),
        "width": f"x{(v >> 4) & 0x3F}",
        "link_training": bool(v & (1 << 11)),
        "slot_clock_config": bool(v & (1 << 12)),
        "dll_link_active": bool(v & (1 << 13)),
        "bw_mgmt_status": bool(v & (1 << 14)),
        "autonomous_bw_status": bool(v & (1 << 15)),
    }

def verdict(cap, sta):
    c, s = lnkcap(cap), lnksta(sta)
    notes = []
    if s["speed"] != c["max_speed"]:
        notes.append(f"speed below this port's capability ({s['speed']} < {c['max_speed']})")
    if s["width"] != c["max_width"]:
        notes.append(f"width below this port's capability ({s['width']} < {c['max_width']})")
    if s["link_training"]:
        notes.append("link training in progress at the moment of reading")
    return c, s, notes or ["negotiated == this port's capability (the partner may still be the limit)"]

if __name__ == "__main__":
    examples = [(0x0047_4C44, 0x3044), (0x0047_4C44, 0x3023)]   # synthetic examples
    if len(sys.argv) == 3:
        examples = [(int(sys.argv[1], 16), int(sys.argv[2], 16))]
    for cap, sta in examples:
        c, s, notes = verdict(cap, sta)
        print(f"LnkCap=0x{cap:08x} LnkSta=0x{sta:04x}")
        print("  cap:", c)
        print("  sta:", s)
        for n in notes:
            print("  ->", n)

For the first synthetic example, Link Status 0x3044 decodes to speed index 4 (16.0 GT/s), width x4, Slot Clock Configuration set and Data Link Layer Link Active set. The second, 0x3023, is index 3 (8.0 GT/s) at x2: a doubly degraded link. Note that Data Link Layer Link Active reporting is required only for ports that advertise the capability for it (commonly downstream ports supporting hot-plug); on an Endpoint the bit may read 0 even when the link is up. Implementation-dependent

A second small simulation shows byte striping and why deskew matters:

def stripe(data: bytes, lanes: int):
    """Deal bytes round-robin onto lanes, as the Physical Layer does for packet data."""
    return [data[i::lanes] for i in range(lanes)]

def unstripe(per_lane, skew):
    """Re-assemble after each lane arrives 'skew[i]' symbol times late (deskew first)."""
    aligned = [lane for lane in per_lane]          # a real receiver delays early lanes
    out = bytearray()
    for col in range(max(len(l) for l in aligned)):
        for lane in aligned:
            if col < len(lane):
                out.append(lane[col])
    return bytes(out)

pkt = bytes(range(16))
lanes = stripe(pkt, 4)
print(lanes)          # lane 0: 00 04 08 0c, lane 1: 01 05 09 0d, ...
assert unstripe(lanes, [0, 1, 0, 2]) == pkt

Worked example

Scenario. An engineer moves the lab SSD (Gen4 x4 capable) from its M.2 socket to a PCIe adapter card in a x16 mechanical slot. After reboot, the drive benchmarks at about half its previous sequential read speed.

  1. Read capability and status on both ends. SSD: LnkCap 16GT/s x4; LnkSta 16GT/s x2. Root Port of the new slot: LnkCap 16GT/s x4 (not x16!); LnkSta 16GT/s x2.
  2. Interpret. The slot is x16 mechanically but its Root Port is x4-capable: the slot is electrically x4. That alone would not halve performance. The negotiated width is x2, below both capabilities, so lanes 2–3 failed somewhere in this path.
  3. Hypotheses. (a) The adapter card wires only two lanes (some cheap adapters do); (b) a damaged slot or adapter contact on lane 2 or 3; (c) the slot shares lanes with another slot or M.2 socket and firmware has given lanes 2–3 elsewhere.
  4. Discriminate. Check the motherboard manual for lane sharing (c). Try the adapter in another slot (b vs a). Inspect the adapter (a).
  5. Quantify. Gen4 x2 gives about 3.9 GB/s of encoded bandwidth per direction versus 7.9 GB/s at x4 (Chapter 6), which matches "about half".

Outcome in this synthetic case: the manual says the slot's lanes 2–3 are shared with a second M.2 socket and are given to that socket by default. A firmware setting changes it. The SSD is blameless.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a lane is a single wire, or that the two directions take turns.
  • Believing there is a clock line per lane. Timing is recovered from data; REFCLK is a 100 MHz reference, not a bit clock.
  • Reading only the device's LnkSta. Read both ends; the partner's capability may be the limit.
  • Reading LnkCap as "what I have". It is "what this port could have".
  • Assuming "x3" or other non-standard widths are possible.
  • Ignoring sideband signals: PERST# and REFCLK problems look like "no link".
  • Assuming that because a link trained, it is healthy. Margin and error rate are separate questions.

Senior engineer insight

A senior engineer reads LnkCap and LnkSta on both ends as the very first step of any NVMe performance or stability case, before looking at a single NVMe command. It takes ten seconds and eliminates or confirms a whole class of explanations.

They also know that a degraded link is stable but wrong, whereas a marginal link is right but unstable. The first shows up in LnkSta; the second shows up in error counters and Recovery events, and often only under load or temperature. Both are physical problems; they need different evidence.

Finally, they treat the physical path as a chain of components owned by different teams (CPU vendor, board vendor, connector vendor, retimer vendor, SSD vendor). A good report says which segment is suspected and why, so it lands on the right desk.

Lab: Is the SSD running at the width and speed it should?

Objective

Determine whether each NVMe SSD's link negotiated its full capability, and if not, which end (or the path) is the limit.

Skills

Reading LnkCap/LnkSta on both ends; decoding raw link registers; distinguishing capability limit from degradation.

Safety

Safe lab Read-only commands. Do not use setpci with =.

Hardware required

A Linux machine with an NVMe SSD. Optional: a second slot or adapter to compare.

Software required

pciutils; Python 3 with link_regs.py from this chapter.

Input

The BDF of each NVMe SSD (lspci -d ::0108 lists class 0108 devices) and of its parent port.

Expected output

A table: device, device LnkCap, port LnkCap, negotiated, verdict (full / limited by device / limited by port / degraded below both).

Background

The negotiated speed is at most the lower of the two ports' maximum speeds; the negotiated width at most the lower of the two maximum widths and the wired lanes. Anything below that is degradation.

Step 1 — Identify the pair of ports

Find the SSD's BDF and its parent with readlink -f /sys/bus/pci/devices/<BDF>; the parent is the path component before the SSD.

Step 2 — Read both ends

Run the lspci -vvv grep from the Linux view on both BDFs. Also read the raw words with setpci … CAP_EXP+0x0c.l and CAP_EXP+0x12.w.

Step 3 — Decode and judge

Run python3 link_regs.py <cap> <sta> for each end. Compute expected = min(speed caps), min(width caps). Compare with the negotiated values.

Observation

Record whether lspci's text and your decode agree. Record whether the kernel log contains a "limited by" bandwidth message.

Questions

  1. Which end limits the link, if any?
  2. If the link is degraded, is it in width, speed, or both?
  3. Does the "limited by" kernel message name the same port you identified?

Challenge

Find a link in your system (not necessarily NVMe) whose negotiated speed is lower than both capabilities. Is it degraded, or is it in a power-saving mode that lowered its speed autonomously? (Hint: Link Autonomous Bandwidth Status; some devices lower their own speed when idle.)

Hint

Some GPUs and NICs drop to 2.5 GT/s when idle and go back up under load. Re-read LnkSta while the device is busy.

Solution

For the lab system: SSD LnkCap 16GT/s x4; Root Port LnkCap 16GT/s x4; LnkSta 16GT/s x4 on both; verdict "full". If you see SSD LnkCap 32GT/s x4 with Root Port 16GT/s x4 and LnkSta 16GT/s x4, the verdict is "limited by port", which is normal compatibility. If LnkSta shows x2 while both caps are x4, the verdict is "degraded below both": a physical path problem in lanes 2–3 or a platform lane-sharing setting, to be investigated as in the worked example.

What a senior engineer notices

  • Whether LnkSta2 reports equalisation phases completed at 8 GT/s and above (a link at 16 GT/s that never completed equalisation is suspicious).
  • Whether the Root Port's Link Status shows Link Bandwidth Management Status set, which hints that the link changed speed or width since it was last cleared.
  • Whether AER correctable error counters are rising on a link that "looks fine".

Failure variants

  • Device LnkSta reads all ones (0xFFFF): the device is not responding; the link is down.
  • LnkSta shows "Speed unknown": a speed index the installed pciutils does not know; update pciutils or decode manually.
  • In a virtual machine: emulated values; the lab must be done on the host.

Extension

Script the lab: for every PCIe device, print capability, negotiated state, partner capability and verdict, and flag degraded links.

Exercises

  1. Draw a x8 link with lane reversal and label which physical lane of each port carries logical lane 0.
  2. A x4 SSD has a broken lane 0 receive pair at the host side. Using only the contiguity rule, which widths could the link possibly form, with and without lane reversal support?
  3. How many signal wires (excluding clock and sideband) does a x16 link use?
  4. Compute the UI at 2.5, 8, 16 and 32 GT/s and the PAM4 symbol duration at 64 GT/s.
  5. Explain why REFCLK is not the same as the bit clock.
Answers

1: Physical lane 0 of the Root Port connects to physical lane 7 of the device; the device maps its physical lane 7 to logical lane 0 internally (or vice versa, depending on which side reverses). 2: Without reversal, logical lane 0 is unusable, and every valid width includes lane 0, so the link cannot form. With reversal, physical lane 3 can become logical lane 0, so the link could form from the far end: physical lanes 3–2 as x2, or physical lane 3 alone as x1. Derived Confirm the exact rules for your revision in the Configuration substates. 3: 64. 4: 400 ps, 125 ps, 62.5 ps, 31.25 ps; at 64 GT/s PAM4, 32 GBaud → 31.25 ps per symbol. 5: REFCLK is a 100 MHz reference that PLLs in each port multiply up to generate the transmit rate; the receive timing is recovered from the data by CDR.

Quiz

  1. How many differential pairs does one lane contain?

    • One
    • Two
    • Four
    • Sixteen
    Answer and reasoning

    Two: one per direction.

  2. What is the main benefit of differential signalling?

    • It doubles the bit rate
    • It removes the need for encoding
    • It rejects common-mode noise and reduces emissions
    • It carries the clock on the second wire
    Answer and reasoning

    The receiver subtracts the legs, so noise coupled equally into both cancels; opposite currents also reduce radiation.

  3. At what rate does every PCIe link begin training?

    • 2.5 GT/s
    • The highest rate of the slower port
    • 8.0 GT/s
    • Whatever firmware set last boot
    Answer and reasoning

    2.5 GT/s with 8b/10b, guaranteeing interoperability. Faster rates are reached through Recovery.

  4. Which state detects whether a receiver is present on each lane?

    • L0
    • Configuration
    • Recovery
    • Detect
    Answer and reasoning

    Detect, using the transmitter's receiver-detection circuit.

  5. A x4 link loses lane 3. Which width results?

    • x3
    • x2
    • x1 only
    • No link
    Answer and reasoning

    x2 on lanes 0–1: widths must be valid powers and contiguous from lane 0.

  6. Which port proposes link and lane numbers?

    • The downstream port
    • The upstream port
    • The retimer
    • Software
    Answer and reasoning

    The downstream port (Root Port or Switch downstream port) leads Configuration; the upstream port echoes.

  7. Why does an AC coupling capacitor matter for receiver detection?

    Model answer

    The transmitter detects a receiver by changing its common-mode voltage and observing the charging time through the coupling capacitor into the far receiver's termination. With a termination present, the time constant is noticeably different than with an open line. A missing capacitor or termination changes the result.

  8. LnkSta says 16GT/s x4 and LnkCap says 32GT/s x4 on the SSD; the Root Port's LnkCap says 16GT/s x4. Is anything wrong?

    Model answer

    No. The link negotiated the highest rate both support (16 GT/s, limited by the Root Port) at full width. That is compatibility, not degradation.

  9. Name two things polarity inversion and lane reversal have in common, and one difference.

    Model answer

    Both fix board routing choices automatically during training and are invisible to software afterwards. Polarity inversion is per lane (swapped D+/D−) and handled in Polling by every receiver; lane reversal is across lanes (order swapped) and handled in Configuration, with support being implementation-dependent.

Interview questions

  1. (Beginner) What is "dual-simplex"?

    Model answer

    Each lane has independent one-way paths in both directions, both active simultaneously, as opposed to half-duplex (taking turns) on shared wires.

  2. (Beginner) Why is there no clock wire per lane?

    Model answer

    Clock and data recovery extracts timing from data transitions; encoding/scrambling guarantees enough transitions. This removes clock-to-data skew, the main limit of parallel buses.

  3. (Intermediate) Walk through how a x4 device in a x16 slot ends up as a x4 link.

    Model answer

    In Detect, the x16 Root Port finds receivers only on lanes 0–3. Polling runs on those. In Configuration, the Root Port proposes link 0; the device echoes on its four lanes; lane numbers 0–3 are assigned; TS2 confirms; L0 at x4.

  4. (Intermediate) What is the difference between a retimer and a redriver?

    Model answer

    A retimer recovers and retransmits data with its own clock, participates in training and equalisation, and resets the jitter budget; a redriver is an analog amplifier/equaliser, protocol-unaware, that boosts the signal including its noise and jitter.

  5. (Senior) A fleet of servers shows a few percent of SSDs at x2 after cold boots but x4 after warm reboots. What do you suspect and how do you prove it?

    Model answer

    A timing-sensitive physical issue: PERST# or REFCLK stability at cold power-on, temperature-dependent connector or channel margin, or a retimer that powers up slowly. Prove it with: logs of LnkSta per boot type; a scope on PERST#/REFCLK/power rails at cold boot; analyzer capture of training on the affected link to find which lanes fail in Polling; lane margining data cold versus warm; swapping drives versus slots to see whether it follows position.

  6. (Senior) Why might SSC cause errors on a link that trains successfully?

    Model answer

    If the two ends use separate reference clocks with independent SSC but the configuration assumes common clock (or SRNS), the instantaneous frequency difference can exceed what the elastic buffers absorb with the SKP rate in use. Training succeeds (short), but during sustained traffic the buffer over/underflows, causing Receiver Errors and Recovery. SRIS mode, with its higher SKP rate, exists for this case.

Teach-back challenge

  • Child: Explain why two strings per direction work better than one in a noisy playground.
  • Junior engineer: Explain how the lab system link became Gen4 x4, starting from power-on.
  • Senior engineer: Argue why "LnkSta shows full width and speed" is necessary but not sufficient evidence of a healthy link.
Model explanation (senior engineer)

LnkSta is a snapshot of the negotiated outcome. It does not include error rate, margin, number of Recovery entries, or whether equalisation converged well. A marginal link can sit at Gen4 x4 while replaying TLPs and generating correctable Receiver Errors, adding latency and jitter to NVMe I/O, and may flap under thermal stress. Sufficient evidence needs stable LnkSta over time, quiet AER correctable counters, completed equalisation, and ideally margining results with headroom.

Chapter summary

  • A lane is two differential pairs (TX and RX), dual-simplex, self-clocked through CDR; REFCLK is a 100 MHz reference.
  • Transmitters encode, serialise and pre-equalise; receivers terminate, equalise, recover the clock and deserialise.
  • A link stripes bytes across lanes; receivers deskew lanes using ordered sets.
  • Width is negotiated in Detect, Polling and Configuration; links form only on valid, contiguous widths; lane reversal and polarity inversion hide some board routing choices.
  • Speed starts at 2.5 GT/s and rises through Recovery; 8 GT/s and above need equalisation.
  • Compatibility is guaranteed, performance is not: always read LnkCap and LnkSta on both ends.

Do not move on until you can:

  • draw a four-lane link with TX/RX pairs and AC coupling capacitors;

  • describe the three steps that settle a link's width;
  • explain why speed always starts at 2.5 GT/s;
  • decode Link Status speed and width from a raw 16-bit value;
  • classify a link as full, limited by one end, or degraded.
