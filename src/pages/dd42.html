This module goes beyond the three power hierarchies of Chapter 50 — Power Management to the engineering questions behind them: how to budget the latency of a wake-up across every layer that slept, how to choose and verify an Autonomous Power State Transition policy, how suspend and resume interact with reset, how to tell power-state throttling from thermal throttling, and how to measure what a drive really does rather than what its descriptors claim.

Orientation

Chapter 50 taught the vocabulary: PCIe link states (L0, L0s, L1, L1.1, L1.2, L2/L3), device states (D0, D3hot, D3cold), NVMe power states (PS0–PS31) with their Power State Descriptors, Set Features Power Management (Feature Identifier 02h) and Autonomous Power State Transition (APST, Feature Identifier 0Ch). This module treats power management as a system with a budget: every layer that sleeps adds wake-up latency to the first command after idle, and every policy is a trade between that latency and energy. The deliverables are numbers you can defend: a wake budget, a break-even time, a measured latency-versus-idle curve, and a root cause for "slow only when quiet".

Re-read Chapter 50 (power hierarchies, PSD fields, APST table layout, ASPM and L1 PM Substates, Latency Tolerance Reporting) and the shutdown and RTD3 material of Chapter 48. Resume from D3cold is a reset: Module 41 — Reset applies.

Kid story

A fire station at night. When no alarm has rung for a while, the firefighters sit in chairs next to the truck. After longer, they lie down in the bunk room upstairs. After much longer, some go home and wait by the phone. When the alarm rings, chair-sitters are on the truck in seconds, bunk-room sleepers need to slide down the pole, and the ones at home need many minutes.

The town's rule says: "on festival nights, nobody goes home". And the street outside has lights that dim when no cars pass: the fire truck has to wait a moment for them to brighten before driving out. The time from alarm to truck rolling is the sum of every delay — the street lights, the sleepers, the people at home.

Analogy boundary

What it represents. Chairs, bunks and home are NVMe operational and non-operational power states with increasing exit latency; the idle time before moving is the APST Idle Time Prior to Transition (ITPT); "nobody goes home on festival nights" is a host latency tolerance that excludes deep states; the dimmed street lights are the PCIe link in L1 or L1.2; the alarm-to-truck time is the first-I/O latency after idle.

What it hides. Firefighters decide together; in a computer the link, the controller and the host CPU each decide independently and their delays add. It hides that waking costs energy: going home and coming back for a two-minute nap burns more fuel than staying in the chair (the break-even time). It also hides the host CPU: the interrupt that reports completion must wake a processor from its own deep idle state.

Where it misleads. A fire station's alarms are rare. A storage device often sees I/O at a period just longer than its idle timer — a database committing every 150 ms against a 100 ms timer — so it "goes upstairs" before every single call. The worst policy is not the deepest sleep; it is the sleep that ends just before every request.

Mechanism

One idle-then-wake cycle on the Lab SSD with APST enabled (PS2 → PS3 after 100 ms, PS3 → PS4 after a further 2,000 ms) and ASPM L1 with L1.2 enabled. Synthetic example for teaching.

  1. Last completion. Who: controller. What: the last CQE and MSI-X are written. Where: CQ1 memory, LAPIC. Evidence: MWr64 to CQ1, MWr32 to 0xFEE0_1000.
  2. Link goes idle. Who: device Upstream Port after its implementation-specific idle time. What: PM_Active_State_Request_L1 DLLPs, Root Port answers PM_Request_Ack; link enters L1; if LTR allows, L1.2 with CLKREQ# deasserted. Where: both PHYs. Evidence: DLLPs then EIOS and electrical idle on an analyzer.
  3. APST timer expires (100 ms). Who: controller firmware. What: PS2 → PS3; DRAM to self-refresh, clocks gated. Where: inside the drive. Evidence: none on the link; a power-rail drop on a power interposer; Get Features 02h would report PS3.
  4. Second timer (2,000 ms more). Who: firmware. What: PS3 → PS4. Evidence: further rail drop only.
  5. Host submits I/O. Who: CPU (woken from its own C-state by the application). What: SQE written, doorbell MWr32 to 0xFE60_1008. Where: host memory, Root Port. Evidence: the doorbell is held at the Root Port while the link is not in L0.
  6. Link exit. Who: Root Port. What: CLKREQ# asserted, REFCLK restarts, T_POWER_ON and common-mode restore, Recovery to L0. Evidence: ~60–70 µs gap before the doorbell TLP appears on the link.
  7. NVMe exit. Who: controller. What: PS4 → last operational state; the doorbell has been latched. Evidence: doorbell-to-SQE-fetch gap up to EXLAT (12 ms); the link may re-enter L1 during the gap.
  8. Command executes. Normal SQE fetch, NAND read, data, CQE, MSI-X. Idle timer restarts at the next idle period.

The wake budget

Twake = TCPU + Tlink + TNVMe + Trelink
Twake
extra latency of the first command after idle compared with a fully awake system
TCPU
host CPU C-state exit for the submitting core (and again for the core handling the interrupt)
Tlink
link exit from L1/L1.2 for the doorbell write
TNVMe
controller exit from its non-operational state, at most EXLAT
Trelink
a second link exit if the link re-entered L1 while the controller was waking (device-initiated this time)

Lab SSD worst case from PS4 and L1.2: about 0.15 + 0.07 + 12 + 0.07 ≈ 12.3 ms. From PS3: about 2.3 ms. With APST disabled but L1.2 enabled: about 0.3 ms. An application with a 5 ms p99 target can tolerate PS3 but not PS4 — so its latency tolerance must sit between 3,000 µs (PS3 ENLAT+EXLAT) and 17,000 µs (PS4).

Break-even, with numbers

tBE = Etrans ÷ (Pshallow − Pdeep), Etrans ≈ Pshallow × (ENLAT + EXLAT)
tBE
minimum residency in the deep state for a net energy saving
Etrans
energy spent entering and leaving, approximated as shallow-state idle power over the transition times
Pshallow, Pdeep
idle power of the state the drive would otherwise stay in, and of the deep state
ENLAT, EXLAT
declared entry and exit latencies of the deep state (maxima)

Assume the Lab SSD idles at 1.20 W in PS2 (a synthetic Idle Power value). PS3: Etrans ≈ 1.20 W × 3 ms = 3.6 mJ; tBE ≈ 3.6 mJ ÷ 1.15 W ≈ 3.1 ms. PS4 directly from PS2: 1.20 W × 17 ms = 20.4 mJ ÷ 1.195 W ≈ 17 ms. PS4 from PS3: 20.4 mJ ÷ 0.045 W ≈ 450 ms. Crude, but the shape is right: from PS3, going deeper pays off only for long idle periods, so sensible tables wait seconds before the deepest state Derived.

The ladder for APST policy

Firefighters move from chairs to bunks to home after longer quiet periods; on festival nights nobody goes home.

The host gives the controller a table: after this much idle time go to this lower-power state; the host excludes states whose wake-up is too slow for its workload.

Firmware gates clocks, puts DRAM in self-refresh or off, powers down NAND interfaces; waking reverses it and costs EXLAT. The link separately enters L1.2 when traffic stops.

NVMe Base 2.4: APST feature (0Ch) with ITPS/ITPT entries, APSTA in Identify Controller, PSD fields NOPS/ENLAT/EXLAT, Power Management feature (02h); 2.4 adds host idle I/O exit latency limits (fields not verified here).

One Set Features with a 256-byte table configures it; the transitions themselves produce no NVMe traffic. The link shows ASPM DLLPs and electrical idle.

An analyzer shows a delayed doorbell TLP (link exit) and a long doorbell-to-SQE-fetch gap (NVMe exit) after quiet periods only.

Latency that worsens as load falls is a power-management signature; prove it by excluding states (lower the tolerance) and watching the tail vanish.

Suspend: keep power, or cut it?

On system suspend a host has two strategies. Host-managed low power: keep the device in D0, set the deepest acceptable NVMe power state (or let APST do it) and let ASPM take the link to L1.2; resume costs only EXLAT, but idle power is whatever PS4 and L1.2 really draw. D3cold: normal shutdown (CC.SHN, wait CSTS.SHST = complete within RTD3E), D3hot, platform removes power; idle power is near zero but resume is a cold reset plus full controller initialization (bounded by RTD3R and CAP.TO). Linux chooses between them from platform hints and per-device quirks Version-dependent. The dangerous case is a mismatch: the OS believes it kept the device powered while the platform removed power anyway — Trace 3 below.

Specification discipline

  • NVM Express Base Specification, Revision 2.4 — Power Management; Identify Controller (Power State Descriptors, APSTA, RTD3E/RTD3R); Features: Power Management, Autonomous Power State Transition, Host Controlled Thermal Management, Non-Operational Power State Config
  • PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 — Power Management; ASPM; L1 PM Substates; Latency Tolerance Reporting; PCI Power Management Capability (PCI-SIG members' document)
  • NVMe over PCIe Transport Specification, Revision 1.4 for PCIe-specific power interactions; form-factor specifications for CLKREQ# and auxiliary power.

Normative Up to 32 power states described in Identify Controller (from byte 2048, 32 bytes each), NPSS 0's based; Power Management is Feature 02h (PS in CDW11 bits 4:0, Workload Hint bits 7:5); APST is Feature 0Ch (APSTE in CDW11 bit 0, 256-byte table of 32 64-bit entries, ITPS bits 7:3, ITPT bits 31:8 in ms); I/O submitted in a non-operational state causes a return to an operational state; ASPM link states and D-states as defined by PCIe.

Informative Example policies and notes about choosing idle times. Optional APST (APSTA), Host Controlled Thermal Management, Non-Operational Power State Config, L1 PM Substates, LTR, D1/D2, the 2.4 idle I/O exit latency limits.

Actual power and latency (PSD values are maxima and may be under-reported), what each state powers down, when the device requests L1, LTR values, how firmware treats admin commands in non-operational states, how throttling is enforced. Platform-dependent: CLKREQ# routing, whether firmware grants the OS ASPM control, suspend strategy hints.

APST arrived in the NVMe 1.1/1.2 era; Host Controlled Thermal Management and Non-Operational Power State Config later; NVMe 2.4 adds host-specified idle I/O exit latency limits (see 2026 NVMe 2.4 Updates; feature identifier and fields not verified here). PCIe L1 PM Substates and LTR are 3.x-era; Flit Mode (6.0) uses L0p and drops L0s. Linux's APST policy moved to two tiers in the 5.x series.

Neither specification says which policy to use, what latency is acceptable, what the controller powers down, or how long a host should wait before entering a state. Check in the text: the exact definition of "idle" for APST, the rule for admin commands in non-operational states, the Feature Identifiers of Host Controlled Thermal Management and Non-Operational Power State Config before encoding them, and the L1.2 entry conditions.

Never assume APST survives reset or resume, that D3hot preserves controller state (No_Soft_Reset decides), that a drive's declared EXLAT is its real worst case, or that L1.2 works on every board that advertises it.

Labs

Beginner lab — from descriptors to a policy, on paper

Safe lab Objective: build an APST table and its latency and energy consequences by hand. Prerequisites: Chapter 50. Setup: the Lab SSD PSDs (PS0–PS2 operational; PS3 0.050 W, ENLAT 1,000 µs, EXLAT 2,000 µs; PS4 0.005 W, 5,000/12,000 µs), PS2 idle 1.20 W, two tolerances: 5,000 µs and 100,000 µs.

Procedure: for each tolerance choose eligible states; write all five 64-bit entries with Linux-style two-tier timeouts (100 ms first tier, 2,000 ms second); compute worst-case wake penalty and break-even times. Observations: which entries are zero and why. Questions: what is entry 2 for tolerance 5,000 µs? What is the worst-case penalty in each case? Why is a 100 ms first tier sensible given tBE ≈ 3.1 ms? Expected reasoning: filter by ENLAT+EXLAT ≤ tolerance, then point each shallower entry at the shallowest qualifying deeper state.

Solution

Tolerance 5,000 µs: only PS3 qualifies (3,000 µs). Entries 0–2 = (100 << 8) | (3 << 3) = 0x6418; entries 3 and 4 = 0. Worst penalty ≈ 2 ms (+ link). Tolerance 100,000 µs: PS3 and PS4 qualify. Entries 0–2 = 0x6418; entry 3 = (2000 << 8) | (4 << 3) = 0x7D020; entry 4 = 0. Worst penalty ≈ 12 ms. The 100 ms first tier is about 30 times PS3's break-even, so the drive almost never enters PS3 for a nap too short to pay back, while still reaching it quickly in genuinely idle periods.

Extension: redo it for a PS3 EXLAT of 20,000 µs.

Intermediate lab — measure the latency-versus-idle curve

Safe lab Read-only: the script opens the device read-only and never writes. Needs root. Objective: measure first-read latency as a function of idle gap and match the steps to APST and ASPM settings. Prerequisites: beginner lab. Setup: a Linux machine with an NVMe drive that is otherwise idle (no mounted file system activity on it if possible); save the script below.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""idle_gap_latency.py - first-read latency after an idle gap (read-only).

Usage: sudo python3 idle_gap_latency.py /dev/nvme0n1 [repeats]
Opens the device READ-ONLY with O_DIRECT, sleeps for each idle gap, then times one
4 KiB read at a random 4 KiB-aligned offset. Nothing is ever written.
"""
import mmap, os, random, statistics, sys, time

GAPS_S = [0.001, 0.01, 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 3.0]
BLOCK = 4096


def main():
    path = sys.argv[1]
    repeats = int(sys.argv[2]) if len(sys.argv) > 2 else 5
    flags = os.O_RDONLY | getattr(os, "O_DIRECT", 0)
    fd = os.open(path, flags)
    size = os.lseek(fd, 0, os.SEEK_END)
    buf = mmap.mmap(-1, BLOCK)                      # page-aligned buffer for O_DIRECT
    rnd = random.Random(1)
    print(f"{'idle gap':>10} {'median us':>10} {'max us':>10}")
    for gap in GAPS_S:
        samples = []
        for _ in range(repeats):
            off = rnd.randrange(0, size // BLOCK) * BLOCK
            time.sleep(gap)
            t0 = time.perf_counter_ns()
            os.preadv(fd, [buf], off)
            samples.append((time.perf_counter_ns() - t0) / 1000)
        print(f"{gap*1000:8.0f} ms {statistics.median(samples):10.0f} {max(samples):10.0f}")
    os.close(fd)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Procedure: record get-feature 0x0c and LnkCtl; run sudo python3 idle_gap_latency.py /dev/nvme0n1 7; then (Caution lab) set a low tolerance such as echo 1000 | sudo tee /sys/class/nvme/nvme0/power/pm_qos_latency_tolerance_us, rerun, and restore the original value. Observations: the gaps at which latency steps up and the size of each step. Questions: which step matches the first APST tier? Why can 1 ms gaps already be slower than back-to-back reads? Why does the curve differ if the file system is mounted and busy? Expected reasoning: step positions match ITPT values; step heights match EXLAT plus link and CPU exits.

Solution

On a drive with a 100 ms first tier, latency is flat until ~100 ms gaps and then rises by roughly the PS3 exit; another step appears beyond the second tier. Small increases even at 1–10 ms come from CPU C-states and ASPM L1/L1.2, which engage within microseconds to milliseconds. With the low tolerance the APST steps disappear (APST disabled or limited), leaving only the CPU/link component. Background I/O from a mounted file system resets the idle timers, flattening the curve — which is why the measurement needs an idle device. Exact values are device- and platform-dependent; treat the shape, not the numbers, as the result.

Advanced lab — compare policies for real workloads

Safe lab Simulation. Objective: quantify energy and tail latency of APST policies for different idle-gap patterns. Setup: save as apst_policy_sim.py.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""apst_policy_sim.py - energy and wake-latency cost of APST policies (teaching model).

Synthetic Lab SSD power states. The model is deliberately simple: the drive idles
in PS2 after each command, then follows the APST table; the next command pays the
exit latency of whatever state the drive is in. Numbers are illustrative only.
"""
import random

# name: (power_W, enlat_us, exlat_us); PS2 power here is its *idle* power
PS = {"PS2": (1.20, 0, 0), "PS3": (0.050, 1_000, 2_000), "PS4": (0.005, 5_000, 12_000)}

POLICIES = {
    "no APST":   [],                                  # stay in PS2
    # (target, cumulative idle ms at which the drive enters it); ITPT in each APST
    # entry counts idle time spent in the current state, so the times add up.
    "legacy":    [("PS3", 50 * 3_000 / 1000), ("PS4", 150 + 50 * 17_000 / 1000)],
    "two-tier":  [("PS3", 100), ("PS4", 100 + 2_000)],
    "PS3 only":  [("PS3", 100)],
}


def gaps(kind, n=20_000, seed=7):
    rnd = random.Random(seed)
    if kind == "log writer every 150 ms":
        return [150.0] * n
    if kind == "steady server, 2 ms gaps":
        return [2.0] * n
    if kind == "bursty laptop":
        out = []
        while len(out) < n:
            out += [0.2] * rnd.randint(5, 50)          # a burst
            out.append(rnd.lognormvariate(7.0, 1.5))   # think time, median ~1.1 s
        return out[:n]
    raise ValueError(kind)


def simulate(gap_list, table):
    energy_j, time_ms, penalties = 0.0, 0.0, []
    p_idle = PS["PS2"][0]
    for g in gap_list:
        state, t = "PS2", 0.0
        for target, start_ms in table:
            if g <= start_ms:
                break
            energy_j += PS[state][0] * (start_ms - t) / 1000
            t, state = start_ms, target
            # entering costs roughly idle power for ENLAT
            energy_j += p_idle * PS[state][1] / 1e6
        energy_j += PS[state][0] * (g - t) / 1000
        exlat_ms = PS[state][2] / 1000
        if state != "PS2":
            energy_j += p_idle * exlat_ms / 1000       # waking costs idle power too
        penalties.append(exlat_ms)
        time_ms += g
    penalties.sort()
    avg_mw = 1000 * energy_j / (time_ms / 1000)
    hit = sum(1 for p in penalties if p > 0) / len(penalties)
    p99 = penalties[int(0.99 * (len(penalties) - 1))]
    return avg_mw, hit, p99


if __name__ == "__main__":
    for kind in ("steady server, 2 ms gaps", "log writer every 150 ms", "bursty laptop"):
        g = gaps(kind)
        print(f"== {kind}")
        for name, table in POLICIES.items():
            mw, hit, p99 = simulate(g, table)
            print(f"   {name:<9} avg power {mw:8.1f} mW   "
                  f"I/Os paying a wake-up {100*hit:5.1f} %   p99 added latency {p99:5.1f} ms")

Procedure: run it; change the log writer's period to 99, 101 and 151 ms; add a policy with the first tier at 200 ms. Observations: average power, fraction of I/Os paying a wake-up, p99 penalty. Questions: why does "legacy" not penalize the 150 ms log writer while "two-tier" penalizes every write? What first-tier value would you pick for a laptop and why? Why does the steady server see no difference at all? What does the model omit? Expected reasoning: the penalty depends on gaps relative to the timer, not on load.

Solution

Output: steady server 1,200 mW for every policy (gaps of 2 ms never reach a timer). Log writer: two-tier and PS3-only drop to ~841 mW but every write pays ~2 ms; legacy enters PS3 only after 150 ms, so a period of exactly 150 ms never triggers it — at 151 ms it would penalize every write too. Bursty laptop: two-tier ~59 mW, legacy ~70 mW, PS3-only ~87 mW, no APST 1,200 mW; about 3.4% of I/Os pay a wake-up, with p99 12 ms when PS4 is allowed. For a laptop a 100–200 ms first tier with PS4 after seconds is defensible; for a latency-sensitive periodic writer, exclude PS3 or set the timer well above the period. The model omits active power, link and CPU wake costs, transition energy accuracy, and the drive's own background work.

Extension: feed it inter-arrival gaps extracted from a blktrace of your own workload.

Senior forensic lab — the regression that only appears at night

Safe lab Evidence analysis. Objective: root-cause a p99.9 latency regression that appeared after a kernel upgrade and worsens as load falls.

Setup — synthetic evidence:

  • Database replicas on identical servers and drives. After a kernel upgrade, p99.9 write latency at 03:00–05:00 rises from 0.4 ms to 2.6 ms; at daytime peak it is unchanged.
  • get-feature 0x0c before: all entries 0 (APST effectively off, because the old kernel's single-tier policy excluded both states for this drive's tolerance). After: entries 0–2 = 0x6418, entry 3 = 0x7D020.
  • Histogram of write latency by preceding idle gap: gaps < 100 ms → 0.35 ms median; gaps 100–2,100 ms → 2.4 ms median.
  • Commit log writes during the night arrive every 120–400 ms.
  • SMART: no thermal transitions; no errors.

Procedure: state hypotheses; identify the first abnormal event; propose and justify a fix; design a regression test. Questions: why only at night? Why not a drive or firmware problem? What fix scope is right — kernel parameter, per-device tolerance, or application? Expected reasoning: the latency step coincides exactly with the first APST tier and PS3's EXLAT.

Solution

The first abnormal event is configuration, not traffic: the new kernel installed an APST table where none was active before. At night, gaps exceed 100 ms, so most commits find the drive in PS3 and pay its ~2 ms exit; at peak, gaps are short and the timer never expires. The drive behaves exactly as declared. Fix at the narrowest scope that matches intent: set the per-device latency tolerance on database servers below 3,000 µs (excludes PS3 and PS4), applied by a udev rule so it survives reboots and device resets; keep APST on other fleets. Regression test: synthetic writes at 150 ms intervals with p99 checked against 1 ms, run on every kernel upgrade. Confidence high: the histogram split at 100 ms is a fingerprint.

Extension: estimate the night-time power the new policy saves and present the trade-off rather than silently reverting.

Interview

Beginner

  1. What is a non-operational power state?

    Model answer

    A state (NOPS = 1) in which the controller processes no I/O; submitting I/O wakes it to an operational state, paying up to EXLAT.

  2. What does APST do?

    Model answer

    Lets the controller move itself to lower-power states after host-specified idle times, using a 32-entry table.

  3. Are ENLAT and EXLAT typical values?

    Model answer

    No — declared maxima; real values are usually lower, occasionally higher if under-reported.

  4. Which layer does ASPM manage?

    Model answer

    The PCIe link (L0s, L1 and its substates), independently of NVMe power states.

  5. Do power settings survive a controller reset?

    Model answer

    Not unless saved; the host must re-apply APST and power state after every controller-level reset.

Intermediate

  1. How do you compute the worst-case first-I/O penalty?

    Model answer

    Sum CPU C-state exit, link exit (L1/L1.2), the deepest allowed state's EXLAT, and a possible second link exit while the controller wakes.

  2. Build the APST entry for "PS3 after 100 ms".

    Model answer

    (100 << 8) | (3 << 3) = 0x6418.

  3. Why can SMART polling cost battery life?

    Model answer

    Each Get Log Page is a command that resets the idle timer and may wake the drive, preventing deep states.

  4. How does LTR relate to L1.2?

    Model answer

    The device reports tolerated latency; the port enters L1.2 only if it exceeds a software-programmed threshold.

  5. Host-managed suspend versus D3cold: trade-off?

    Model answer

    Host-managed: fast resume (EXLAT), higher sleep power. D3cold: near-zero power, but full re-initialization and RTD3R on resume.

Advanced

  1. What is break-even time and why does it matter for APST?

    Model answer

    The minimum residency for a net energy saving; idle timers well above it avoid naps that cost more than they save.

  2. Why can a 100 ms timer hurt a workload at 150 ms intervals more than one at 50 ms?

    Model answer

    At 150 ms every gap exceeds the timer, so every I/O pays the exit; at 50 ms the timer never expires.

  3. How do you tell link exit from NVMe exit in a trace?

    Model answer

    Link exit delays the doorbell TLP itself (and shows Recovery); NVMe exit appears as a long doorbell-to-SQE-fetch gap.

  4. A resume trace shows training from Detect. What does that tell you?

    Model answer

    Power or PERST# reached the device; the controller is in its reset state and needs full initialization.

  5. How do you separate a power cap from thermal throttling?

    Model answer

    Get Features 02h for the current state, SMART thermal transition counts and time, and the time profile (cap is flat from the start; thermal appears after heating).

Senior

  1. Set power policy for laptops, edge boxes and data centers.

    Model answer

    Laptops: APST with PS4 after seconds, L1.2, host-managed suspend where validated. Edge: moderate tolerance, thermal thresholds tuned to enclosure. Data center: deep states usually excluded for latency, operational state caps for slot power budgets, ASPM per platform validation.

  2. How would you validate a vendor's EXLAT claim?

    Model answer

    Force each state, measure doorbell-to-fetch with an analyzer across temperatures and fill levels thousands of times, compare the maximum with EXLAT, and check rail power for entry timing.

  3. A kernel upgrade changed APST behaviour fleet-wide. How do you manage that risk?

    Model answer

    Record APST tables and tolerances as part of the fleet's configuration baseline, diff them on upgrade, and run gap-based latency tests in canary before rollout.

  4. Where do 2.4's idle I/O exit latency limits fit?

    Model answer

    They let the host state a latency bound the controller must respect when choosing idle states, moving part of the policy into the device; confirm fields in Base 2.4 before relying on them.

  5. What evidence would you demand before blaming SSD firmware for "vanishes after idle"?

    Model answer

    Whether CSTS was all-ones or valid; whether disabling L1.2 alone or PS4 alone removes the failure; platform comparison with the same drive; analyzer evidence of the failing exit.

Quiz

  1. Which APST entry value means "go to PS4 after 2,000 ms"?

    • 0x0000_6418
    • 0x0007_D004
    • 0x0007_D020
    • 0x0000_07D4
    Answer and reasoning

    (2000 << 8) | (4 << 3) = 0x7D000 | 0x20.

  2. In Trace 2, which layer contributed most of the 11 ms?

    • PCIe L1.2 exit
    • NVMe PS4 exit
    • CPU C-state exit
    • NAND read
    Answer and reasoning

    Doorbell-to-fetch took 10.8 ms; the link exit was ~70 µs.

  3. A resume capture shows LTSSM Detect → Polling → … What happened?

    • The device lost power or saw PERST#
    • Normal L1.2 exit
    • APST exit
    • A Retrain Link
    Answer and reasoning

    L1.x exits go through Recovery, not Detect.

  4. Which change most directly removes a 2 ms penalty caused by PS3?

    • pcie_aspm=off
    • Bigger queue depth
    • Disabling MSI-X coalescing
    • Latency tolerance below 3,000 µs
    Answer and reasoning

    The tolerance excludes PS3 (ENLAT+EXLAT = 3,000 µs) from the APST table.

  5. Throughput is flat at 70% from the first second and SMART shows no thermal transitions. Most likely?

    • Thermal throttling
    • A host-selected power cap
    • APST
    • L1.2
    Answer and reasoning

    Thermal throttling develops as the drive heats and is counted; APST and ASPM do not act under sustained load.

  6. Compute break-even for PS3 with Pshallow = 1.20 W, Pdeep = 0.05 W, ENLAT + EXLAT = 3 ms.

    Model answer

    E ≈ 1.20 × 0.003 = 3.6 mJ; tBE ≈ 3.6 mJ ÷ 1.15 W ≈ 3.1 ms.

  7. Why does reading the current power state disturb what you measure?

    Model answer

    Get Features is an admin command; it ends idleness and may wake the controller.

  8. Why should a driver check CSTS on resume even when it chose host-managed suspend?

    Model answer

    The platform may have removed power anyway; the controller would then be disabled (RDY = 0) and doorbells would be ignored.

Teach-back

  1. To a child: why does the fire truck sometimes leave fast and sometimes slow?

    Model explanation

    "It depends how deeply the firefighters were sleeping and whether the street lights were dimmed. If they were in chairs, it's fast; if some went home, it's slow. On busy nights we tell them not to go home."

  2. To a junior engineer: how do you build and check an APST policy?

    Model explanation

    "Read the PSDs; keep non-operational states with ENLAT+EXLAT under the tolerance; set idle times far above each state's break-even; write the 256-byte table with Set Features 0Ch; re-apply after reset and resume; then measure latency against idle gap and power on the rails."

  3. To a senior engineer: how should a fleet own power policy?

    Model explanation

    "Treat the wake budget as a product requirement per workload class, derive tolerances from it, encode them as configuration that survives kernels and resets, measure declared versus real latencies in qualification, and watch latency-by-gap and Unsafe Shutdowns-per-suspend as fleet signals. Use power caps for slot budgets and thermal thresholds for enclosures, and keep the three separate in dashboards."
