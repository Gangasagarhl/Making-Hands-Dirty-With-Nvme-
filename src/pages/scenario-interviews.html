Thirteen long-form interview scenarios. In each one, the interviewer gives a one-line symptom and hands out evidence only when the candidate asks the right question. Each comes with a model dialogue, the hidden root cause, and a scoring guide that says what a 4, a 6 and an 8 sound like. The skill being tested is layered reasoning: physical link, Data Link Layer, Transaction Layer, configuration, NVM Express (NVMe) controller, driver, platform.

How scenario interviews work

A scenario interview imitates a real escalation. The candidate gets a short symptom, the kind that arrives in a chat message at 4 p.m. on a Friday, and has to drive the investigation by asking for evidence. The interviewer holds a stack of evidence cards and hands one over only when the candidate asks for something that would plausibly contain it. Candidates who ask for everything at once ("send me all the logs") get everything at once, and are scored on whether they can find the signal in it.

Learning objectives

  • Turn a one-line symptom into a layered set of hypotheses, ranked by likelihood and cost to test.
  • Ask for evidence that separates hypotheses, not evidence that merely describes the symptom again.
  • Find the first abnormal event in a mixed bundle of Linux logs, Advanced Error Reporting (AER) registers, NVM Express (NVMe) logs and analyzer traces.
  • State a root cause with its mechanism, the evidence for it, a confirming experiment and a confidence level.
  • Recognise when the evidence points at the host, the platform or the test setup rather than the solid-state drive (SSD).

Prerequisites

The investigation method from Debugging Methodology and Senior Engineer Thinking; error correlation from Chapter 47; the end-to-end flow from Chapter 39 and Chapter 41; reset and power from Chapter 48–Chapter 50. The short-form questions in Interview Academy are a good warm-up.

Running a scenario

Rules for the interviewer

  • Read the opening line exactly. Answer clarifying questions with the "Facts on request" list only.
  • Hand out an evidence card when the candidate asks for anything that would contain it. Do not hint.
  • If the candidate is stuck for more than five minutes, ask "What layer do you think this is in, and what would prove it?" and note that you prompted.
  • Score with the scenario's guide and the general rubric in Senior Rubric and Final Certification Exam.

Rules for the candidate

  • Think out loud. Silence scores zero, even if you are reasoning well.
  • Say what each piece of evidence rules in and rules out.
  • Separate the detector of an error from its cause.
  • End with a root cause, a confirming experiment, a fix, a regression test and a confidence level.

All traces are synthetic, written for teaching on the course's reference lab system: Lab SSD at 0000:01:00.0 (Requester ID 0x0100), root port 0000:00:01.1, PCI Express (PCIe) Gen4 x4 unless stated, Max Payload Size (MPS) 256 bytes, Max Read Request Size (MRRS) 512 bytes, BAR0 at 0xFE60_0000, Input/Output Memory Management Unit (IOMMU) enabled. Kernel messages are paraphrased; real wording differs between kernel versions. Values that a real investigation would read from a specification are named, not invented.

Imagine a detective game where the detective has to ask the right question to get each clue. Asking "What did the butler see?" gets you the butler's clue. Asking "Tell me everything" gets you a pile of a hundred notes, and the detective who can find the right one in that pile also wins, but more slowly.

In a detective game, there is always one culprit and the clues are fair. In real engineering incidents there may be two interacting causes, a clue may be wrong (an analyzer artefact, a log from the wrong machine), and the "culprit" may be a design decision nobody made on purpose. Good candidates say when evidence is weak.

Scenario 1 — Works at Gen4, unstable at Gen5

Opening line (interviewer reads): "We moved our NVMe SSD from a Gen4 platform to a new Gen5 platform. It works, but under heavy load it sometimes logs errors and once dropped off the bus. On the old platform it was perfect. What do you investigate?"

Facts on request

  • SSD is Gen5 x4 capable. New platform has a Gen5 root port; the SSD sits on a riser card.
  • Same SSD firmware on both platforms.
  • Errors appear after 10–40 minutes of sustained mixed I/O; more often in the afternoon (warmer lab).
  • Forcing the link to Gen4 on the new platform: nobody has tried.

Candidate

Candidate

Interviewer
(cards 1 and 2)
Candidate
The link trains fine, so this is not a training failure; it degrades over time. Correctable errors climb with temperature, mostly detected by the root port, so the upstream direction (SSD transmitter → riser → root port receiver) is weaker. The Surprise Down at the end is a consequence: errors grew until the link could not recover. The NVMe removal is the last consequence. I want lane-level detail.
Interviewer
(card 3)
Candidate
NAKs and replays with errors on lane 2. A lane-specific problem points to that lane's path: a connector pin, a riser trace, or a marginal transmitter lane. To localise: force Gen4 (expect clean), swap the SSD (does the problem follow the drive?), remove the riser (does it follow the riser?).
Interviewer
(card 4)
Candidate
It follows the riser, not the drive, and it gets worse with heat. Root cause: the riser's lane 2 does not have enough margin at 32 GT/s when warm. Fix: a Gen5-qualified riser or a retimer. Regression test: a thermal soak at Gen5 under load with AER counters monitored on both ends, pass threshold zero uncorrectable and a correctable rate below an agreed limit.
Hidden root cause

The riser card's lane 2 trace has too much insertion loss for 32 GT/s, and the loss increases with temperature. Equalization completes at power-on (cool), but margin shrinks as the riser warms, receiver errors rise, replays increase, and eventually Recovery fails and the link goes down. The SSD is not at fault.

Scenario 2 — The controller receives the command, but the host never sees the completion

Opening line: "Our team wrote a user-space NVMe driver for a test tool. The controller clearly receives the commands (we see the Submission Queue Entry (SQE) fetches on the analyzer), but our completion loop never sees a completion and times out. The SSD vendor says their drive is fine. Who is right?"

Facts on request

  • The tool creates two I/O queue pairs: SQ1/CQ1 and SQ2/CQ2. Admin commands work.
  • The tool polls CQ1 for commands submitted on SQ1. Interrupts are not used.
  • Commands on SQ2 work. Commands on SQ1 never complete.

Candidate
"Receives the command" means the SQE fetch happened. Possible outcomes: the controller never completes; it completes to an address the host is not watching; it completes correctly but the host misreads the phase or the CQ head. I want to see everything the device sent after the fetch.
Interviewer
(card 1)
Candidate
The data was written, and a 16-byte write went to 0x1_2342_0000. That size is a Completion Queue Entry (CQE). CQ1's base, from the lab's memory map, is 0x1_2341_0000, so this went somewhere else. Which CQ was SQ1 bound to when it was created?
Interviewer
(card 2)
Candidate
SQ1 was created with CQID=2. The controller did exactly what it was told: completions for SQ1 go to CQ2. The host polls CQ1 and never sees them. The CQE in CQ2 will confirm SQID=1.
Interviewer
(card 3)
Candidate
Confirmed: SQID 1, CID 7, in CQ2. Root cause is the tool's Create I/O SQ command. The vendor is right. Fix the CQID field; add a self-check that reads back the queue mapping in the tool; add a regression test that submits on every SQ and checks the CQE's SQ Identifier. A controller is allowed to accept this mapping because several SQs may legally share one CQ. Confidence: high.
Hidden root cause

The test tool's queue-creation code used the wrong loop variable for the Completion Queue Identifier when creating SQ1, binding it to CQ2. Sharing a CQ between SQs is legal, so the controller accepted it. The tool's own "unexpected completion" warning was there all along.

Candidate
Completion Timeout is detected by the requester. If the root port logged it, the requester was the CPU (via the root port), and the request was a non-posted read or configuration access to the SSD. I want to know which request and when.
Interviewer

Candidate
It coincides with "activate immediately" firmware commits. An Unexpected Completion from the SSD afterwards suggests the SSD answered late: after the root port had given up, so the late completion matched nothing. Hypothesis: during activation the SSD stops answering Base Address Register (BAR) reads for longer than the root port's completion timeout. The NVMe specification has a mechanism for this window: the controller reports Processing Paused in CSTS and advertises a maximum activation time in Identify Controller. A controller still has to complete PCIe reads in time, though. I want an analyzer capture of the window.
Interviewer

Root cause: firmware's immediate-activation path stalls the BAR responder for ~120 ms, longer than the platform's completion timeout. Workarounds: use activation at next reset; or make the updater stop polling during activation. Real fix: firmware must keep answering register reads (even with PP set) during activation. Regression test: activate-immediately under register polling, with root port AER monitored, on platforms with the shortest supported completion timeout. Also check the advertised maximum activation time against the measured one. Confidence high.
Hidden root cause

In the new firmware, the immediate activation path resets the controller's register-access logic while loading the new image and does not service memory reads for about 120 ms. The root port's completion timeout is shorter, so it logs Completion Timeout; when the delayed completion finally arrives, the root port logs Unexpected Completion. Applications see nothing because I/O was quiesced for activation. Only 2% of servers are affected because only those had the updater polling CSTS during activation.

Scenario 4 — Works at queue depth 1, fails at queue depth 128

Opening line: "Sequential 128 KiB writes work at queue depth 1. At queue depth 128 we get write errors and, once, data that fails verification. Where do you start?"

Facts on request

  • Reads at queue depth 128 are fine. Small writes (4 KiB) at queue depth 128 are fine.
  • Write commands fail with status Data Transfer Error. No PCIe errors at the root port.
  • Extended Tag Field Enable is set on both ends; 10-bit tags are not enabled.
Evidence card 1 — hand out when the candidate asks what changes with queue depth on the wire

Analyzer statistics: at QD1, the SSD has at most 8 memory reads outstanding. At QD128 with 128 KiB writes, it has up to 254 outstanding, peaking at 256 just before the first failure.

Model dialogue (strong candidate, condensed)
Candidate
Queue depth changes concurrency. What scales with concurrency: outstanding non-posted requests (tags), internal buffers, flow-control credits, completion queue occupancy. Large writes stress device reads specifically: a 128 KiB write at MRRS 512 is 256 memory reads. Small writes and reads do not stress that. So I suspect the device's outstanding-read handling, tags first.
Interviewer
(card 1)
Candidate
Peaking at 256 outstanding reads is exactly the 8-bit tag space. I want a tag-reuse check.
Interviewer
(card 2)
Candidate
Tag 0x00 reused while still outstanding: that is a device-side protocol violation. The device's tag allocator wraps instead of stalling when all tags are in use. Completions then match the wrong request: data corruption or a dropped completion. The root port sees nothing wrong because each completion is well formed. Confirm by capping outstanding reads, for example by lowering MRRS or queue depth so the device never needs 256, and showing the errors vanish. Fix in firmware: stall when the tag pool is empty. Regression test: QD256 large writes with an analyzer tag-uniqueness check and data verification. Confidence high.
Hidden root cause

The SSD's firmware tag allocator has an off-by-one: when all 256 tags are in use it hands out the oldest tag again instead of blocking. Duplicate outstanding tags make completions ambiguous. This only happens when the device needs more than 255 outstanding reads, which only large writes at high queue depth produce.

Scenario 5 — Only large I/O fails

Opening line: "Our storage application uses a user-space NVMe driver. 4 KiB and 8 KiB I/O works. Anything 12 KiB or larger fails with an NVMe error. What is going on?"

  • Status in the CQE: Status Code Type 0 (generic), Status Code Data Transfer Error.

  • Buffers are 4 KiB aligned.

The boundary between 8 and 12 KiB is exactly where PRP2 stops being a data pointer and becomes a pointer to a PRP list. The user-space driver allocates PRP list pages from a separate memory pool that it never mapped through VFIO. Small transfers never touch the list; anything needing three or more pages does, and the IOMMU blocks the device's read of the list.

For an NVMe Read, the SSD writes data to the host: posted writes, nothing to wait for. For an NVMe Write, the SSD reads the data: non-posted reads that need completions. "Reads work, writes hang" is the posted/non-posted split. Where are the write buffers?
Interviewer
(facts, card 1)
Candidate
A Completion Timeout logged by the SSD fits: its read requests are not answered. The buffers are in GPU memory, so the SSD's reads must travel peer to peer across root ports. Many platforms forward P2P writes but not P2P reads, or handle them differently. I want the addresses.
Interviewer
(card 2, card 3)
Candidate
Confirmed: reads to the GPU BAR range get no completion. The platform does not support P2P reads. Not an SSD bug. Workarounds: bounce writes through system memory; place SSD and GPU under a common PCIe switch that supports P2P; use the library's capability check rather than a manual allow-list. Regression: the library's platform qualification should test both directions.
Hidden root cause

The root complex silently drops peer-to-peer memory read requests between root ports. NVMe Writes from GPU memory therefore never get their data; the SSD's reads time out and the host's I/O timeout triggers a controller reset. The team had overridden the P2P library's platform check.

Scenario 11 — Occasionally reads data belonging to another request

Opening line: "A few times a week, our database reads a 4 KiB page and gets content that belongs to a different page. No errors anywhere. Our storage stack uses an in-house kernel-bypass NVMe driver."

Facts on request

  • The wrong content is always a whole 4 KiB page from another recent read request.
  • It happens only on servers that also log occasional I/O timeouts (slow reads during heavy background work).
  • The driver's timeout handling: on timeout, send Abort; on Abort completion, release the request's resources and retry.

Candidate
Whole 4 KiB pages of another request's data, no errors: that is a DMA landing in the wrong buffer, almost certainly a data-pointer problem rather than media. Correlation with timeouts suggests a resource-lifetime bug in the timeout path. I want the driver's timeline for a timed-out command.
Interviewer
(card 1)
Candidate
The Abort completed with "not aborted", and the driver released the buffers and PRP list anyway. The command is still live in the controller. The PRP list page was immediately reused for CID 0x5A. If the controller fetches the list late, it will follow the new contents.
Interviewer
(card 2)
Candidate
Confirmed. Root cause: the driver treats "Abort completed" as "command finished". An Abort's completion only reports whether the target was aborted; when it was not, the original command can still transfer data. Resources may only be released when the original command's own CQE arrives, or after a controller reset that guarantees all outstanding commands are terminated. Fix: honour the Abort result; on "not aborted", wait or escalate to reset before releasing. Regression: inject slow reads with an error-injection or throttling mechanism, force aborts, check buffers with poisoned patterns and the IOMMU in strict mode, which would also have turned some of these into faults. Confidence high.
Hidden root cause

A use-after-free in the driver's timeout path: it frees a command's PRP list and data buffers after an Abort that reported "not aborted". The controller later fetches the reused PRP list, which now points at another request's buffers, and writes the old command's data there.

Facts on request

  • The bay was empty when the server booted; the first drive was hot-inserted later and worked.
  • The new drive is a different model with a larger BAR (64 KiB instead of 16 KiB).

The memory window behind the hot-plug-capable root port was reserved at boot with a small default size (16 KiB), which happened to fit the first drive. The new drive's 64 KiB BAR (which must also be naturally aligned) does not fit, and neighbouring windows prevent the kernel from enlarging or moving it. No NVMe or SSD fault.

General scoring guide for scenario interviews

Each scenario has its own guide above. Across all scenarios, interviewers look for the same behaviours, which map onto the course rubric (see Senior Rubric and Final Certification Exam).

Behaviours scored in every scenario
BehaviourWeightStrong evidence of it
Scoping questions first10%Asks what changed, how often, which population, what is not affected
Layered hypotheses20%Names at least two layers and says what evidence would separate them
Targeted evidence requests15%Asks for the card that would discriminate, not "all logs"
First abnormal event20%Finds it and explains why later errors are consequences
Detector versus cause10%Never treats the logging agent as automatically guilty
Confirming experiment10%Proposes an A/B or injection test that would fail if the hypothesis were wrong
Fix, workaround, regression test10%Separates them; the regression targets the mechanism
Confidence and communication5%States confidence; disagrees respectfully with evidence

Red flags that cap the score at 3

  • Proposing a fix before any evidence ("update the firmware").
  • Inventing a specification detail with certainty when asked to justify a claim.
  • Ignoring evidence that contradicts the first hypothesis.
  • Blaming a team or vendor without showing their component's first abnormal event.

Write your own scenario

To create a new scenario, start from a case in the Failure Library and fill in this template:

  1. Opening line: the symptom as a non-specialist would report it.
  2. Facts on request: three to five facts the candidate can obtain by asking.
  3. Evidence cards: two to four, each one ruling something in or out; one should be a trace with the first abnormal event.
  4. A distractor: one piece of real but misleading evidence (a correctable error, a framing from another team).
  5. Hidden root cause: mechanism, not component.
  6. Scoring guide: what 0–3, 4, 5–6, 7 and 8 sound like.

Mastery gate and next steps

Do not move on until you can:

  • run any scenario on this page as the candidate and reach the hidden root cause using at most one interviewer prompt
  • for each scenario, name the first abnormal event and two later events that are consequences
  • explain in each case who detected the error and why that is or is not the cause
  • propose a confirming experiment and a regression test for each root cause
  • run a scenario as the interviewer, handing out cards only on request, and score the candidate with the guide
  • write one new scenario with the template, including a distractor
