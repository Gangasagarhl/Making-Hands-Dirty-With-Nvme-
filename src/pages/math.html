The arithmetic a storage and link engineer does every day — reading hex register dumps, converting units without losing 7%, turning GT/s into real bytes per second, connecting IOPS, latency and queue depth with Little's law, reading latency percentiles, and estimating write amplification, endurance and error rates. Every formula has its variables defined and at least one worked example on the course's synthetic lab system.

Why this page exists

Most wrong conclusions in performance and debugging work are not caused by a misunderstanding of the protocol. They are caused by arithmetic: a GB/s compared with a GiB/s, a zero-based field read as one-based, a link's raw rate compared with a device's payload rate, an average latency used where a percentile was needed, or a queue depth chosen without asking what latency it implies. This page collects the mathematics the rest of the course uses, explains each formula in plain English, and shows a worked example for each.

Learning objectives

  • Convert between binary, hexadecimal and decimal, and extract bit fields from register values.
  • Use decimal (SI) and binary (IEC) units correctly, and convert GT/s to bytes per second through the line encoding.
  • Compute effective Peripheral Component Interconnect Express (PCIe) bandwidth including Transaction Layer Packet (TLP) and framing overhead, and the per-command overhead of Non-Volatile Memory Express (NVMe).
  • Move between input/output operations per second (IOPS), throughput and transfer size.
  • Apply Little's law to queue depth, outstanding PCIe reads (tags) and flow-control credits.
  • Compute and interpret latency percentiles and tail behaviour, including fan-out.
  • Explain the queueing "knee" with utilization and a simple queueing model, and know the model's limits.
  • Estimate write amplification, endurance (TBW, DWPD) and error rates, and project wear from SMART data.

Prerequisites

You need the vocabulary of PCIe Generations (GT/s, encoding), TLP Header Anatomy (header sizes, MPS, MRRS), Queue Architecture (head, tail, queue depth) and What Is Storage? (NAND, FTL). The interactive versions of several formulas are in Bandwidth, Latency and Little's Law Calculators.

Conventions on this page

  • Hexadecimal is written 0x1F or 1Fh; underscores group digits for readability (0xFE60_0000).
  • KB, MB, GB, TB are decimal (103, 106, 109, 1012); KiB, MiB, GiB, TiB are binary (210, 220, 230, 240). Lowercase b means bits, uppercase B bytes.
  • Numbers labelled Derived are calculated from stated assumptions; they are not measurements. All examples use the synthetic lab system of Example Data Sets (PCIe Gen4 x4, MPS 256 B, MRRS 512 B, 4 KiB pages, 512-byte LBAs). Real values are implementation- and platform-dependent.

Binary, hexadecimal and bit fields

Why engineers think in hex

Each hexadecimal digit is exactly four bits, so a 32-bit register is exactly eight hex digits, and field boundaries that fall on multiples of four bits are visible at a glance. Decimal hides structure: 4,026,531,840 means nothing, while 0xF000_0000 is obviously "the top four bits set".

Extracting a bit field

field = (v >> lo) AND (2hi − lo + 1 − 1)
v
the whole register or DWORD value
lo
the lowest bit number of the field (bit 0 is the least significant)
hi
the highest bit number of the field
>>

In plain English: slide the field down to bit 0, then keep only as many bits as the field is wide.

Zero-based ("0's based") values

Many NVMe fields store one less than the quantity: queue sizes in Create I/O SQ/CQ and AQA, MQES, and the Number of Logical Blocks (NLB) in Read and Write. The reason is range: a 16-bit field can then express 1 to 65,536 instead of 0 to 65,535, and zero is never a useful size.

quantity = stored + 1
quantity
the real count (entries, blocks)
stored
the value in the field

Example: a Read with NLB = 7 on a 512-byte-LBA namespace transfers (7 + 1) × 512 = 4,096 bytes. Forgetting the +1 produces transfers one block short, and the device is right to complain about the buffer.

Endianness

NVMe data structures and PCIe configuration registers are little-endian: the byte at the lowest address is the least significant. A dump line 00: cd ab 20 04 therefore holds Vendor ID 0xABCD and Device ID 0x0420. TLP headers, by contrast, are conventionally drawn and transmitted byte 0 first with the most significant bits of each field leftmost. Analyzers already account for this; your own scripts must.

Circular queue arithmetic

used = (tail − head + N) mod N    full ⇔ (tail + 1) mod N = head
tail
the producer's next free slot (host for an SQ, controller for a CQ)
head
the consumer's next slot to read
N
the number of entries in the queue
used
entries currently in the queue

One slot is always left empty, so a queue of N entries holds at most N − 1 commands; otherwise "full" and "empty" would both look like head = tail. Example: SQ1 with N = 1024, tail = 3, head = 1020 → used = (3 − 1020 + 1024) mod 1024 = 7 commands waiting.

Transfers, bits and bytes on a link

A PCIe lane's rate is given in gigatransfers per second (GT/s): symbols (unit intervals) per second. With two-level non-return-to-zero (NRZ) signalling (Gen1–Gen5) one transfer carries one raw bit. With four-level pulse amplitude modulation (PAM4, Gen6 and Gen7) each symbol carries two bits, so 64 GT/s is reached at a symbol rate of 32 GBaud; the GT/s figure still tracks raw bits per second per lane. Raw bits then pass through a line encoding (or, in flit mode, flit-level CRC and FEC) before any data is carried.

Blane = R × e / 8
Blane
usable bytes per second per lane per direction, after encoding but before protocol overhead
R
raw bit rate per lane in bits per second (for NRZ, numerically equal to the GT/s figure × 109)
e
encoding efficiency (0.8 for 8b/10b; 128/130 for 128b/130b)
8
bits per byte

Example (Gen4): 16 × 109 × 128/130 / 8 = 1.969 × 109 B/s per lane per direction. A x4 link carries 4 × 1.969 = 7.877 GB/s in each direction simultaneously. Derived

Other unit traps

  • SMART Data Units. Data Units Read/Written count thousands of 512-byte units: one unit = 512,000 bytes. A value of 1,500,000 means 1,500,000 × 512,000 = 768 × 109 bytes = 768 GB written.
  • Temperature. NVMe reports temperatures in kelvin. 0x013C = 316 K → 316 − 273.15 = 42.85 °C (tools typically print 43 °C or 42 °C depending on rounding).
  • Time. 1 µs = 1,000 ns; 1 ms = 1,000 µs. CAP.TO is in 500 ms units; NVMe power-state latencies are in microseconds; PCIe completion timeout ranges span tens of microseconds to seconds.
  • DWORDs. TLP Length is in DWORDs (4 bytes): Length = 0x40 means 256 bytes.
  • Percent of what? "Link 90% utilized" means nothing until you say whether the denominator is raw bit rate, post-encoding rate or payload capacity.

Flit mode

In flit mode (required at 64 GT/s and above, optional at lower rates for flit-capable ports), data travels in fixed 256-byte flits that include TLP bytes, data-link-layer payload, CRC and forward error correction (FEC). Commonly published descriptions give 236 TLP bytes, 6 data-link bytes, 8 CRC bytes and 6 FEC bytes per flit, so about 236/256 ≈ 92.2% of flit bytes carry TLPs. Confirm the flit layout in the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 TLP headers are also encoded differently in flit mode, so the per-TLP overhead numbers in the next section apply to non-flit links (Gen1–Gen5) only.

Throughput tables in vendor material sometimes quote bidirectional totals (both directions added) — a x4 Gen4 link is then advertised as "~16 GB/s". An NVMe read uses mainly one direction (device to host), so for storage the one-direction number is the relevant ceiling.

DLLP and ordered-set overhead

Each Ack and UpdateFC DLLP occupies 8 bytes on the link (6-byte DLLP plus framing). How many are sent is implementation-dependent: receivers coalesce Acks and credit updates. A reasonable planning figure is a further 1–3% loss in each direction, plus a fraction of a percent for SKP ordered sets. Note that the Acks for device-to-host data flow host-to-device, so heavy reads put DLLP traffic on the otherwise quiet downstream direction. Implementation-dependent

Reads are different: completion size matters

When the device reads host memory (an NVMe Write command, or fetching SQEs), data comes back in completions (CplD, 3-DW header, H = 12). The completer — the root complex — decides how big each completion is, constrained by MPS and the read completion boundary (RCB). Many root complexes return data in 64-byte, cache-line-sized completions; others coalesce up to MPS. Implementation-dependent

The downstream (host-to-device) direction for the same command carries the SQ doorbell (MWr 32-bit, 24 bytes), the 64-byte SQE in a CplD (84 bytes), the CQ head doorbell (24 bytes) and the Acks for the upstream TLPs — under 200 bytes per command, so the downstream direction is nowhere near saturation in a read workload.

Before investigating a "slow SSD", compute the ceiling. If the measured throughput is 3.6 GB/s on what should be a Gen4 x4 link, the first suspect is not firmware: it is a link trained at Gen3 (3.94 GB/s ceiling) or at x2. Check LnkSta with lspci -vv before anything else (Chapter 69).

IOPS, throughput and transfer size

T = IOPS × S
T
throughput in bytes per second
IOPS
completed I/O commands per second
S
bytes transferred per command (transfer size, block size)

The same device is "1 million IOPS" and "4 GB/s" at 4 KiB; it might be "56,000 IOPS" and "7.3 GB/s" at 128 KiB. Neither number alone describes it. Small transfers are limited by per-command costs (controller processing, interrupts, CPU work in the driver); large transfers by bandwidth (link, NAND channels).

Worked examples

  • 1,000,000 IOPS × 4,096 B = 4.096 × 109 B/s = 4.10 GB/s = 3.81 GiB/s. Both are right; write which one you mean.
  • A 7.0 GB/s sequential read with 128 KiB transfers: IOPS = 7.0 × 109 / 131,072 ≈ 53,400 commands per second. Each command carries 131,072 / 256 = 512 MPS-sized data MWr TLPs, so a trace of one second contains about 27 million data TLPs, which is why analyzer captures fill fast and filters matter.
  • CPU budget: if the host stack costs 2 µs of CPU per I/O, one core sustains at most 1 / (2 × 10−6) = 500,000 IOPS. Reaching 1.5 million IOPS needs at least three cores' worth of submission and completion work — the reason NVMe has per-CPU queues. Derived

The roofline view

For a given device, achievable throughput is the minimum of two ceilings:

T(S) = min( IOPSmax × S ,  Bmax )
T(S)
best achievable throughput at transfer size S
IOPSmax
the device's (or host's) maximum command rate for small transfers
Bmax

S

The crossover size S* = Bmax / IOPSmax tells you where a workload stops being command-bound. Example: IOPSmax = 1.2 million, Bmax = 7.0 GB/s → S* = 5,833 bytes: transfers of 8 KiB and above are bandwidth-bound, 4 KiB and below command-bound.

Latency: budgets and components

Latency is a sum of stages, and every measurement point includes a different subset of them. The first rule of latency arithmetic is to say where the clock starts and stops: application call to return, block-layer submit to complete, doorbell write to CQE write on an analyzer, or command fetch to data on the link.

qi

si

Serialization time

tser

Example: the 4,480 link bytes carrying 4 KiB of read data at Gen4 x4 take 4,480 / 7.877 × 109 = 0.57 µs. At Gen3 x4 it would be 1.14 µs; at Gen5 x4, 0.28 µs. Compared with a NAND read of tens of microseconds, the link is a small part of a single read's latency — which is why moving an SSD from Gen4 to Gen5 improves bandwidth far more than it improves QD1 latency. Little's law: the queue-depth equation

L = λ × W
L
the average number of items in the system — for storage, the average number of commands outstanding (the queue depth actually in flight)
λ
the average throughput (arrival rate equals completion rate in a stable system) — for storage, IOPS
W
the average time each item spends in the system, in seconds — for storage, average latency measured at the same boundary as L

In plain English: if commands finish at a rate of λ per second and each one stays W seconds, then at any moment about λW of them are in flight. The law needs almost no assumptions — only that the system is stable over the measurement interval and that L, λ and W are measured over the same boundary. It does not care about distributions, scheduling order or the number of servers. That generality is what makes it the most useful equation in storage performance.

Why it matters for queues

  • Choosing queue depth. To reach a target IOPS at a given latency you need at least λW commands outstanding. Rearranged: λ = L / W — throughput is queue depth divided by latency.
  • Explaining QD1. At queue depth 1, IOPS = 1 / W. A drive with 80 µs read latency can do only 12,500 QD1 IOPS no matter how many "million IOPS" its datasheet says.
  • Detecting queueing. If you raise L and λ stops rising, W must be rising: you have hit a bottleneck and are now only adding waiting time.
  • Checking measurements. Measured IOPS × measured average latency should equal the configured queue depth (for a closed-loop benchmark that keeps the queue full). If it does not, the measurement boundaries differ or the benchmark is not keeping the queue full.

Worked example 1: what queue depth is needed?

Target 800,000 IOPS of 4 KiB random reads; the device's average latency at that load is 120 µs. L = 800,000 × 120 × 10−6 = 96 commands in flight. Across 8 submission queues that is 12 per queue. Running fio with iodepth=4 on 8 jobs (32 outstanding) can therefore deliver at most 32 / 120 µs ≈ 267,000 IOPS — the benchmark, not the device, is the limit. Derived

Worked example 2: a sanity check that caught a bug

A test reports 450,000 IOPS at QD 64 with average latency 400 µs. Little's law predicts L = 450,000 × 0.0004 = 180, not 64. Either latency is measured over a longer path than the queue depth (it includes time waiting in a software queue before submission), or the reported queue depth is per job and there are about three jobs. The arithmetic tells you which question to ask.

Little's law for PCIe tags

A device reading host memory (an NVMe Write, PRP list fetch or SQE fetch) can only have as many reads outstanding as it has tags. The bytes in flight needed to sustain a bandwidth is the bandwidth-delay product:

Ntags ≥ ( B × RTT ) / MRRS
Ntags
number of memory read requests that must be outstanding at once
B
target read bandwidth in bytes per second
RTT
average time from a read request leaving the device until its last completion arrives
MRRS
bytes requested per read (Max Read Request Size, assuming full-size requests)

Example: 7 GB/s with RTT = 1 µs and MRRS = 512 B needs 7,000 / 512 ≈ 14 outstanding reads — easy with 8-bit tags (256). Behind a switch with IOMMU misses, RTT = 4 µs and MRRS = 128 B needs 7 × 109 × 4 × 10−6 / 128 ≈ 219 — close to the 256 limit, and only 32 would be available if Extended Tag were disabled (5-bit tags). That is the quantitative case for 10-bit tags (Chapter 13). Derived

Little's law for flow-control credits

The same reasoning sizes receiver buffers: the credits advertised must cover the data that can be in flight during one credit loop (the time from sending a TLP until the UpdateFC that returns its credit is processed). Data credits needed ≈ B × Tloop / 16, because one data credit is 16 bytes. Example: 7.9 GB/s and a 1 µs credit loop → about 494 data credits. See Flow Control and Credits.

Latency distributions, percentiles and tail latency

Storage latency is not a number; it is a distribution, almost always skewed to the right: most commands finish quickly, a few take much longer because of garbage collection, read retries with stronger error correction, power-state exits, firmware housekeeping or host-side scheduling. The average hides that tail.

  • Mean = 2,299 / 20 ≈ 115 µs — larger than 19 of the 20 samples. The mean is dragged by one outlier.
  • p50: k = ⌈0.5 × 20⌉ = 10 → 68 µs.
  • p90: k = 18 → 85 µs.
  • p95: k = 19 → 140 µs.
  • p99: k = ⌈19.8⌉ = 20 → 910 µs. With only 20 samples, p99 is simply the maximum — a percentile needs enough samples to mean anything. Estimating p99.9 sensibly needs well over 1,000 samples, preferably tens of thousands.

Why the tail dominates at scale: fan-out

P(all fast) = pn    P(at least one slow) = 1 − pn
p
the probability that one sub-request is fast (for "faster than p99", p = 0.99)
n
the number of independent sub-requests a user request waits for (stripes, shards, replicas, a large I/O split into many commands)

If a request fans out to 100 drives and waits for all of them, P(all faster than their p99) = 0.99100 = 0.366. So 63% of user requests experience at least one p99-or-worse sub-request: the drive's p99 has become the system's median. The same applies inside one host when a large I/O is split at MDTS into many NVMe commands. This is the core argument of Dean and Barroso's "The Tail at Scale" (see References). The model assumes independence; correlated slowness (all drives garbage-collecting together) is worse. Derived

Mean, variance and why "average latency" misleads

μ = (1/N) Σ xi    σ2 = (1/N) Σ (xi − μ)2
μ
mean (average) latency
σ2
variance; σ is the standard deviation
xi
the i-th latency sample
N
number of samples

Use the mean for Little's law (it requires averages) and for capacity planning. Use percentiles for service-level objectives and debugging. Report both, with the sample count and the measurement boundary. A rule of thumb: when p99.9 is more than about ten times p50, something episodic (garbage collection, thermal throttling, retries, power-state exits, host preemption) is worth investigating.

Reading a latency histogram

A bimodal histogram — a large peak near 70 µs and a smaller one near 600 µs — almost always means two distinct paths, for example reads that hit a die busy with a program or erase versus reads that do not, or commands landing on a CPU whose interrupt handler is delayed. Split the samples by an attribute (LBA range, queue, CPU, time) until one population disappears; that attribute is your clue. See Performance Engineering.

Utilization and queueing intuition

ρ = λ × S  (one server)    ρ = λ × S / c  (c parallel servers)
ρ
utilization: the fraction of time the server (or each server) is busy, between 0 and 1
λ
arrival rate (requests per second)
S
average service time per request when there is no waiting
c
number of identical servers working in parallel (for an SSD, very roughly the number of NAND dies or planes that can work independently)

The simplest queueing model, the M/M/1 queue (random Poisson arrivals, exponentially distributed service times, one server), gives closed forms that capture the essential shape:

W = S / (1 − ρ)    L = ρ / (1 − ρ)
W
average time in the system (waiting plus service)
S
average service time
ρ
utilization
L
average number in the system (consistent with Little's law: L = λW)
M/M/1 response time as utilization rises (S = 10 µs)
ρW = S/(1−ρ)Average waiting (W − S)L

In plain English: waiting time explodes as a resource approaches full use. Going from 50% to 90% busy multiplies response time by five; going from 90% to 99% multiplies it by ten more. This "knee" is why latency-sensitive systems run devices well below their maximum IOPS, and why a latency-versus-IOPS curve from fio bends sharply upward near saturation.

An SSD is not an M/M/1 queue: it has many parallel dies, service times are far from exponential, and background work (garbage collection) steals capacity unpredictably. Use the model for intuition about the shape of the curve and for reasoning about a single bottleneck (one CPU core handling interrupts, one PCIe link direction), not for predicting exact numbers. Multi-server systems (M/M/c) have flatter curves until utilization is high, then the same explosion. Derived

Utilization of a PCIe link

The link is a single server per direction. Using the earlier per-command figure, at 1.2 million 4 KiB reads per second the upstream direction carries 1.2 × 106 × 4,568 = 5.48 GB/s of 7.877 GB/s: ρ ≈ 0.70. Queueing on the link itself adds little latency at that level because each TLP's service time is tiny (≈ 36 ns for 280 bytes), but at ρ above 0.9 TLPs start to wait behind each other and flow-control credit limits begin to show. Write amplification and over-provisioning

WAF = bytes written to NAND / bytes written by host
WAF
write amplification factor (1 means no amplification)
bytes written to NAND
host data plus everything the FTL writes on its own behalf: garbage-collection copies, mapping tables, metadata, refresh writes
bytes written by host
data the host sent with Write commands (the SMART Data Units Written figure, converted to bytes)

The host-side number is visible in the SMART / Health Information log; the NAND-side number is usually available only in vendor-specific logs. Both must cover the same interval: take two snapshots and subtract.

Where amplification comes from: garbage collection

Consider a victim erase block chosen for garbage collection that still has a fraction u of valid pages. To reclaim it, the FTL copies the valid pages elsewhere (u blocks' worth of writes) and gains (1 − u) blocks of free space for new host data.

WAFGC = 1 / (1 − u)
WAFGC
write amplification caused by garbage collection alone in steady state
u
average fraction of valid data in the blocks the FTL reclaims (0 ≤ u < 1)

In plain English: every host page written eventually costs one NAND page for itself plus u/(1 − u) pages of copying. Example: if reclaimed blocks are on average 75% valid, WAF = 1 / 0.25 = 4 — four NAND bytes per host byte. If they are 20% valid, WAF = 1.25.

What controls u? Mostly how much free space the FTL has to let blocks "age" before reclaiming them. That is set by over-provisioning:

OP = (Cphysical − Cuser) / Cuser
OP
over-provisioning ratio
Cphysical
NAND capacity available to the FTL for user data and spare (excluding capacity reserved for firmware and bad blocks)
Cuser
capacity the host can address (NSZE × LBA size across namespaces)

Example: 1,024 GiB of NAND exposing 1,000 GB to the user: Cphysical = 1,099.5 GB, OP = (1,099.5 − 1,000) / 1,000 ≈ 10%. A "960 GB" drive built on the same NAND has OP ≈ 14.5%. Data-centre drives with lower advertised capacity than client drives on the same NAND are trading capacity for lower WAF and higher endurance.

  • Sequential writes invalidate whole blocks together, so u is near 0 and WAF approaches 1.
  • Uniform random small writes over the full LBA space are the worst case; published analytical models (for example Hu et al., SYSTOR 2009) show WAF falling steeply as OP rises, with values of several at low OP. Model-dependent
  • Deallocate (TRIM) shrinks the valid data the FTL must preserve, effectively adding over-provisioning.
  • ZNS and Flexible Data Placement let the host group data with similar lifetimes, which aims to keep u low by design (Chapter 59).

Endurance: TBW, DWPD and wear projection

TBW = C × DWPD × 365 × Y
TBW
terabytes written: rated total host writes over the warranty (in TB when C is in TB)
C
user capacity of the drive (TB)
DWPD
drive writes per day: how many times per day the full user capacity may be written, every day of the warranty
Y
warranty period in years

Example: a 3.84 TB drive rated 1 DWPD for 5 years has TBW = 3.84 × 1 × 365 × 5 = 7,008 TB ≈ 7.0 PB. Conversely, a 1 TB drive rated 600 TBW over 5 years is 600 / (1 × 365 × 5) = 0.33 DWPD. Derived

From NAND cycles to host writes

host writesmax ≈ CNAND × PE / WAF
host writesmax
approximate total host data before the NAND reaches its rated wear
CNAND
NAND capacity subject to wear (roughly the physical capacity)
PE
rated program/erase cycles per block
WAF
average write amplification of the workload

Example: 1.1 TB of NAND rated 3,000 cycles under a workload with WAF = 2.5 → 1.1 × 3,000 / 2.5 = 1,320 TB of host writes. The same drive under a sequential workload (WAF ≈ 1.1) could absorb about 3,000 TB. A TBW rating therefore implies an assumed workload; JEDEC JESD218/JESD219 define standard endurance workloads for ratings. Derived Ratings are vendor-defined

Projecting wear from SMART

days left ≈ (100 − PUnow) × Δt / (PUnow − PUthen)
PUnow, PUthen
SMART Percentage Used at the two snapshots
Δt
days between the snapshots
days left
estimated days until Percentage Used reaches 100, if the workload stays the same

Example: Percentage Used went from 3 to 5 over 60 days. Days left ≈ (100 − 5) × 60 / 2 = 2,850 days ≈ 7.8 years. Percentage Used is a coarse integer, so use long intervals; cross-check with Data Units Written: if 1,500,000 units (768 GB) were written in those 60 days, the drive absorbs 12.8 GB/day, about 0.013 DWPD on a 1 TB drive. Reaching 100 does not mean failure; it means rated life is consumed (Chapter 57).

Error rates and reliability arithmetic

Bit error rate on a link

E = Rbits × BER    tbetween = 1 / E
E
expected bit errors per second
Rbits
raw bits per second on the link (all lanes, one direction)
BER
bit error ratio: errored bits / total bits
tbetween
average time between errors

Example: a Gen4 x4 link carries 64 × 109 raw bits per second per direction. At the commonly cited NRZ design target of BER = 10−12, E = 0.064 errors/s — about one every 16 seconds per direction. Each such error usually corrupts one TLP, which is caught by its LCRC, Nak'ed and replayed: a Bad TLP correctable error, a few hundred nanoseconds of lost time, and no data loss. A healthy link typically performs far better than the target. If AER correctable counters rise by thousands per hour, the link is well beyond its design margin. Derived

Probability of at least one error

P(≥ 1 error in n bits) = 1 − (1 − p)n ≈ 1 − e−np ≈ np  (when np ≪ 1)
p
probability that one bit is in error (the BER)
n
number of bits considered (one TLP, one transfer, one hour of traffic)

Example: a 280-byte TLP is 2,240 bits (plus encoding). At BER 10−12, P(error) ≈ 2.2 × 10−9. Replay overhead is therefore negligible on a healthy link; it becomes significant only when BER rises by several orders of magnitude, which is exactly when Naks, replays and Recovery entries show up in traces.

PAM4 and first-bit error rate

PAM4 links (Gen6, Gen7) operate at a much higher raw error rate (published figures are in the order of 10−6 first-bit error rate) and rely on FEC to correct most errors and on CRC plus flit retry for the rest. The arithmetic moves from "how often is a TLP replayed" to "how often does FEC fail and a flit get retried". Confirm targets in the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0

Unrecoverable read errors on the media

E[errors] = bits read × UBER
E[errors]
expected number of unrecoverable read errors
bits read
total bits read from the device
UBER
uncorrectable bit error rate: data errors per bit read after all error correction

JESD218 sets UBER requirements for SSD endurance ratings (commonly cited as 10−15 for client and 10−16 for enterprise classes; confirm in the standard). Reading a full 7.68 TB drive once is 6.1 × 1013 bits; at UBER 10−16 the expected number of unrecoverable errors is 0.006. Each one would appear to the host as a command completing with a media and data integrity error status (Status Code Type 2h) and an Error Information log entry, not as a PCIe error.

MTBF and annualized failure rate

AFR = 1 − e−H/MTBF ≈ H / MTBF
AFR
annualized failure rate: probability a device fails within one year of operation
H
hours of operation per year (8,766 for continuous operation)
MTBF
mean time between failures, in hours, as rated

Example: MTBF = 2,000,000 hours → AFR ≈ 8,766 / 2,000,000 = 0.44%. In a fleet of 10,000 drives, expect about 44 failures per year. MTBF assumes a constant failure rate, which real populations violate early and late in life; field studies (References) consistently show that rated and observed rates differ. Derived

Calculator code

All formulas from this page in one script. Executable with Python 3, standard library only. Change the inputs at the bottom to reproduce any worked example; the browser versions are in the calculators page.

"""Storage and link arithmetic from the Mathematics page.
All results are derived from stated assumptions, not measurements."""
import math

ENC = {1: (2.5, 0.8), 2: (5.0, 0.8), 3: (8.0, 128 / 130), 4: (16.0, 128 / 130), 5: (32.0, 128 / 130)}
FRAMING = 8  # framing + sequence number + LCRC bytes per TLP (non-flit)

def link_bytes_per_s(gen, lanes):
    """Post-encoding bandwidth, one direction (Gen1-Gen5)."""
    gts, eff = ENC[gen]
    return gts * 1e9 * eff / 8 * lanes

def tlp_efficiency(payload, header=16, ecrc=False):
    return payload / (payload + header + (4 if ecrc else 0) + FRAMING)

def tlp_bytes(payload, header):
    return payload + header + FRAMING

def read_cmd_upstream_bytes(xfer=4096, mps=256):
    """Device-to-host link bytes for one NVMe Read over PCIe, 64-bit addresses, MSI-X."""
    data_tlps = math.ceil(xfer / mps)
    return (tlp_bytes(0, 16)                    # SQE fetch MRd
            + data_tlps * tlp_bytes(mps, 16)    # data MWr
            + tlp_bytes(16, 16)                 # CQE MWr
            + tlp_bytes(4, 12))                 # MSI-X MWr (32-bit address)

def littles_law(iops=None, latency_s=None, outstanding=None):
    """Give any two of the three; returns the third."""
    if outstanding is None:
        return iops * latency_s
    if iops is None:
        return outstanding / latency_s
    return outstanding / iops

def percentile(samples, q):
    s = sorted(samples)
    k = math.ceil(q / 100 * len(s))
    return s[max(k, 1) - 1]

def mm1_response(service_s, rho):
    return service_s / (1 - rho)

def waf_gc(valid_fraction):
    return 1 / (1 - valid_fraction)

def tbw(capacity_tb, dwpd, years):
    return capacity_tb * dwpd * 365 * years

def errors_per_s(raw_bits_per_s, ber):
    return raw_bits_per_s * ber

if __name__ == "__main__":
    bw = link_bytes_per_s(4, 4)
    print(f"Gen4 x4 post-encoding: {bw / 1e9:.3f} GB/s per direction")
    for p in (128, 256, 512):
        print(f"  MWr payload {p:4d} B: efficiency {tlp_efficiency(p):.1%}, ceiling {bw * tlp_efficiency(p) / 1e9:.2f} GB/s")
    per_cmd = read_cmd_upstream_bytes()
    print(f"4 KiB read: {per_cmd} upstream bytes, link-limited {bw / per_cmd / 1e6:.2f} M IOPS")
    print(f"Little: 800k IOPS at 120 us needs {littles_law(iops=800e3, latency_s=120e-6):.0f} outstanding")
    print(f"Little: QD1 at 80 us gives {littles_law(outstanding=1, latency_s=80e-6):.0f} IOPS")
    lat = [62, 63, 64, 64, 65, 66, 66, 67, 68, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72, 74, 76, 79, 85, 140, 910]
    print("p50/p90/p95/p99:", [percentile(lat, q) for q in (50, 90, 95, 99)], "mean", sum(lat) / len(lat))
    print(f"Fan-out 100, all below p99: {0.99 ** 100:.3f}")
    print("M/M/1, S=10us:", [round(mm1_response(10e-6, r) * 1e6) for r in (0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99)], "us")
    print(f"WAF at 75% valid victims: {waf_gc(0.75):.2f}")
    print(f"TBW 3.84 TB, 1 DWPD, 5 y: {tbw(3.84, 1, 5):.0f} TB")
    e = errors_per_s(64e9, 1e-12)
    print(f"Gen4 x4 at BER 1e-12: {e:.3f} errors/s, one every {1 / e:.1f} s")
    print(f"AFR at MTBF 2e6 h: {1 - math.exp(-8766 / 2e6):.2%}")

Expected output (derived from the formulas, rounded): Gen4 x4 7.877 GB/s; efficiencies 84.2%, 91.4%, 95.5%; 4,568 upstream bytes and about 1.72 M IOPS; 96 outstanding; 12,500 IOPS; percentiles [68, 85, 140, 910] with mean 114.95; fan-out 0.366; M/M/1 [20, 50, 100, 200, 1000] µs; WAF 4.00; TBW 7,008 TB; 0.064 errors/s, one every 15.6 s; AFR 0.44%.

Exercises

  1. A dump shows AQA = 0x001F_001F. How many entries does each admin queue have, and how many admin commands can be outstanding at once?
    Answer

    Both fields are 0x1F = 31, zero-based → 32 entries each. One slot must stay empty, so at most 31 admin commands can be in the SQ at once.

  2. Compute the post-encoding bandwidth of a Gen5 x4 link and the payload ceiling for 256-byte MWr TLPs with 64-bit addresses.
    Answer

    32 × 109 × 128/130 / 8 × 4 = 15.75 GB/s; × 256/280 = 14.40 GB/s before DLLP overhead.

  3. An application needs 300,000 IOPS of 16 KiB reads. What throughput is that, in GB/s and GiB/s, and is a Gen3 x4 link enough?
    Answer

    300,000 × 16,384 = 4.92 GB/s = 4.58 GiB/s. Gen3 x4 offers 3.94 GB/s before protocol overhead: not enough. Gen4 x4 (about 7.2 GB/s of payload at MPS 256) is.

  4. fio reports 210,000 IOPS with average completion latency 610 µs, iodepth=128 on one job. Is this consistent?
    Answer

    L = 210,000 × 0.00061 = 128.1. Consistent: the benchmark kept 128 commands in flight, and the device's throughput at that depth is 210k IOPS. The high latency is queueing: the device is saturated, and lowering iodepth would lower latency with little IOPS loss.

  5. A drive's Percentage Used went from 12 to 15 in 90 days. Estimate the remaining rated life.
    Answer

    (100 − 15) × 90 / 3 = 2,550 days ≈ 7 years, assuming the workload stays the same. Note the coarse resolution: with one-percent steps, use long intervals.

  6. For an SSD writing data from host memory through a root complex that returns 64-byte completions, what fraction of the downstream link carries data?
    Answer

    64 / (64 + 12 + 8) = 76.2% of TLP bytes, before DLLP overhead. Compare 92.8% for 256-byte completions.

  7. Write a function that, given BAR0, DSTRD and a doorbell address seen in a trace, returns whether it is an SQ tail or CQ head doorbell and for which queue.
    Answer

    stride = 4 << DSTRD; idx = (addr − BAR0 − 0x1000) / stride; queue = idx // 2; kind = "SQ tail" if idx is even else "CQ head". Reject addresses below BAR0 + 0x1000 or not aligned to the stride.

Quiz

  1. A "2 TB" SSD shows as about how many GiB in a tool that uses binary units?

    • 2,048 GiB
    • 1,863 GiB
    • 2,000 GiB
    • 1,907 GiB
    Answer and reasoning

    2 × 1012 / 230 = 1,862.6 GiB. Nothing is missing; the units differ by 7.4% at the giga scale.

  2. Why did PCIe Gen3 almost double Gen2's throughput while raising the transfer rate only from 5 to 8 GT/s?

    • Gen3 uses PAM4
    • Gen3 doubled the lane count
    • Gen3 replaced 8b/10b (80%) with 128b/130b (98.5%) encoding
    • Gen3 removed the LCRC
    Answer and reasoning

    8 × 0.9846 = 7.88 Gb/s of payload bits versus 5 × 0.8 = 4 Gb/s: a factor of 1.97. PAM4 arrived with Gen6, and the LCRC remains.

  3. A drive has 100 µs average latency at queue depth 1. What is its QD1 IOPS?

    • 10,000
    • 100,000
    • 1,000,000
    • It cannot be calculated without the transfer size
    Answer and reasoning

    Little's law: λ = L / W = 1 / 100 µs = 10,000 IOPS. Transfer size affects throughput in bytes, not this command rate.

  4. Which statement about Little's law is correct?

    • It requires Poisson arrivals
    • It only holds for a single server
    • It applies to percentiles as well as averages
    • It holds for averages in any stable system measured over one boundary
    Answer and reasoning

    Little's law is distribution-free and works for any number of servers, but relates averages only. It says nothing about p99.

  5. A request fans out to 50 independent drives and waits for all of them. Roughly what fraction of requests see at least one sub-request slower than the drives' p99?

    • 1%
    • 39%
    • 50%
    • 99%
    Answer and reasoning

    1 − 0.9950 = 1 − 0.605 = 0.395.

  6. In a simple single-server queue, utilization rises from 0.8 to 0.95. By what factor does average response time grow?

    • 1.19
    • 2
    • 4
    • It stays the same
    Answer and reasoning

    W = S/(1 − ρ): S/0.2 = 5S versus S/0.05 = 20S — a factor of 4, while throughput rose only 19%.

  7. Garbage collection reclaims blocks that are on average 60% valid. What is the GC write amplification?

    • 2.5
    • 1.6
    • 0.4
    • 1.67
    Answer and reasoning

    1 / (1 − 0.6) = 2.5: each host page costs itself plus 1.5 pages of copying.

  8. A 1.92 TB drive is rated 3 DWPD for 5 years. What is its TBW?

    • 2,880 TB
    • 5,760 TB
    • 10,512 TB
    • 28.8 TB
    Answer and reasoning

    1.92 × 3 × 365 × 5 = 10,512 TB ≈ 10.5 PB.

  9. Explain why moving an SSD from a Gen4 x4 to a Gen5 x4 link may double sequential throughput but barely change 4 KiB QD1 read latency.

    Model answer

    Sequential throughput is bandwidth-bound, and Gen5 doubles the link bandwidth (if the NAND and controller can keep up). QD1 latency is a sum of stages dominated by the NAND read (tens of microseconds) and host software (microseconds); the link's share — serialization of about 4.5 KB plus a few small TLPs — falls from about 0.6 µs to 0.3 µs, under 1% of the total.

  10. An engineer reports "the SSD averages 90 µs, so it meets the 200 µs SLO". What is missing?

    Model answer

    The SLO is almost certainly about a percentile (e.g. p99 ≤ 200 µs), not the mean. A mean of 90 µs is compatible with a p99 of several milliseconds. Report p50, p99, p99.9 and max, with sample count, measurement boundary, queue depth and the workload's steady-state status.
