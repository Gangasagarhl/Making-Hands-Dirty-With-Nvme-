In Chapter 1 the Warehouse was a black box that "never forgets". This chapter opens it. You will learn how spinning disks and flash chips actually keep data, why flash needs a hidden layer of software to look like a disk, and the three numbers every storage engineer lives by: latency, input/output operations per second (IOPS) and throughput. By the end you will see exactly why the old ways of talking to storage could not keep up, and why NVM Express (NVMe) had to exist.

Why this chapter exists

You cannot debug a storage protocol without knowing what is on the other end of it. A completion that arrives 4 milliseconds late is a mystery if you think of the drive as a box of memory; it is obvious if you know that flash blocks must be erased before reuse and that the drive sometimes has to move data around to make room. Protocol engineers who understand the media read traces differently: they know which gaps are normal and which are not.

This chapter also explains why NVMe looks the way it does. NVMe's many deep queues are not an arbitrary design: they are the answer to a device that contains dozens of independent flash units that can all work at the same time.

Learning objectives

  • Explain how a hard disk drive (HDD) stores and retrieves data and why its latency is measured in milliseconds.
  • Describe Serial ATA (SATA) and Serial Attached SCSI (SAS) as interfaces and name their key limits.
  • Describe NAND flash organization: cells, pages, blocks, planes, dies and channels.
  • Explain the erase-before-write constraint and how the flash translation layer hides it.
  • Explain garbage collection, write amplification, wear leveling, over-provisioning and endurance, and compute each from given numbers.
  • Define latency, IOPS and throughput precisely and relate them with formulas, including Little's Law.
  • Explain why a solid-state drive's (SSD's) internal parallelism demanded a new host interface.

The story

The town from Chapter 1 has had two warehouses in its history.

The old warehouse was a huge round room with shelves arranged on a spinning carousel. One robot arm stood beside it. To fetch a page, the arm had to slide to the right ring of shelves, then wait for the carousel to spin the right shelf around to it. Sliding and waiting took a long time, about as long as it takes you to blink, which for the Thinker felt like a year. And there was only one arm, so requests were answered one at a time.

The new warehouse has no moving parts at all. It is made of thousands of chalkboards, grouped into walls. Any chalkboard can be read in an instant, and there are many clerks, each in charge of a few walls, all able to work at once. But chalkboards here have two odd rules. First, you can only write on a board that has been wiped clean. Second, you cannot wipe a single board: you can only wipe an entire wall at once. And every wipe wears the boards a little; after a few thousand wipes, a board stops holding chalk.

So the new warehouse's chief clerk keeps a big map book. When the Thinker says "change page 7", the clerk does not wipe anything. He writes the new page 7 on a fresh, clean board somewhere else, crosses out the old one in his map, and updates the map: "page 7 now lives on wall 30, board 12". When a wall is mostly crossed-out boards, the clerk copies the few still-good boards to a fresh wall and then wipes the whole old wall. He also makes sure no wall gets wiped much more often than the others, so they all wear out evenly. And he secretly keeps some spare walls the Thinker does not know about, so there is always room to shuffle.

The Thinker never sees any of this. She just says "page 7" and gets page 7.

Picture a self-storage facility where any empty locker can be filled, but lockers can only be emptied a whole corridor at a time, and each emptying slightly damages the locker doors. The front desk keeps a register that maps each customer's box number to a locker. Updating a box means putting the new version in an empty locker and marking the old locker "stale" in the register. At night, staff consolidate the few live boxes out of mostly-stale corridors and empty those corridors. The facility keeps some corridors off the public plan as working space. Customers only ever see their box numbers.

What the analogies hide. Flash "erasing" is not cleaning: it is applying a high voltage to remove stored charge from every cell in a block, and the wear comes from damage to the insulating layer each time. Pages are not individually crossed out on the flash itself; "stale" exists only in the controller's tables. Flash cells also leak charge over time and disturb their neighbors when read, so the clerk must sometimes rewrite data that nobody changed. And in the story, reading is instant; real flash reads take tens of microseconds, which is fast compared with a disk but slow compared with memory.

Where they mislead. The story's "map book" sounds like a small notebook. In a real 1 TB SSD it is typically around a gigabyte of mapping data, kept in the drive's own DRAM (dynamic random-access memory) and saved to flash. Losing it, or corrupting it, would make the whole drive unreadable, which is why power-loss protection matters.

Beginner explanation

Storage is any place a computer can put information so that it survives a power cut. The two kinds that matter today are:

  • Hard disk drives (HDDs): magnetic spinning platters read by heads on a moving arm. Cheap per byte, huge, but slow to reach any particular place.
  • Solid-state drives (SSDs): flash memory chips managed by a controller. No moving parts, fast to reach any place, highly parallel inside, but with awkward rules about how data can be written.

Both are presented to the computer in the same simple way: a long list of numbered logical blocks (usually 512 bytes or 4 KiB each) that can be read and written. The computer says "read 8 blocks starting at block 2048" and does not care how the drive finds them. Behind that simple face, an SSD's controller runs a sophisticated program, the flash translation layer, to hide flash's awkward rules.

To talk to a drive, a computer uses an interface: a physical connection plus a protocol. For decades these were SATA (for consumer disks) and SAS (for enterprise disks). Both were designed when the device was a spinning disk. When SSDs arrived, they were fast enough to make those interfaces the bottleneck, which is the story of Chapter 3.

Storage performance is described by three numbers:

  • Latency: how long one request takes, from asking to getting the answer.
  • IOPS (input/output operations per second): how many requests complete each second.
  • Throughput (or bandwidth): how many bytes move per second.

Hard disks, SATA and SAS

HDD

A record player that can also write: a spinning disc, and an arm that swings to the right ring.

A hard disk drive (HDD) stores bits as tiny magnetized regions on the surface of rigid platters spinning at a constant speed (commonly 5,400, 7,200, 10,000 or 15,000 revolutions per minute). Read/write heads float nanometres above each surface on an actuator arm. Data lies in concentric tracks, divided into sectors of 512 bytes or 4 KiB.

To service a request at a random location the drive must:

  1. Seek: move the arm to the right track (several milliseconds on average).
  2. Wait for rotation: wait until the right sector passes under the head (on average half a revolution).
  3. Transfer: read the sectors as they pass (fast once started).
Taccess = Tseek + Trot + Txfer, with Trot = ½ × 60 / RPM
Taccess
average time to service one random request, in seconds
Tseek
average time to move the heads to the target track
Trot
average rotational latency: half of one revolution
RPM
platter speed in revolutions per minute (60 / RPM is the time for one revolution, in seconds)
Txfer
time to read or write the requested sectors once they arrive under the head

For a 7,200 RPM drive, one revolution takes 60 / 7200 = 8.33 ms, so average rotational latency is 4.17 ms. Add a typical average seek of around 8 ms and a negligible transfer for 4 KiB, and a random read costs about 12 ms. That is roughly 80 random reads per second. Sequential transfers, by contrast, can stream at a few hundred megabytes per second because no seeking is needed. Implementation-dependent figures vary by model.

Why this matters for protocols. Because a disk has one actuator and mechanical delays, a deep queue helps only by letting the drive reorder requests to shorten seeks. Interfaces built for disks therefore needed modest queue depths (tens of commands) and could afford microseconds of protocol overhead per command; nobody would notice 10 µs against 12 ms.

HDD random access: milliseconds. One mechanical arm. Queues used for reordering, not for parallelism.

SATA

Serial ATA (SATA) is the serial successor to the parallel ATA (AT Attachment) disk interface, defined by the SATA International Organization (SATA-IO). It connects one drive to one host port over a point-to-point serial link. Its third revision runs at 6 Gb/s; with 8b/10b encoding (10 bits on the wire for every 8 bits of data) that is about 600 MB/s of usable bandwidth, a ceiling every SATA SSD hits.

Commands are the ATA command set, carried in packets called Frame Information Structures (FIS). Native Command Queuing (NCQ) lets a drive hold up to 32 outstanding commands and complete them out of order. The link is effectively half-duplex: frames move in one direction at a time. On the host side, SATA ports are almost always driven through an AHCI (Advanced Host Controller Interface) host controller, the subject of Chapter 3.

SAS

Serial Attached SCSI (SAS) is the enterprise counterpart, carrying the SCSI (Small Computer System Interface) command set over serial links. Its standards are developed by the INCITS T10 committee. Compared with SATA, SAS offers full-duplex links, dual ports (two independent paths to the same drive, so a server can survive a controller failure), expanders that connect many drives to one host bus adapter, deeper command queuing, and higher rates (12 Gb/s, then a generation marketed as "24G"). SAS host bus adapters can also connect SATA drives.

SAS was a better fit for enterprise SSDs than SATA, and SAS SSDs remain in service. But a SAS SSD still sits behind a host bus adapter: the central processing unit (CPU) talks to the adapter over PCI Express (PCIe), and the adapter talks SAS to the drive. Every command crosses two protocols and an extra chip.

SATA and SAS line rates and features evolved across revisions; the figures above are the commonly deployed generations. Confirm exact revision features in the SATA-IO and T10 documents if you need them.

Flash memory inside an SSD

SSD

A solid-state drive (SSD) is a storage device built from non-volatile semiconductor memory, today almost always NAND flash, plus a controller that makes it look like a disk: a linear array of logical blocks. A typical SSD contains:

  • a host interface (SATA, SAS, or PCIe for NVMe);
  • a controller system-on-chip with several embedded processor cores running firmware, hardware accelerators for error correction, encryption and data movement, and DMA engines;
  • DRAM (on many drives) holding the mapping table and buffering data; some drives instead borrow a slice of host memory through an NVMe feature called the Host Memory Buffer;
  • several flash channels, each connecting the controller to a set of NAND dies;
  • on enterprise drives, power-loss protection capacitors that keep the drive alive long enough to save in-flight data and tables if power fails.

Its key property for this course: an SSD can perform many operations at once. Its latency is tens of microseconds rather than milliseconds, and its internal parallelism can be dozens of independent operations.

NAND flash

NAND flash (named after the NOT-AND logic arrangement of its cell strings) stores bits as electric charge trapped in each cell's charge-storage layer, which shifts the cell's threshold voltage. Reading senses which voltage range the cell is in. Storing more bits per cell means distinguishing more voltage levels:

Modern NAND is 3D: cells are stacked in vertical strings of a hundred or more layers. Three operations exist, with very different costs:

Flash cannot be overwritten in place. A programmed page must be erased before it can be programmed again, and erase works only on whole blocks containing hundreds of pages. Every interesting SSD behavior (the flash translation layer, garbage collection, write amplification, unpredictable write latency) follows from this one rule.

NAND pages

A page is the unit of reading and programming, typically 16 KiB of data on current TLC parts, plus a spare area holding error-correcting code (ECC) and metadata. Note the mismatch: the host thinks in 512-byte or 4 KiB logical blocks; the flash thinks in 16 KiB pages. The controller packs several logical blocks into each page and must handle partial updates.

NAND blocks

A block (erase block) is the unit of erasure: hundreds to thousands of pages, so many megabytes. Pages within a block are generally programmed in order. A block wears out after its rated number of program/erase cycles; it is then retired, which is one reason drives keep spare blocks.

Channels

A channel is a bus between the controller and a group of NAND packages, carrying commands and data (by interfaces standardized by groups such as ONFI, the Open NAND Flash Interface, and the JEDEC Toggle interface). Controllers have several channels, commonly 8 or 16, each transferring data independently. More channels means more data can move between flash and controller at once.

Dies

A die is one piece of silicon: the smallest unit that can execute a flash operation independently. Several dies share each channel. While one die is busy with a 60 µs read or a 1 ms program, the channel can be used to send commands to, and move data for, the others. A drive with 8 channels and 4 dies per channel has 32 dies that can work in parallel.

Planes

Each die is divided into planes (commonly 2 to 6), each with its own page buffer. Multi-plane operations let a die read or program one page in each plane at the same time, multiplying throughput again, usually with restrictions on which page addresses can be combined.

Sixty-four units that can each work independently only help if there are enough requests in flight to keep them busy. A host that sends one request and waits for it uses one unit at a time. This single fact is the deepest reason NVMe exists: the device needed a host interface that could keep hundreds or thousands of requests in flight cheaply.

The flash translation layer

Flash translation layer

The flash translation layer (FTL) is the controller firmware (with hardware help) that presents flash as a rewritable array of logical blocks. Its central data structure is the logical-to-physical (L2P) mapping table: for each logical unit (often 4 KiB, that is, several logical block addresses or LBAs), where its current copy lives in flash (channel, die, plane, block, page, offset).

A write never overwrites. The FTL writes the new data to a fresh location at the current write point, updates the table, and marks the old location stale. This is called out-of-place update. Reads look up the table and fetch the current copy.

With a 4-byte entry per 4 KiB logical unit, the table is 1/1024 of capacity: about 1 GB of table for a 1 TB drive. This is the origin of the common rule of thumb "1 GB of SSD DRAM per 1 TB of flash". Real FTLs vary: some use larger mapping units or cache only part of the table. Implementation-dependent

Garbage collection

Out-of-place updates leave stale pages scattered across blocks. Eventually the FTL runs out of erased blocks and must reclaim space: it picks a victim block, copies that block's remaining valid pages to the write point, and erases the victim. This is garbage collection (GC).

Copying valid pages costs extra flash writes that the host never asked for. The ratio is write amplification:

WAF = WNAND / Whost
WAF
write amplification factor (1.0 is ideal; higher means more wear and less performance)
WNAND
bytes actually programmed to flash, including garbage-collection copies
Whost
bytes the host asked to write

Garbage collection competes with host input/output (I/O) for dies and channels. When it runs heavily, host commands wait behind it. This is the main source of SSD tail latency: most commands complete in tens of microseconds, but a few take milliseconds. You will see it in the trace view below.

The host can help. When files are deleted, the operating system can tell the drive which logical blocks no longer hold useful data. In NVMe this is the Deallocate attribute of the Dataset Management command (Chapter 35); in SATA it is TRIM; in SCSI, UNMAP. Deallocated blocks are stale immediately, so GC has less to copy.

Wear leveling

Each block survives a limited number of program/erase cycles. If some data is rewritten constantly (a log file) and some never (an archive), the blocks holding hot data would wear out first. Wear leveling spreads erases evenly: dynamic wear leveling steers new writes to less-worn free blocks; static wear leveling occasionally moves cold data off barely-worn blocks so those blocks can take their share of the churn. Static wear leveling adds write amplification of its own.

Over-provisioning

Over-provisioning (OP) is flash capacity the host cannot address, reserved for the FTL's working space, bad-block replacement and metadata.

OP = (Cphysical − Cuser) / Cuser
OP
over-provisioning ratio (often quoted as a percentage)
Cphysical
raw flash capacity available to the FTL
Cuser

Flash is manufactured in binary sizes; drives are sold in decimal sizes. A drive with 1024 GiB of flash sold as "1 TB" has about 10% spare from that alone. Enterprise drives sold as 800 GB with 1 TiB-class flash have far more. More spare means GC finds victims with fewer valid pages, so write amplification falls, and sustained random-write performance and endurance rise. The lab below lets you measure this.

Endurance

Endurance is how much data can be written before the drive is expected to wear out. It is quoted two ways:

TBW = DWPD × C × 365 × Y
TBW
terabytes written: total host writes the drive is rated for, in TB
DWPD

C

Y

Example: a 1.92 TB drive rated 1 DWPD for 5 years: 1 × 1.92 × 365 × 5 ≈ 3,504 TBW. Rated endurance depends on the NAND's cycles, the expected write amplification and over-provisioning. NVMe drives report wear through the SMART (Self-Monitoring, Analysis and Reporting Technology) / Health Information log page, including a "Percentage Used" estimate and counts of data units read and written (Chapter 57). Retention also matters: worn flash holds charge for less time, so a heavily worn drive left unpowered may lose data sooner.

Latency, IOPS and throughput

Latency

Latency is the time from issuing a request to receiving its completion. Always say where it is measured: inside the drive, at the PCIe link (doorbell to completion entry), at the driver, or at the application. Each adds layers. And always say which statistic: average latency hides the tail. Storage engineers quote percentiles: p50 (median), p99, p99.9, p99.99. A drive with a 70 µs median and a 5 ms p99.9 behaves very differently from one with a 90 µs median and a 300 µs p99.9 under a database.

MediumTypical latency

IOPS

IOPS (input/output operations per second) counts completed commands per second. It is meaningful only with three qualifiers: the block size (4 KiB is standard for random tests), the pattern (random or sequential, read or write or mixed) and the queue depth (how many commands are outstanding). Little's Law links them:

L = λ × W ⇔ λ = L / W
L
average number of commands outstanding (effective queue depth)
λ
throughput in commands per second (IOPS)
W
average latency per command, in seconds

At queue depth 1 with 80 µs latency, IOPS = 1 / 0.00008 = 12,500. At queue depth 32, if latency stayed at 80 µs because 32 dies work in parallel, IOPS = 32 / 0.00008 = 400,000. In practice latency rises as the device saturates, so IOPS flattens. An HDD at 12 ms gives about 83 IOPS at queue depth 1, and reordering only modestly improves that, because it has one arm.

Throughput

Throughput (bandwidth) is bytes per second:

B = λ × S
B
throughput, bytes per second
λ
IOPS
S
bytes transferred per command (block size)

400,000 IOPS × 4096 bytes ≈ 1.64 GB/s. Large sequential transfers reach the limit of whichever is smallest: the flash back end, the controller, or the host link. For the reference machine's Gen4 x4 link that ceiling is about 7.9 GB/s per direction before protocol overhead; for SATA it is about 600 MB/s. Notice that a SATA SSD doing 4 KiB random reads at 100,000 IOPS already moves 410 MB/s, most of its link.

Never quote one storage number alone. "500k IOPS" means nothing without block size, pattern, queue depth and the latency percentile achieved.

The industry's answer had two parts: put the SSD directly on PCIe (which already scaled in lanes and generations and connected straight to the CPU's root complex), and define a new, lean, queue-based protocol designed for non-volatile memory: NVM Express. Chapter 3 tells that story in detail.

The central concept, climbed: the out-of-place write

You cannot rub out one chalkboard, only a whole wall. So to change page 7 the clerk writes it fresh on a clean board, crosses out the old one in his map, and later wipes whole walls that are mostly crossed out, first copying any boards that are still needed.

NAND pages can only be programmed when erased, and only whole blocks can be erased. The flash translation layer writes every update to a new page, updates the logical-to-physical map, and marks the old page stale. Garbage collection later relocates valid pages and erases blocks.

The controller's firmware runs the FTL on embedded cores; the map lives in the drive's DRAM (persisted to flash); flash commands go out over channels to dies; ECC engines check every page read; power-loss capacitors let the controller save the map and buffered writes on sudden power failure.

No NVMe specification defines the FTL. The NVMe Base and NVM Command Set specifications define the logical view (namespaces of logical blocks, Read, Write, Flush, Dataset Management with Deallocate), and reporting such as the SMART / Health Information log. How the controller places data in flash is entirely implementation-specific. Implementation-dependent

On the host interface you see only the logical operations: a Write command, its data transfer, its completion. Garbage collection, relocations and erases generate no host-visible protocol traffic at all.

In a trace, the FTL's work appears only as timing: the gap between command fetch and data transfer for reads, or between data transfer and completion for writes, grows when GC is busy. Occasional multi-millisecond gaps under sustained random writes are the classic signature.

Tail-latency complaints under write-heavy workloads: a senior engineer checks the fill level and over-provisioning, whether the host issues Deallocate, the write pattern (random small writes amplify most), whether latency spikes correlate with write volume, and the drive's health log. The protocol is rarely at fault; the media management is.

Specification view

Several standards bodies touch this chapter: SATA-IO (SATA), INCITS T10 (SCSI and SAS), ONFI and JEDEC (NAND interfaces), and NVM Express (NVMe). For this course, the NVMe documents matter most, and the key lesson is how little of an SSD they define.

  • NVM Express Base Specification 2.4 — namespaces, the SMART / Health Information log page, features such as Volatile Write Cache and Host Memory Buffer, Endurance Groups
  • NVM Command Set Specification 1.3 — Read, Write, Flush, Dataset Management (Deallocate), Write Zeroes, logical block formats

NVMe exposes storage as namespaces of logical blocks whose size is given by the selected LBA format. Dataset Management (opcode 09h) can mark ranges as deallocated; Flush is opcode 00h. The SMART / Health Information log page includes fields named Percentage Used, Data Units Read and Data Units Written. Verified

That GC causes tail latency; that over-provisioning lowers write amplification; that the L2P table is about 1/1024 of capacity with 4-byte entries per 4 KiB. These follow from how flash works and are widely observed, but no NVMe document states them.

Everything inside the drive: geometry (channels, dies, planes), page and block sizes, FTL mapping granularity, GC policy, wear-leveling policy, over-provisioning amount, DRAM size, read-retry behavior, and the exact latency distribution. Also what a drive does physically when it receives Deallocate.

NVMe has added media-aware features over time: Endurance Groups and NVM Sets, Predictable Latency Mode, Zoned Namespaces (now its own command set, revision 1.5 in the 2026 set), and Flexible Data Placement in the 2.x family. Check the revision that introduced a feature before assuming a drive supports it. Uncertain on the exact revision for each; confirm in the Revision Changes document.

The exact units of Data Units Read/Written (they are counted in large multiples of 512 bytes, not in bytes or logical blocks; confirm the multiplier in the SMART / Health Information log definition), how Percentage Used may exceed 100, what Deallocate guarantees about subsequent reads (the reported deallocated-read behavior), and whether Flush is required for durability given the Volatile Write Cache feature.

That reading deallocated blocks returns zeros unless the drive reports that behavior; that a successful Write completion means data is on flash (it may be in a volatile cache); that two drives with the same NAND behave alike; that "IOPS" quoted on a datasheet applies to your workload.

Protocol view

The host-to-drive protocol carries only logical operations. Internally, the controller speaks a completely different protocol to its NAND dies over its channels. Two views of the same write show the boundary:

Direction matters even here. For a host Read, data flows device → host (the device issues Memory Writes). For a host Write, data flows host → device, but the device still initiates: it issues Memory Read requests and the root complex returns Completions with the data. In NVMe over PCIe, the device moves the data in both cases.

Code / pseudocode

A toy flash translation layer

This program models a page-mapped FTL with greedy garbage collection on a tiny flash of 128 blocks × 64 pages. It fills the drive once, then performs four drive-fulls of random overwrites, and reports write amplification at three over-provisioning levels. It is a simulation for teaching; real FTLs are far more sophisticated.


"""ftl_sim.py - a tiny page-mapped flash translation layer with greedy
garbage collection. Measures write amplification for random overwrites at
different over-provisioning levels. Teaching model only: real FTLs are far
more sophisticated (and implementation-specific). Standard library only."""
import random

PAGES_PER_BLOCK = 64
BLOCKS = 128                      # physical erase blocks in our toy flash


class ToyFTL:
    def __init__(self, op_fraction):
        phys_pages = PAGES_PER_BLOCK * BLOCKS
        # user-visible logical pages = physical pages minus the spare share
        self.logical_pages = int(phys_pages * (1 - op_fraction))
        self.l2p = {}                                   # logical page -> block
        self.valid = [set() for _ in range(BLOCKS)]     # valid logical pages per block
        self.free_blocks = list(range(1, BLOCKS))       # erased blocks
        self.open_block, self.next_page = 0, 0          # where the next write goes
        self.host_writes = self.nand_writes = 0
        self.erases = [0] * BLOCKS

    def _append(self, lpn):
        """Program one page at the write point (flash is never overwritten in place)."""
        if self.next_page == PAGES_PER_BLOCK:
            self.open_block, self.next_page = self.free_blocks.pop(0), 0
        old = self.l2p.get(lpn)
        if old is not None:
            self.valid[old].discard(lpn)                # the old copy becomes stale
        self.l2p[lpn] = self.open_block
        self.valid[self.open_block].add(lpn)
        self.next_page += 1
        self.nand_writes += 1

    def _garbage_collect(self):
        """Greedy GC: reclaim the closed block holding the fewest valid pages."""
        closed = [b for b in range(BLOCKS)
                  if b != self.open_block and b not in self.free_blocks]
        victim = min(closed, key=lambda b: len(self.valid[b]))
        for lpn in list(self.valid[victim]):            # copy still-valid data out
            self._append(lpn)
        self.erases[victim] += 1                        # erase the whole block
        self.free_blocks.append(victim)

    def host_write(self, lpn):
        while len(self.free_blocks) < 2:                # keep a spare block for GC
            self._garbage_collect()
        self.host_writes += 1
        self._append(lpn)


def run(op_fraction, seed=1):
    ftl = ToyFTL(op_fraction)
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    for lpn in range(ftl.logical_pages):                # fill the drive once
        ftl.host_write(lpn)
    ftl.host_writes = ftl.nand_writes = 0               # then measure steady state
    for _ in range(ftl.logical_pages * 4):              # 4 drive-fulls of random overwrites
        ftl.host_write(rng.randrange(ftl.logical_pages))
    return ftl.nand_writes / ftl.host_writes, max(ftl.erases), min(ftl.erases)


print("spare (OP)   write amplification   erases per block max/min")
for op in (0.07, 0.15, 0.28):
    waf, emax, emin = run(op)
    print(f"   {op:>4.0%}            {waf:5.2f}                  {emax}/{emin}")

Output with Python 3 (the random sequence is seeded, so the numbers are reproducible on current CPython versions):

     7%             8.08                  36/24
    15%             3.59                  14/10
    28%             1.96                  7/4

With little spare space, garbage collection finds victims that are still mostly valid, so it copies a lot: each host write causes about eight flash writes. Quadrupling the spare cuts that by a factor of four. Real drives see lower numbers than this toy (they have far more blocks, smarter victim selection, and separate hot and cold data), but the trend is the same. The spread between maximum and minimum erases per block shows why wear leveling is needed even with random writes.

Latency arithmetic

def hdd_random_ms(rpm, seek_ms, xfer_ms=0.05):
    rot_ms = 0.5 * 60_000 / rpm          # half a revolution, in ms
    return seek_ms + rot_ms + xfer_ms

def iops(queue_depth, latency_s):        # Little's Law: L = lambda * W
    return queue_depth / latency_s

def throughput_mb_s(iops_value, block_bytes):
    return iops_value * block_bytes / 1e6

def tbw(dwpd, capacity_tb, years):
    return dwpd * capacity_tb * 365 * years

t = hdd_random_ms(7200, 8.0)

      f"{throughput_mb_s(iops(32, 80e-6), 4096):,.0f} MB/s at 4 KiB")

Worked example

A database needs 50,000 random 4 KiB reads per second with a median latency under 1 ms. Compare three ways to provide it.

Option 1: hard disks

One 7,200 RPM disk: about 12.2 ms per random read, so about 82 IOPS at queue depth 1. Deeper queues let the disk reorder seeks; assume an optimistic 150 IOPS per disk. Disks needed: 50,000 / 150 ≈ 334, and the median latency per request is still around 10 ms, ten times over budget. HDDs cannot meet this requirement at any count.

Option 2: one SATA SSD

Assume 100 µs per read inside the drive and stack. Little's Law: outstanding commands needed L = λ × W = 50,000 × 0.0001 = 5. NCQ allows 32, so queue depth is fine. Bandwidth: 50,000 × 4096 = 205 MB/s, about a third of the SATA link. Feasible, but at 150,000 IOPS (the next growth step) the link would need 614 MB/s: over the ≈ 600 MB/s ceiling.

Option 3: one NVMe SSD on the reference machine

Assume 80 µs. L = 50,000 × 0.00008 = 4 outstanding commands. Bandwidth 205 MB/s is under 3% of the Gen4 x4 link. Headroom: with 32 dies, the drive could sustain several hundred thousand IOPS at similar latency if the host keeps enough commands in flight, which NVMe's many deep queues allow.

When a storage engineer says "the drive is slow", the next question must be: slow at what percentile, for what pattern, at what queue depth, at what fill level and temperature? Each answer removes whole classes of cause.

Senior engineer insight

An HDD's performance today is roughly its performance yesterday. An SSD's performance depends on everything written to it before: fill level, fragmentation of valid data across blocks, how much was deallocated, how worn it is. A fresh-out-of-box benchmark and a benchmark after a week of production writes can differ by several times. Seniors always ask for the preconditioning state before believing a number, and validation teams define it precisely.

A protocol trace gives you excellent timing of what crossed the link, and nothing about what the controller did between. When a gap is inside the device, the next evidence comes from the device: its health log, vendor telemetry, or controlled experiments. Do not over-read a trace.

The headline of SSD design is not "flash is fast". A single die is slow by memory standards. The product is parallelism: dozens of slow units made to look like one fast one. Everything about NVMe queueing follows from feeding that parallelism.

Challenge

Modify the model's victim selection to "oldest block first" (first in, first out) instead of greedy. Compare WAF. Then explain why real FTLs use cost-benefit policies that consider both valid count and data age.

Hint for question 1

Think about how many valid pages the best victim block has, on average, when free space is very scarce versus moderately scarce.

Hint for the challenge

FIFO ignores how many valid pages a block has; greedy ignores whether the remaining valid data is likely to be overwritten soon.

Solution

Q1. With little spare space, almost every block is nearly full of valid data, so the best victim still has many valid pages to copy; each reclaimed block frees only a few pages, and copying dominates. As spare grows, victims are emptier and each GC frees more space per copy. The relationship is strongly non-linear: the first extra percent of spare is worth far more than the last.

Q2. In this simple greedy model, hot and cold data get mixed in the same blocks, so skew can make WAF worse or only slightly better depending on parameters. Real FTLs separate hot and cold data into different blocks (so hot blocks become fully stale quickly and cold blocks are rarely touched), which can lower WAF substantially on skewed workloads.

Q3. Typically IOPS grows almost linearly at low depths, then flattens somewhere between 16 and 64 as the drive's parallel units saturate; p99 rises sharply at the knee because commands queue inside the device. Exact numbers depend on the drive.

Q4. It should hold closely for a steady-state run, because Little's Law is a mathematical identity for stable systems. Differences usually come from the effective queue depth being lower than requested (the CPU or the submission path cannot keep the queue full), or from mixing up latency definitions (submission-to-completion versus total).

Challenge. FIFO usually produces higher WAF than greedy on uniform random writes, since it picks victims regardless of how much valid data they hold. Cost-benefit policies prefer blocks with few valid pages and old (cold) data, because cold valid data is unlikely to be invalidated soon, so moving it once is worth it.

What a senior engineer notices

That the model measured steady state only after filling the drive. Real SSD benchmarks must do the same (precondition), or they measure an empty drive that will never exist in production. And that the Part B latency distribution, not the mean, is what an application feels.

Failure variants

  • fio reports permission denied: run with sudo, or point it at a file on the drive's filesystem instead of the raw device.
  • IOPS identical at every queue depth: the ioengine may be synchronous; use libaio or io_uring with --direct=1.
  • Unrealistically high IOPS: without --direct=1 you may be measuring the page cache, not the drive.
  • The model runs slowly: reduce BLOCKS; the trend survives.

Extension

Add a deallocate operation to the model (remove a logical page from l2p and its block's valid set) and issue it for 10% of logical pages before the overwrite phase. Measure how much WAF improves. This is what NVMe's Deallocate does for real drives (Chapter 35).

Answers

1. One revolution = 60 / 15000 = 4 ms; average rotational latency = 2 ms.

2. 8 × 4 × 2 = 64 planes; each delivers 16,384 B per 60 µs ≈ 273 MB/s; total ≈ 17.5 GB/s. Channel transfer rates and the controller would limit this well below in practice.

3. 400,000 × 4096 ≈ 1,638 MB/s. No: SATA tops out at about 600 MB/s.

4. 32 / 10 = 3.2.

5. DWPD = TBW / (C × 365 × Y) = 14,016 / (7.68 × 365 × 5) = 14,016 / 14,016 = 1.0.

6. The die holding the data may be busy with an erase or program (often for garbage collection), or the read may need retries. Those happen inside the device and are invisible on the link.

Quiz

  1. What is the unit of erasure in NAND flash?

    • The bit
    • The page
    • The block
    • The die
    Answer and reasoning

    Erase works on whole blocks of many pages. Reads and programs work on pages. This mismatch is why the FTL and garbage collection exist.

  2. Which component maps logical block addresses to physical flash locations?

    • The NVMe driver
    • The flash translation layer
    • The IOMMU
    • The PCIe root complex
    Answer and reasoning

    The FTL in the drive's firmware owns the logical-to-physical map. The host never sees physical flash addresses.

  3. Write amplification factor is defined as:

    • Flash bytes written ÷ host bytes written
    • Host bytes written ÷ flash bytes written
    • Erases ÷ writes
    • IOPS ÷ throughput
    Answer and reasoning

    It measures how many flash writes each host write causes; 1.0 is ideal.

  4. Increasing over-provisioning typically:

    • Increases write amplification
    • Has no effect on endurance
    • Lowers read latency at queue depth 1 dramatically
    • Lowers write amplification and improves sustained write performance
    Answer and reasoning

    More spare means GC victims hold fewer valid pages, so less copying is needed.

  5. At queue depth 8 with 100 µs average latency, Little's Law predicts:

    • 8,000 IOPS
    • 80,000 IOPS
    • 800,000 IOPS
    • 10,000 IOPS
    Answer and reasoning

    λ = L / W = 8 / 0.0001 = 80,000.

  6. SATA's Native Command Queuing allows how many outstanding commands?

    • 1
    • 8
    • 32
    • 65,536
    Answer and reasoning

    32, in a single queue. NVMe allows up to 65,535 I/O queues of up to 65,536 entries each.

  7. In the slow-read trace, which observation shows the host and link are not the cause?

    • The command fetch happened as quickly as in the normal trace
    • The completion status was success
    • The data arrived intact
    • MSI-X was delivered
    Answer and reasoning

    A prompt fetch proves the doorbell reached the controller and the link carried the command normally. The delay begins after that, inside the device. (Success and intact data show it is not an error, but not where the time went.)

  8. Open question: why are SSD latency figures meaningless without a percentile?

    Model answer

    SSD latency distributions have long tails caused by garbage collection, read retries and internal contention. The mean can look excellent while one request in a thousand takes milliseconds, and applications that wait on many requests feel the tail.

  9. Open question: explain why an SSD's internal parallelism required a new host interface.

    Model answer

    Dozens of dies and planes can work at once only if enough requests are outstanding to keep them busy, and requests must be cheap enough to issue at hundreds of thousands per second from many CPUs. SATA/AHCI offered one queue of 32 commands, a 600 MB/s link and microseconds of register overhead per command; NVMe over PCIe offers many deep per-core queues, low per-command overhead and a link that scales with lanes and generations.

  10. Open question: a successful Write completion has been received. Is the data guaranteed to survive a power cut?

    Model answer

    Not necessarily. If the drive reports a volatile write cache and lacks power-loss protection, completed data may still be in DRAM. Durability requires a Flush (or writes with forced unit access semantics), or a drive whose cache is protected. Check the Volatile Write Cache feature and the drive's documentation.

Interview questions

Beginner

  1. What is the difference between latency, IOPS and throughput?

    Model answer

    Latency is the time for one request; IOPS is requests completed per second; throughput is bytes per second. Throughput = IOPS × block size, and IOPS = outstanding requests ÷ latency (Little's Law).

  2. Why do SSDs need garbage collection?

    Model answer

    Flash cannot be overwritten in place and is erased in large blocks, so updates go to new pages and leave stale ones behind. GC reclaims blocks by copying any remaining valid pages elsewhere and erasing the block.

Intermediate

  1. What is write amplification and what reduces it?

    Model answer

    The ratio of flash writes to host writes, caused mainly by GC copies. It is reduced by more over-provisioning, host Deallocate/TRIM, sequential or large writes, hot/cold data separation in the FTL, and features that let the host place data (zoned namespaces, flexible data placement).

  2. Why does a SATA SSD top out around 550 MB/s regardless of its flash?

    Model answer

    SATA 6 Gb/s with 8b/10b encoding gives about 600 MB/s of data bandwidth before protocol overhead; that link is the bottleneck.

Senior

  1. A customer reports p99.9 read latency of 8 ms on an NVMe drive during their nightly batch job, while p50 is 90 µs. How do you investigate?

    Model answer

    Characterize first: is the tail tied to the batch job's write volume? Collect per-command latency with timestamps and the concurrent write rate; check drive fill level, whether the host issues Deallocate, temperature and throttling events, and media error counters in the health log. Reproduce with a controlled mixed workload on a preconditioned drive; pause writes to see if the tail vanishes. If GC interference is confirmed, options include more spare capacity, write shaping, I/O prioritization, Deallocate, or drives and features designed for predictable latency. If not, look at read retries on specific LBAs or host-side causes such as interrupt affinity and CPU contention. State confidence at each step.

  2. Why is preconditioning essential for SSD validation, and how would you define it?

    Model answer

    Because SSD performance depends on its write history: a fresh drive has no GC work to do. Preconditioning fills the drive (typically writing the full capacity sequentially, then random writes of the test block size) until performance reaches a steady state, verified by measuring that throughput varies within a set tolerance over successive intervals. Results should report the preconditioning recipe, fill level and steady-state criterion.

Teach-back challenge

Explain "why an SSD needs a flash translation layer" to three audiences.

Model explanations

To a child: "The SSD is made of chalkboards that can only be wiped a whole wall at a time. So when you change something, it writes the new version on a clean board and keeps a map of where everything really is. Every so often it tidies up walls full of old stuff."

To a junior engineer: "NAND pages can only be programmed after their whole block is erased, and erases wear the flash. The FTL writes every update out of place, keeps a logical-to-physical map, garbage-collects blocks full of stale pages by relocating valid ones, levels wear across blocks, and uses spare capacity as working space. The host just sees logical blocks."

To a senior engineer: "The FTL turns random host writes into sequential programs and exploits die-level parallelism, at the cost of write amplification and GC-induced tail latency that scale with spare capacity and workload entropy. None of it is in the NVMe specifications; the host's levers are Deallocate, write patterns, spare capacity and newer placement features. On a trace it shows only as fetch-to-data or data-to-completion gaps."

Chapter summary

Hard disks are mechanical: one arm, milliseconds per random access, queues used for reordering. SATA and SAS were designed for them: SATA with one queue of 32 commands and a ≈ 600 MB/s half-duplex link behind an AHCI host controller; SAS with dual ports and deeper queues behind a host bus adapter. SSDs replace mechanics with NAND flash organized as pages (program unit), blocks (erase unit), planes, dies and channels, giving tens-of-microsecond latency and massive parallelism. Flash's erase-before-write rule requires a flash translation layer with out-of-place updates, garbage collection (causing write amplification and tail latency), wear leveling, over-provisioning and endurance management, none of which the NVMe specifications define. Performance is three linked numbers: latency (with percentiles), IOPS (with block size, pattern and queue depth) and throughput, tied together by Little's Law and B = λ × S. Feeding an SSD's parallelism needs many cheap outstanding commands and a scalable link: the reason NVMe over PCIe exists.

Do not move on until you can:

  • compute HDD random access time from RPM and seek time;
  • state SATA's queue and bandwidth limits and why they constrain SSDs;
  • draw the channel → die → plane → block → page hierarchy and say what each level is the unit of;
  • explain out-of-place updates, garbage collection and write amplification, and compute WAF;
  • compute OP, TBW and DWPD from given numbers;
  • apply Little's Law and B = λ × S to a storage problem;
  • explain from the slow-read trace why the delay is inside the device;
  • complete Lab 2 and explain the shape of your WAF-versus-OP curve.
