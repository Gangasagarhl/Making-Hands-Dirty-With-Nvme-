Chapters 44–47 taught you to detect, classify and correlate errors. This chapter is about what happens next: how a platform contains an uncorrectable error before bad data spreads, how it keeps devices from talking around the IOMMU, who handles the error (firmware or the operating system), how hot-plug and containment interact, and how an NVMe SSD is brought back to service — or deliberately removed.

Why this chapter exists

In a laptop, an uncorrectable PCI Express (PCIe) error usually means a crash or a reboot. In a server running hundreds of virtual machines on dozens of NVMe drives, that is unacceptable: one drive's fatal error must not take down the host, corrupt another tenant's data, or leave the operating system waiting forever for a completion that will never come. The machinery that makes this possible — Downstream Port Containment (DPC), Access Control Services (ACS), firmware-first error handling, hot-plug, and the Linux PCI error recovery framework — is where PCIe errors meet system reliability.

It is also where many of the hardest field bugs live, because the failure crosses layers and owners: the SSD, the root port, platform firmware, the kernel's PCIe port driver and the NVMe driver all act in turn, often within a few milliseconds, and each logs a different piece of the story.

Learning objectives

  • Explain error containment and why simply reporting an uncorrectable error is not enough.
  • Describe DPC: triggers, what the port does to the link, how outstanding requests are completed, and how software releases containment. Explain what the root-port extensions (often called enhanced DPC, eDPC) add.
  • Explain ACS and its controls, and how ACS relates to IOMMU groups, peer-to-peer traffic and isolation.
  • Compare firmware-first and OS-first error handling, including ACPI _OSC negotiation, APEI/GHES, CPER records and Error Disconnect Recover (EDR).
  • Explain how hot-plug (planned and surprise removal) interacts with DPC and AER.
  • Walk through the Linux PCI error recovery callbacks and how the NVMe driver uses them.
  • Read a containment event in a synthetic trace and in kernel logs, and identify the first abnormal event.

The story

Imagine a water park with many slides, all fed from one big pipe. One day a slide cracks and starts leaking dirty water. If nobody does anything, the dirty water could flow back into the pipe and into the other slides.

So each slide has a valve at the top. The moment a lifeguard sees a crack, they close that slide's valve. The other slides keep running. Then a repair crew fixes the slide, tests it, and opens the valve again. If they cannot fix it, the slide stays closed and the park tells everyone to use another one. And there are also fences between slides, so children cannot cut across from one slide to another without passing the lifeguard.

In a building, fire doors close automatically when a sensor detects smoke in one section, so the fire cannot spread down the corridor. The building management system logs the alarm; the fire service decides when the doors may reopen. Some buildings call the fire service directly from the alarm panel (the panel handles it first); others let the building manager decide (the manager handles it first). Either way, someone must decide, and the doors must stay closed until they do.

What it hides: containment in PCIe is not only "stop new traffic." Requests already in flight toward the contained device must still be answered, or the CPU will hang waiting. DPC answers them with synthesized error completions — fire doors do not reply to anyone.

Where it misleads: the "fences" (ACS) are not about errors at all in the first place; they are routing rules that force peer-to-peer traffic through the root complex where the IOMMU can check it. Their error-handling benefit is a consequence of isolation, not their primary purpose. And in PCIe the "repair crew" (the driver) often brings the device back within a second, which is very unlike a cracked slide.

Beginner explanation

From Chapter 45 you know PCIe errors are correctable (fixed by hardware, logged only), uncorrectable non-fatal (a transaction failed but the link is fine) or uncorrectable fatal (the link or device can no longer be trusted). Advanced Error Reporting (AER) records them and sends ERR_COR, ERR_NONFATAL or ERR_FATAL messages toward the root complex.

Reporting is not the same as stopping damage. Between the moment an SSD sends a corrupted packet and the moment software reacts (microseconds to milliseconds later), more packets can flow. Some of those might carry corrupted data into memory, or the CPU might issue reads that will never complete. Containment means: as soon as hardware detects a serious error, it blocks the path so nothing more crosses, answers anything outstanding with an error, and waits for software.

The main PCIe containment mechanism is Downstream Port Containment (DPC). A downstream port (a root port or a switch downstream port) with DPC enabled reacts to an uncorrectable error from below by taking its link down and holding it down. Everything beneath that port is cut off; everything else in the system keeps running. Software then logs what happened, resets and reinitializes the device, and releases the containment.

Three more ideas complete the picture:

  • Who handles the error: either the operating system directly (OS-first or "native") or platform firmware first, which then tells the OS (firmware-first).
  • Isolation: Access Control Services (ACS) in switches and root ports force traffic between devices to go up through the IOMMU, so one device (or a VM controlling it) cannot reach another directly.
  • Hot-plug: a device being removed looks, electrically, a lot like a fatal error. The platform must tell the difference, or at least handle both safely.

Engineering explanation

Why containment is needed

Consider an NVMe SSD whose controller has a firmware bug that sends a malformed TLP. Without containment:

  • The root port logs a Malformed TLP (uncorrectable, fatal by default) and sends an ERR_FATAL indication to the root complex.
  • Meanwhile, the SSD may continue to DMA data (possibly corrupted) into host memory and write completion queue entries saying "success."
  • The CPU may have MMIO reads outstanding to the SSD's BAR (for example the driver reading CSTS). If the SSD stops responding, those reads wait for a Completion Timeout — and on some platforms a timed-out CPU read is itself escalated to a machine check.
  • If the platform's response to ERR_FATAL is a system-wide reset, every other device and VM is lost.

Containment addresses the second and third points directly and makes the fourth unnecessary.

Downstream Port Containment (DPC)

DPC is an optional extended capability of downstream ports, added to the PCI Express Base Specification by an engineering change notice in the 3.x era and part of the base specification since. When triggered, the port:

  1. Sets DPC Trigger Status and records the DPC Trigger Reason (and, for some reasons, the DPC Error Source ID — the Requester ID of the function that sent the error message).
  2. Directs its LTSSM to the Disabled state, taking the link down, and keeps it there while Trigger Status is set.
  3. Discards TLPs it would otherwise forward downstream, and stops accepting TLPs from below.
  4. Completes outstanding and new non-posted requests that target the contained hierarchy with an error completion, either Unsupported Request (UR) or Completer Abort (CA), as selected by the DPC Completion Control setting, so requesters do not hang.
  5. Optionally signals a DPC interrupt (MSI/MSI-X) or ERR_COR to software.

Software investigates, then clears DPC Trigger Status (a write-1-to-clear bit). The port leaves Disabled, the link retrains, and the device below can be reinitialized.

Root-port extensions ("eDPC")

The term eDPC (enhanced DPC) is widely used for the Root Port Extensions for DPC. They add RP PIO (Root Port Programmed I/O) error handling: when a CPU load or store to the contained hierarchy fails (a completion timeout, UR or CA on a CPU-originated request), the root port can log it in RP PIO status, header log and related registers and trigger DPC, and can synthesize a completion so the CPU does not stall. Without these extensions, a CPU read that times out may surface as an uncontained machine check on some platforms. The extensions also report "DPC RP Busy" while the port is still completing its containment work, so software knows when it is safe to release. Implementation-dependent: what the CPU actually receives for a synthesized error completion on a read (typically all-ones data) and whether a machine check is raised are platform behaviours.

When a device is contained, removed, or dead, CPU reads to its BAR return all ones: 0xFFFFFFFF. The NVMe driver reading CSTS sees 0xFFFFFFFF — CSTS.RDY = 1 and CSTS.CFS = 1 at the same time, which is nonsense for a live controller. Linux treats CSTS = all-ones as "controller is gone." Learn to spot 0xFFFFFFFF in any register dump as "the path to the device is broken," not as a register value.

Poisoned TLPs and egress blocking

A TLP with the EP (poisoned) bit set carries data known to be bad. The receiver logs a Poisoned TLP Received error. By default, the poisoned data can still be forwarded (for example, a switch passes it on). DPC adds an optional Poisoned TLP Egress Blocking feature: the downstream port can refuse to transmit poisoned TLPs to the device below, logging the event, so known-bad data never reaches a device that might not check poison. This is containment of data, not of the link.

Access Control Services (ACS)

ACS is an extended capability of switch downstream ports, root ports and multi-function devices that controls how requests are routed between peers. Its controls, by name, are:

Linux builds IOMMU groups from this information: devices that can reach each other without going through the IOMMU must be in the same group, and a group is the smallest unit that can be assigned to a VM. A switch without ACS puts all devices below it into one group. A request that violates an enabled ACS rule is blocked and logged as an ACS Violation, an uncorrectable error reported through AER, which may in turn trigger DPC.

ACS and peer-to-peer DMA pull in opposite directions. NVMe peer-to-peer (for example, a GPU or NIC reading directly from an SSD's Controller Memory Buffer) is fastest with redirect disabled; secure multi-tenant isolation needs it enabled. This is a policy decision with security consequences, not a tuning knob. Kernel parameters that override ACS checks to split IOMMU groups remove isolation and should never be used where tenants do not trust each other.

If AER or DPC is missing from that list, the OS is not handling those errors natively and you must look for firmware-first logs (lines beginning with "[Hardware Error]" from the GHES driver, and the BMC's event log).

Hot-plug interactions

PCIe hot-plug lets a device be added or removed while the system runs. Two kinds of removal matter:

  • Orderly removal: the operator requests removal (attention button, management command or software), the OS unbinds the driver, powers down the slot, then the device is pulled. No errors occur.
  • Surprise removal: the device is pulled, or fails, with no warning. The link goes down. The downstream port detects a Surprise Down uncorrectable error (if it reports it) and the hot-plug controller sees Presence Detect Changed and/or Data Link Layer State Changed.

Surprise Down is also a DPC trigger. On a port with both hot-plug and DPC, a surprise removal can therefore raise two responses: the hot-plug driver wants to remove the device's software state, while the DPC driver wants to recover it. Platforms and kernels coordinate these: a common approach is that the hot-plug driver ignores link-down/link-up events that DPC caused and recovered from, and handles removal only when the device is really gone (presence lost or recovery fails). Some platforms mask Surprise Down for DPC on hot-plug-capable slots. Implementation-dependent: Linux behaviour has changed across kernel versions; check your kernel's pciehp and dpc drivers.

For NVMe in hot-plug bays (U.2, U.3, E3.S), the classic confusion is "the drive reset itself" versus "the drive was removed and re-inserted" versus "DPC contained and recovered it." All three produce a link down and up. The distinguishing evidence: presence-detect events (removal), DPC Trigger Status and Reason (containment), and NVMe controller-level resets without any PCIe link event (NVMe reset only).

When the OS handles an uncorrectable error natively, the PCIe port driver runs a recovery procedure across all devices below the affected port, calling each driver's struct pci_error_handlers callbacks:

The NVMe PCIe driver implements these callbacks: on error_detected it quiesces queues and (for a frozen channel) prepares to disable the controller; on slot_reset it restores PCI state and schedules a controller reset (the NVMe reset sequence of Chapter 48: CC.EN = 0, wait CSTS.RDY = 0, reconfigure admin queue, CC.EN = 1, recreate I/O queues); on resume it restarts I/O. Commands that were in flight are retried or failed according to the block layer's policy and the NVMe driver's retry rules. Implementation-dependent on kernel version.

The central concept on the ladder

When a slide cracks, the lifeguard closes its valve at once so dirty water does not spread; the repair crew fixes it and reopens it.

On an uncorrectable error, the downstream port shuts the link, answers waiting requests with errors, and waits for software to recover the device and release the link.

The DPC capability in a root port or switch downstream port: trigger logic tied to AER, LTSSM forced to Disabled, synthesized completions for pending requests, status and source-ID registers, optional RP PIO logging.

The PCI Express Base Specification defines the DPC Extended Capability (control, status, error source ID, RP PIO registers), ACS, AER, Surprise Down and hot-plug; the PCI Firmware Specification and ACPI define _OSC and EDR.

An ERR_FATAL message or a detected error at the port; then the link drops (electrical idle); CPU requests to the hierarchy get UR/CA completions synthesized by the port; later TS1s as the link retrains after release.

An analyzer on the SSD link sees the triggering TLP or message, then the link go to electrical idle with no further DLLPs; minutes or milliseconds later, a full retrain. An analyzer above the port sees nothing unusual except UR completions.

Read DPC Trigger Reason and Source ID, then the AER logs of the source device, then the header log to find the offending TLP; correlate with the NVMe driver's reset messages and I/O errors.

The 16 questions, for DPC

1. What is it?
A downstream-port feature that disables the link to a hierarchy when an uncorrectable error occurs, and holds it down until software releases it.
2. Why was it created?
To stop errors and corrupted data from spreading, and to avoid system-wide resets for one bad device.
3. What problem does it solve?
The window between error detection and software response, during which bad data and hung requests can propagate.
4. What existed before?
AER reporting plus software-initiated secondary bus reset, or a full platform reset; no hardware containment.
5. Why is it better?
It acts in hardware immediately, keeps the rest of the system running, and gives a clean, defined state to recover from.
6. Who creates it?
The downstream port's hardware triggers it; software enables and configures it.
7. Who consumes it?
The OS DPC driver (OS-first) or platform firmware (firmware-first, with EDR to the OS).
8. Where does it live?
In root ports and switch downstream ports, as an extended capability.
9. Registers?
DPC Capability, Control, Status, Error Source ID; RP PIO status, mask, severity, header log (with root-port extensions); related AER registers.
10. Before it?
An uncorrectable error is detected or reported, and passes masking/severity checks.
11. After it?
Software logs, resets the hierarchy's drivers, clears Trigger Status; link retrains; devices reinitialize.
12. If it is wrong?
Too aggressive: unnecessary device outages. Disabled or misconfigured: errors propagate, CPU hangs, platform resets. Poor coordination with hot-plug: devices removed instead of recovered.
13. Analyzer shows?
Triggering TLP/message, then link to electrical idle, later retraining.
14. Linux shows?
DPC containment messages from the port driver, AER logs, NVMe reset or removal messages.
15. Validation tests?
Error injection for each trigger reason, outstanding-request completion behaviour, release and recovery timing, interaction with hot-plug, firmware-first EDR flow.
16. Senior suspects?
The device's first error (from AER header logs), signal integrity behind Surprise Down, and hot-plug/DPC coordination bugs when devices disappear instead of recovering.

Hardware view

  • Blast radius is decided by which port triggers. A platform that only enables DPC at root ports loses the whole switch hierarchy on any error below it.
  • Endpoints do not implement DPC. The SSD's role is only to report errors correctly and to recover cleanly after reset.
  • Synthesized completions are generated by the port itself, with the port as completer; the Requester ID and Tag match the original request so the requester can retire it.
  • Hot-plug slot hardware (presence detect, power controller, attention button, indicators) lives in the downstream port's Slot Capabilities/Control/Status registers, alongside DPC.

Specification view

The relevant documents are the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 — Downstream Port Containment; Access Control Services; Advanced Error Reporting; Error Signaling and Logging; Hot-Plug Support, the PCI Firmware Specification — _OSC and Error Disconnect Recover, the ACPI Specification — Platform Error Interfaces (APEI), and the UEFI Specification — Common Platform Error Record. On the NVMe side, the NVMe Base Specification 2.4 — Controller Reset and Controller Fatal Status and the NVMe over PCIe Transport Specification 1.4 describe how the controller behaves across PCIe resets.

What is verified? DPC is an optional extended capability of downstream ports; when triggered it disables the link and holds it down until software clears DPC Trigger Status; outstanding requests are completed with UR or CA per DPC Completion Control; root-port extensions add RP PIO logging; ACS controls listed above exist and violations are reported as ACS Violation; Surprise Down is an uncorrectable error; _OSC negotiates OS control of AER, DPC and hot-plug; CPER is the firmware-first record format.

What is derived? The blast-radius reasoning, the recovery-flow comparison table, and the diagnostic order (trigger reason → source → header log → device history).

What is implementation-dependent? Which ports implement DPC; which triggers platforms enable; whether Surprise Down is masked on hot-plug slots; firmware-first vs OS-first policy; what a CPU sees for a synthesized error completion (all-ones, machine check, or both); Linux recovery behaviour by kernel version; NVMe driver retry policy.

What is version-dependent? DPC entered via an ECN in the 3.x era; EDR was added to the PCI Firmware Specification later; Linux DPC, EDR and hot-plug coordination evolved over many kernel releases. Flit mode (6.0+) changes error-detection details at the link level but not the DPC concept.

What must be checked in the specification? DPC Trigger Enable and Trigger Reason encodings; RP PIO register layout; timing requirements after clearing Trigger Status before configuration accesses are allowed; ACS capability presence rules for multi-function devices; EDR handshake details.

What the specification does not say: which errors a platform should contain; how an OS should retry I/O; how NVMe namespaces should be presented to applications during recovery.

What should never be assumed? That DPC is enabled because the capability exists; that the OS owns AER (check _OSC); that a device that came back after DPC is healthy; that all-ones data from a register is a real register value; that ACS is enabled in every switch.

Protocol view

Protocol facts to keep in mind:

  • The error message (ERR_FATAL/ERR_NONFATAL) travels upstream as a Message TLP; DPC can trigger on receiving it at the downstream port.
  • After triggering, nothing crosses the contained link. Any requests the CPU sends are answered by the port, not the device.
  • Posted writes from the CPU to the contained device (doorbells) are silently dropped. The driver cannot tell from the write itself that it was lost.
  • Recovery involves configuration requests (restore BARs, command register, MSI-X) before any MMIO, and the NVMe controller reset follows.
  • With firmware-first, the same hardware events occur, but the first software actor is firmware, and the OS acts on an EDR notification or a GHES record.

Code / pseudocode

Containment timeline builder (runnable)

This script turns kernel log lines into a layered timeline and decodes the unmasked AER uncorrectable bits. It is executable with the standard library; the embedded log is synthetic. You can pass a saved dmesg text file instead.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""dpc_timeline.py - build a layered timeline from kernel log lines around a
containment event, decode the AER Uncorrectable Error Status value, and name
the first abnormal event.  The embedded log is SYNTHETIC (teaching example);
line formats imitate recent Linux kernels but vary between versions.
Usage: python3 dpc_timeline.py            (uses the embedded sample)
       python3 dpc_timeline.py dmesg.txt  (your own saved 'dmesg -T' or 'dmesg' text)
"""
import re
import sys

# AER Uncorrectable Error Status bits (PCIe Base Spec, AER capability).
UNCOR_BITS = {
    4: "Data Link Protocol Error", 5: "Surprise Down Error",
    12: "Poisoned TLP Received", 13: "Flow Control Protocol Error",
    14: "Completion Timeout", 15: "Completer Abort",
    16: "Unexpected Completion", 17: "Receiver Overflow",
    18: "Malformed TLP", 19: "ECRC Error", 20: "Unsupported Request",
    21: "ACS Violation", 22: "Uncorrectable Internal Error",
    23: "MC Blocked TLP", 24: "AtomicOp Egress Blocked",
    25: "TLP Prefix Blocked", 26: "Poisoned TLP Egress Blocked",
}

SAMPLE = """\
[ 8811.402113] pcieport 0000:00:01.1: DPC: containment event, status:0x0001 source:0x0000
[ 8811.402120] pcieport 0000:00:01.1: DPC: unmasked uncorrectable error detected
[ 8811.402131] pcieport 0000:00:01.1: PCIe Bus Error: severity=Uncorrected (Fatal), type=Transaction Layer, (Receiver ID)
[ 8811.402135] pcieport 0000:00:01.1:   device [1234:5678] error status/mask=00040000/00000000
[ 8811.402139] pcieport 0000:00:01.1:    [18] MalfTLP                (First)
[ 8811.402150] pcieport 0000:00:01.1: AER:   TLP Header: 60000080 01000fff 00000002 00400000
[ 8811.402203] nvme nvme0: frozen state error detected, reset controller
[ 8811.731880] pcieport 0000:00:01.1: pciehp: Slot(1): Link Down/Up ignored (recovered by DPC)
[ 8811.912447] nvme nvme0: 32/0/0 default/read/poll queues
[ 8811.913002] pcieport 0000:00:01.1: AER: device recovery successful
"""

RULES = [  # (regex, layer, label, abnormal?)
    (r"DPC: containment event", "PCIe/DPC", "link contained by downstream port", True),
    (r"DPC: .*error detected", "PCIe/DPC", "DPC trigger reason", False),
    (r"PCIe Bus Error", "PCIe/AER", "uncorrectable error logged", False),
    (r"error status/mask=", "PCIe/AER", "AER status register", False),
    (r"TLP Header", "PCIe/AER", "header of offending TLP", False),
    (r"Surprise Down|Link Down", "PCIe/hot-plug", "link state change", False),
    (r"nvme nvme\d+: .*(reset|frozen)", "NVMe driver", "controller recovery", False),
    (r"nvme nvme\d+: .*queues", "NVMe driver", "queues recreated", False),
    (r"controller is down", "NVMe driver", "controller unreachable (all-ones)", True),
    (r"recovery successful", "PCIe/AER", "recovery complete", False),
    (r"recovery failed|removing", "PCIe/AER", "device removed", True),
]

def decode_uncor(value):
    return [f"[{b}] {name}" for b, name in UNCOR_BITS.items() if value >> b & 1]

def main():
    text = open(sys.argv[1]).read() if len(sys.argv) > 1 else SAMPLE
    first = None
    print(f"{'time (s)':>12}  {'layer':<14} event")
    for line in text.splitlines():
        m = re.match(r"\[\s*([\d.]+)\]\s*(.*)", line)
        if not m:
            continue
        t, msg = float(m.group(1)), m.group(2)
        for rx, layer, label, abnormal in RULES:
            if re.search(rx, msg):
                print(f"{t:12.6f}  {layer:<14} {label}")
                s = re.search(r"error status/mask=([0-9a-fA-F]+)/([0-9a-fA-F]+)", msg)
                if s:
                    st, mask = int(s.group(1), 16), int(s.group(2), 16)
                    for bit in decode_uncor(st & ~mask):
                        print(f"{'':12}  {'':<14}   unmasked: {bit}")
                if abnormal and first is None:
                    first = (t, label)
                break
    if first:
        print(f"\nFirst abnormal event in the log: t={first[0]:.6f} s, {first[1]}.")
        print("The log shows the containment, not the cause: the cause is the error that")
        print("triggered it (see the AER lines) and whatever happened on the link before.")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Driver error handlers (pseudocode, modelled on the Linux callbacks)

pci_ers_result_t nvme_like_error_detected(struct pci_dev *pdev, pci_channel_state_t state) {
    switch (state) {
    case pci_channel_io_normal:      /* non-fatal: device still reachable */
        return PCI_ERS_RESULT_CAN_RECOVER;
    case pci_channel_io_frozen:      /* fatal or DPC: link down, MMIO returns all-ones */
        stop_submitting_io();
        mark_controller_resetting();  /* do not touch BAR0 now */
        return PCI_ERS_RESULT_NEED_RESET;
    case pci_channel_io_perm_failure:
        fail_all_io();
        return PCI_ERS_RESULT_DISCONNECT;
    }
}

pci_ers_result_t nvme_like_slot_reset(struct pci_dev *pdev) {
    pci_restore_state(pdev);          /* BARs, command register, MSI-X */
    schedule_controller_reset();      /* CC.EN=0 → RDY=0 → AQA/ASQ/ACQ → CC.EN=1 → RDY=1 → queues */
    return PCI_ERS_RESULT_RECOVERED;
}

void nvme_like_resume(struct pci_dev *pdev) {
    wait_for_reset_complete();
    restart_io();                     /* requeued commands are resubmitted */
}

Worked example

Choosing DPC policy for an NVMe storage server

A server has 24 U.2 NVMe SSDs behind three PCIe switches (8 drives each), with root ports above the switches. All switch downstream ports and root ports implement DPC. The question: which ports should enable DPC, with which triggers?

  1. Blast radius. Enabling DPC only at root ports means a fatal error from one drive takes down 8 drives. Enabling DPC at each switch downstream port limits it to 1 drive. → Enable at switch downstream ports.
  2. Trigger choice. Fatal errors from drives (Malformed TLP, Surprise Down, Receiver Overflow) should trigger. Non-fatal errors (e.g. an Unsupported Request on one transaction) are usually recoverable by the driver without dropping the link; triggering DPC on them would turn small errors into outages. → "ERR_FATAL" trigger at drive-facing ports. Derived policy; platforms differ.
  3. Root ports. Keep DPC enabled at root ports too, for errors in the switch itself (its upstream link). Enable root-port extensions so CPU MMIO timeouts are contained instead of escalating.
  4. Hot-plug coordination. These are hot-plug bays. Surprise Down will occur on every unannounced removal; confirm the kernel handles DPC + hot-plug together, or the drive may be "recovered" while physically absent.
  5. Firmware-first? If the operator needs BMC-level logs for fleet analysis, firmware-first with EDR gives central visibility; if the team debugs with kernel logs and analyzers, OS-first keeps full detail. Document whichever is chosen; a mixed configuration (AER native, DPC firmware) is the hardest to debug.
  6. ACS. If drives are passed through to different VMs, ACS redirect must be enabled at switch downstream ports so each drive gets its own IOMMU group. If drives use peer-to-peer DMA to a local accelerator, that path is blocked or redirected — measure the performance cost before deciding.

Recovery time budget

Suppose containment takes < 1 µs, the OS reacts in 1 ms, the kernel waits for the link after release and for the device to become ready (often hundreds of milliseconds), and the NVMe controller reset takes 1–2 s for a drive with a large mapping table. Total ≈ 2 s. If the I/O timeout in the stack above (for example, a distributed storage heartbeat) is shorter than that, the node will be declared failed even though recovery works. Derived; measure on your platform.

Common mistakes

  • Blaming the port that logged the error. The root port logs errors it detects; the source may be the device below (check Error Source ID and header log).
  • Reading all-ones as data. 0xFFFFFFFF from a BAR means the path is broken.
  • Assuming DPC is on. The capability may exist with triggers disabled.
  • Enabling DPC for non-fatal errors everywhere, turning recoverable errors into device outages.
  • Forgetting that posted writes are silently dropped during containment — doorbells written in that window are lost and the driver must rebuild state.
  • Using ACS override to "fix" IOMMU groups in multi-tenant systems, removing isolation.
  • Ignoring _OSC. Searching kernel logs for AER details on a firmware-first platform, finding nothing, and concluding there was no error.
  • Treating successful recovery as root cause closure. Recovery hides the bug; the first error still needs an owner.

Senior engineer insight

  • Containment converts an unknown-state problem into a known-state problem. Its value is less the stopping of traffic than the guarantee that the system is in a defined, recoverable state.
  • The first error is the only one that matters for root cause. Everything after containment is the platform behaving correctly. Teams lose weeks analysing recovery noise.
  • Every recovery path needs a test. Error injection per trigger reason, with I/O running, on every supported kernel and firmware combination. Recovery code that is never exercised does not work.
  • Firmware-first is a contract, not a black box. Insist that CPER records include the AER header log and that firmware versioning is tracked with every incident.
  • Isolation is a security property. ACS decisions belong in a threat model, not in a performance tuning guide.
  • Recovered is not healthy. After any containment event, check the device's NVMe logs (error log, SMART, telemetry) before returning it to service.

Challenge

Extend dpc_timeline.py to recognise "[Hardware Error]" CPER lines and extract aer_uncor_status, decoding it with the same bit table.

Hints

DW0 0x60000080: Fmt = 011b (4 DW header with data), Type = 00000b (memory request) → MWr64; Length field = 0x080 = 128 DW. DW1: Requester ID 0x0100, Tag 0x0F, byte enables FFh. DW2–DW3: 64-bit address.

Solution

Step 2: MWr64 from 0x0100 (Lab SSD), Tag 0x0F, 128 DW = 512 bytes to address 0x2_0040_0000 (the sample's DW3; the trace used the next 256-byte block). 512 > MPS 256 → Malformed TLP.

Step 3 (model): "OS-first (AER and DPC granted by _OSC). Contained at root port 00:01.1 (the only port above the SSD). Trigger: unmasked uncorrectable error detected by the port. First error: Malformed TLP, header shows a 512-byte MWr from 01:00.0 while MPS is 256. Offending component: SSD firmware. Recovery: successful in ≈ 0.5 s, controller reset, queues recreated. Corrective action: SSD firmware must honour Device Control MPS. Regression: run I/O at MPS 128 and 256 with an analyzer checking MWr lengths, plus a DPC recovery soak."

Q1: the containment message is the platform's reaction; the cause is the earlier Malformed TLP, shown in the AER lines that follow it in the log (log order is not event order).

Q2: the link went down because DPC disabled it and came back when DPC released it; the hot-plug driver recognises this and does not treat it as a removal.

Q3: the native DPC/AER lines would be replaced by "[Hardware Error]" CPER lines from the GHES driver (and possibly EDR-related messages); the header log may or may not be included.

Q4: all eight SSDs behind the switch (and the switch) would be cut off and recovered, not just the faulty one.

What a senior engineer notices

That the log's order is not the event order (DPC printed before AER though AER detection came first); that the recovery time depends mostly on device readiness, not on the platform; and that the SSD's own logs have not yet been examined.

Failure variants

  • Recovery reports "failed" and the device is removed: look for link training failure after release, or the device not responding to configuration reads in time.
  • CSTS=0xffffffff with no DPC message: the device may have dropped off the bus without a reported error (power loss, firmware crash with link down while DPC disabled).
  • DPC events with Completion Timeout on a hot-plug slot after removal: expected; confirm presence detect.

Answers

1. A CPU load hangs waiting for a completion (or causes a machine check on timeout); corrupted DMA data reaches memory used by the OS or applications; a platform-wide reset is triggered by an unhandled fatal error.

2. The requester matches completions to its outstanding requests by Requester ID and Tag; without them, it could not retire the request and would time out anyway.

3. The switch does not provide (or has not enabled) ACS redirect between those downstream ports, so the kernel assumes they can communicate peer-to-peer without IOMMU checks.

4. Evidence moves from AER/DPC registers and kernel lines to CPER records, BMC event logs and firmware logs; the OS may have only a summary.

5. To let hot-plug handle removals without a containment event racing it. Risk: a real link failure (not a removal) is then not contained by DPC.

Quiz

  1. What does a downstream port do to its link when DPC triggers?

    • Retrains it at a lower speed
    • Disables it and holds it down until software clears Trigger Status
    • Sends a hot reset to the device
    • Nothing; DPC only logs
    Answer and reasoning

    DPC directs the LTSSM to Disabled and keeps the link down while DPC Trigger Status is set.

  2. How are outstanding CPU reads to a contained device completed?

    • They time out normally
    • The device completes them after recovery
    • The port synthesizes UR or CA completions, per DPC Completion Control
    Answer and reasoning

    DPC answers requests targeting the contained hierarchy so requesters do not hang; the CPU typically sees all-ones data.

  3. Which ACS control forces peer-to-peer requests between switch downstream ports to go upstream?

    • Source Validation
    • P2P Request Redirect
    • Translation Blocking
    • Direct Translated P2P
    Answer and reasoning

    Request Redirect (with Upstream Forwarding) sends peer requests to the root complex where the IOMMU checks them.

  4. Which ACPI mechanism lets the OS take native control of AER and DPC?

    • The _OSC method
    • The HEST table
    • The MCFG table
    • The _PRT method
    Answer and reasoning

    _OSC negotiates control; HEST describes firmware-first error sources.

  5. The NVMe driver reads CSTS = 0xFFFFFFFF. What is the most accurate conclusion?

    Model answer

    The driver cannot reach the controller: it is contained, removed, powered off or its link is down. It does not prove the controller itself failed. Check DPC/AER/hot-plug logs.

  6. Which Linux callback reinitializes the device after the link has been reset or DPC released?

    • error_detected()
    • mmio_enabled()
    • slot_reset()
    • resume()
    Answer and reasoning

    slot_reset() is called after the reset; resume() restarts I/O afterwards.

  7. What do the root-port extensions for DPC add?

    Model answer

    RP PIO error handling: logging and containment of failures on CPU-originated requests (e.g. completion timeouts on MMIO), with status, mask, severity and header log registers, plus a busy indicator for safe release.

  8. A drive in a hot-plug bay shows link down, Surprise Down, presence still asserted, then successful recovery. Was it removed?

    • Yes, removal always causes Surprise Down
    • Probably not: presence stayed asserted; suspect power or contact
    • Cannot have happened: Surprise Down is correctable
    Answer and reasoning

    Presence detect still asserted means the drive was in the slot. Surprise Down is uncorrectable, and with presence asserted points to the link or the device's power.

  9. Why should DPC usually trigger on ERR_FATAL but not ERR_NONFATAL at drive-facing ports?

    Model answer

    Non-fatal errors affect individual transactions while the link remains reliable; drivers can recover without taking the link down. Triggering DPC on them converts recoverable errors into device outages. Fatal errors mean the link or device cannot be trusted, so containment is appropriate.

Interview questions

  1. Beginner: What is the difference between reporting and containing an error?

    Model answer

    Reporting (AER) records the error and notifies software. Containment (DPC) stops further traffic across the affected link immediately in hardware and answers pending requests, so damage cannot spread while software reacts.

  2. Beginner: What is an IOMMU group and what decides it?

    Model answer

    The smallest set of devices the kernel can isolate from each other; devices that can communicate without passing through the IOMMU share a group. ACS capabilities in switches and root ports (and multi-function devices) decide it.

  3. Intermediate: Walk through what happens from an SSD sending a malformed TLP to I/O resuming.

    Model answer

    Root port detects Malformed TLP, logs AER, triggers DPC; link disabled; CPU accesses get synthesized error completions; DPC interrupt; OS calls error_detected() on the NVMe driver (frozen); OS clears DPC Trigger Status after logging; link retrains; slot_reset() restores config and resets the NVMe controller; resume() restarts I/O; requeued commands are retried.

  4. Intermediate: Firmware-first vs OS-first: pros and cons?

    Model answer

    Firmware-first: central platform policy, BMC logging, consistency across OSes; but added latency, less detail to the OS, and firmware bugs are harder to see. OS-first: fast, detailed, debuggable; but less centralized visibility. EDR bridges firmware-first containment with OS-driven device recovery.

  5. Advanced: How do DPC and hot-plug interact and what can go wrong?

    Model answer

    Surprise removal triggers both Surprise Down (DPC) and hot-plug link/presence events. The hot-plug driver must not remove a device that DPC is recovering, and DPC must not keep trying to recover a device that is gone. Races lead to devices removed unnecessarily or stale devices left behind. Kernels coordinate by having pciehp ignore DPC-caused link changes and by checking presence.

  6. Advanced: Why are synthesized completions necessary?

    Model answer

    Without them, requests already issued to the contained hierarchy would wait for Completion Timeout, during which CPUs can stall, and some platforms escalate timeouts to machine checks. Synthesized UR/CA completions retire them promptly.

  7. Senior: Design the error-handling policy for a multi-tenant NVMe server.

    Model answer

    Enable DPC at the closest port above each drive with ERR_FATAL triggers; root-port extensions for MMIO timeouts; ACS redirect for per-drive IOMMU groups (accept P2P cost or use trusted P2P only within a tenant); choose OS-first or firmware-first explicitly and ensure CPER includes header logs if firmware-first; coordinate DPC with hot-plug; set upper-layer timeouts above worst-case recovery time; test each path with injection; after any recovery, check device logs before returning it to service.

  8. Senior: A fleet reports "NVMe drive disappeared" after rare DPC events on one kernel version only. How do you approach it?

    Model answer

    Collect logs from affected and unaffected kernels and compare message order (dpc, pciehp, nvme). Suspect a regression in DPC/hot-plug coordination or device readiness waiting after release. Reproduce on a test system with software-triggered DPC (if supported) or injection, bisect kernel changes, and in parallel examine the root cause of the DPC triggers themselves from AER header logs.

Chapter summary

Reporting an uncorrectable error is not enough; it must be contained. DPC in root ports and switch downstream ports disables the link on configured triggers, completes pending requests with UR or CA, and waits for software; root-port extensions extend containment to CPU-initiated failures. Poisoned TLP egress blocking contains bad data. ACS forces peer traffic through the IOMMU, defining IOMMU groups and isolation. Error handling is negotiated between firmware and OS through _OSC; firmware-first uses APEI/GHES/CPER and EDR. Hot-plug and DPC must be coordinated because removal looks like a fatal error. Linux recovers devices with error_detected, mmio_enabled, slot_reset and resume, and the NVMe driver turns slot_reset into a controller reset. The first error, not the recovery, holds the root cause.

  • distinguish removal, containment and an NVMe-only reset from evidence
  • map the Linux recovery callbacks to the NVMe reset sequence
