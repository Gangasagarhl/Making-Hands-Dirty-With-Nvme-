Six small calculators for the arithmetic every storage engineer does daily: Peripheral Component Interconnect Express (PCIe) bandwidth from first principles, Input/Output Operations Per Second (IOPS) and throughput, Little's law, an end-to-end latency budget, SSD endurance, and latency percentiles. Each one shows its formula, its assumptions, and how to tell a measurement from a fantasy.

Why these calculators exist

Most performance arguments are settled by arithmetic that nobody wrote down. "The drive is slow" turns out to be a Gen3 link; "the link is saturated" turns out to be a queue depth of 1; "we need more IOPS" turns out to be a latency problem. Master-prompt sections 39–42 and the mathematics part ask for exactly these tools, and for every formula to be explained in plain English. The rule for this page is the rule of the course: never present a theoretical number as guaranteed application throughput. Every result below is a ceiling or an estimate, labelled as such.

PCIe bandwidth calculator

Bandwidth is peeled off in layers. Start with the raw signalling rate, remove line-encoding overhead, remove per-packet overhead, remove link-maintenance traffic. What is left is the most payload the link can carry in one direction. The device behind it may deliver much less.

Beff = (R × N × e ÷ 8) × f × P ÷ (P + O) × (1 − d)
R
signalling rate per lane in GT/s (2.5, 5, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128 for Gen1–Gen7)
N
number of lanes (link width)
e
encoding efficiency: 8/10 for Gen1–2, 128/130 for Gen3–5, 1 for the PAM4 Flit Mode generations
f
flit efficiency in Flit Mode (taken as 236/256: TLP bytes per 256-byte flit); 1 otherwise
P
payload bytes per TLP (at most Max Payload Size)
O
per-TLP overhead: header (12 or 16 bytes) + 4 bytes framing/sequence + 4 bytes LCRC (+ 4 if ECRC) in non-Flit mode; header only in Flit Mode
d
fraction of link time used by DLLPs (ACK/NAK, flow-control updates) and ordered sets — an assumption you choose

Reading it

The defaults are the lab system: Gen4 x4, 256-byte payloads, MWr64 (the TLPs that carry NVMe Read data to the host). The answer is about 7.0 GB/s per direction. A Gen4 x4 SSD advertising "7,000 MB/s" sequential reads is therefore claiming to run the link at its practical ceiling; a datasheet claiming 7,500 MB/s would deserve a question about units or test conditions.

Change the payload to 128 bytes and watch TLP efficiency fall from about 91 % to 84 %: Max Payload Size is not a cosmetic setting. Switch to CplD to see NVMe Write data, which reaches the device as completions to the device's reads; their 12-byte header makes them slightly more efficient per byte, but the reads themselves (MRd TLPs) also consume bandwidth in the other direction.

The Flit Mode figures (Gen6, Gen7) use a simplified model: 236 of every 256 flit bytes carry TLPs, and TLPs have no per-packet framing or LCRC. That is enough to compare generations, not to predict a specific device. Confirm flit layout and header sizes in the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 — Flit Mode. Approximation

What this calculator cannot know: the SSD controller's internal bandwidth, NAND parallelism, the root complex's ability to sink writes, IOMMU translation misses, other devices behind the same switch, and the fact that reads also generate traffic in the opposite direction. "Device limitation" is very often the real answer; see the bandwidth lab below.

IOPS ↔ throughput

T = IOPS × S
T
throughput in bytes per second
IOPS
completed I/O operations per second
S
bytes per operation (block size of the workload)

One million 4 KiB IOPS is 4.1 GB/s: within a Gen4 x4 link's 7 GB/s, so a "1M IOPS" 4 KiB random-read claim is not link-limited. The same link at 7,000 MB/s of 128 KiB reads is only about 53,000 IOPS. IOPS and throughput are the same quantity seen through block size; quoting one without the block size is meaningless.

Little's law

L = λ × W
L
average number of commands outstanding (actually in flight, not the configured maximum queue depth)
λ
throughput in commands per second (IOPS)
W
average time each command spends in the system, in seconds (the calculator takes microseconds)

Little's law in plain English: if commands arrive and leave at a steady rate, the number inside equals the rate times how long each stays. It holds for any stable system regardless of distribution, which is what makes it so useful. For storage it answers the most common capacity question: how many commands must I keep in flight to get the IOPS I want, given the latency I have?

Fill in any two; leave the third empty.

Storage example

A drive delivers 80 µs average 4 KiB random-read latency. To reach 500,000 IOPS you need L = 500,000 × 0.000080 = 40 commands in flight on average. One thread issuing synchronously (L = 1) can never exceed 1 / 80 µs = 12,500 IOPS, no matter how fast the drive's datasheet says it is. Raise the queue depth and IOPS rises roughly in proportion — until some resource saturates, after which extra depth only adds waiting time: λ stops growing and W grows instead. That bend in the curve is "queue saturation".

Run the law backwards on a benchmark result. fio reports 400,000 IOPS at 640 µs average with iodepth = 256 across one job. L = 400,000 × 0.00064 = 256: every slot is full, and latency is eight times the unloaded 80 µs. The drive is saturated; the extra depth bought queueing delay, not throughput.

Utilization follows from the same idea: a resource that serves λ requests per second and spends S seconds on each is busy a fraction ρ = λ × S of the time (divide by the number of parallel servers if there are several). As ρ approaches 1, waiting time grows without bound; that is why tail latency explodes near peak IOPS long before the average moves much.

Latency budget builder

A 4 KiB read at queue depth 1 crosses every layer of the stack once. The defaults are the course's illustrative numbers for the lab system (not measurements): edit any value and the budget updates. The highlighted row is the dominant component.

The latency budget lab

  1. With the defaults, which component dominates? (Media, at about 85 % of 70.6 µs.)
  2. Halve the driver submit time. What changes? (0.5 µs, under 1 %. Optimising the driver does nothing visible for a TLC read.)
  3. Load "Low-latency media". Total falls to about 20.6 µs and media is still the largest item, but software now accounts for over a quarter of the budget.
  4. Load "Hit in controller cache". Now the host software path and interrupt delivery dominate. This is why polling (io_uring with polled queues, for example) matters only for fast media.
  5. Load "Low-latency media + polling" and compare QD1 IOPS with the default: roughly 1 / 70.6 µs ≈ 14,200 versus 1 / 18.4 µs ≈ 54,300.

Every number here is illustrative and platform-dependent. Measure your own with tracing (block-layer and NVMe trace events in Linux, see Chapter 70) and a protocol analyzer (doorbell-to-CQE time is the device's share).

Endurance: TBW ↔ DWPD

TBW = DWPD × C × 365 × Y
TBW
terabytes written: total host writes the drive is rated for
DWPD
drive writes per day: how many times the full user capacity may be written each day
C
user capacity in TB
Y
warranty period in years
TBWNAND ≈ C × PE ÷ WAF
PE
rated program/erase cycles of the NAND
WAF
write amplification factor: bytes written to NAND ÷ bytes written by the host (≥ 1; depends on workload, over-provisioning and firmware)
C
capacity in TB (strictly, raw NAND capacity, which is larger than user capacity)

A 1.92 TB drive at 1 DWPD for 5 years is rated for 3,504 TBW — about 22 MB/s of writes, around the clock, for five years. Random small writes raise WAF and consume the rating faster than sequential writes of the same volume. The SMART / Health log's Data Units Written and Percentage Used fields (Chapter 57) tell you where a real drive stands.

Percentiles and tail latency

The mean is the sum divided by the count. The median (p50) is the value half the samples are below. The pth percentile is the value that p % of samples are at or below; this calculator uses the nearest-rank method: sort the n samples and take the one at rank ⌈p/100 × n⌉. Tail percentiles (p99, p99.9) describe the slow requests, which in a fan-out system (one user request touching many drives) decide the user's experience: with 100 drives per request, nearly two-thirds of requests (1 − 0.99100 ≈ 63 %) include at least one drive's p99 latency.

The synthetic workload is a log-normal body around 78 µs with 1.5 % of requests delayed to 0.9–2.5 ms (think garbage collection) and 0.2 % to 4–10 ms. Its median barely notices; its mean rises by a third; its p99 is more than ten times the median. That is what "a fine average and angry users" looks like.

Performance labs with these calculators

Bandwidth lab: separate the four numbers

A Gen4 x4 SSD reads sequentially at 6.8 GB/s in one test and 3.4 GB/s in another machine. For each, write down: the raw signalling rate (64 Gb/s), after encoding (7.88 GB/s), the effective payload ceiling (≈ 7.0 GB/s with MPS 256), and the observed figure. Then check the second machine's link with lspci -vv (LnkSta) or the Config Space Decoder: 3.4 GB/s is almost exactly a Gen4 x4 device trained at Gen3 x4 (3.5 GB/s ceiling) — a link limitation, not a device limitation.

Solution

Check each row with Little's law: 12,300 × 81 µs ≈ 1.0; 96,000 × 83 µs ≈ 8.0; 370,000 × 86 µs ≈ 31.8; 720,000 × 178 µs ≈ 128; 735,000 × 348 µs ≈ 256. The data is consistent. Up to depth 32 latency is flat and IOPS scales: latency-limited (each command's own service time limits a thread). Between 128 and 256 IOPS barely grows while latency doubles: queue saturation at about 730k IOPS. Throughput at the plateau is 735,000 × 4,096 ≈ 3.0 GB/s, well under the 7 GB/s link ceiling: the limit is the device (controller or NAND parallelism), not bandwidth. The p99 jump at 256 is queueing. The right operating point for latency-sensitive work is around depth 32–64.

Practice

  1. Gen5 x4, 128b/130b. What is the throughput after encoding, per direction?

    • 16 GB/s
    • 15.75 GB/s
    • 12.8 GB/s
    Answer and reasoning

    32 × 4 = 128 Gb/s × 128/130 ÷ 8 = 15.75 GB/s. 16 GB/s ignores encoding; 12.8 would be 8b/10b.

  2. You need 200,000 IOPS at 100 µs average latency. What average queue depth?

    Answer

    L = 200,000 × 0.0001 = 20.

  3. Why does a 4 DW header (MWr64) cost more bandwidth than a 3 DW header?

    Answer

    Four extra bytes of overhead on every TLP: at 256-byte payloads, 280 versus 276 bytes on the wire per TLP, about 1.4 % less payload efficiency.

  4. A 3.84 TB drive is rated 7,008 TBW over 5 years. What is its DWPD?

    Answer

    7,008 ÷ (3.84 × 365 × 5) = 1.0 DWPD.

  5. The mean latency is 95 µs and the median 78 µs. What does that tell you?

    Answer

    The distribution is skewed by a tail of slow requests. Look at p99 and p99.9; the mean alone hides them.

  6. An application gets 12,000 IOPS from a drive rated for 1,000,000. Name the first thing you would check.

    Answer

    Queue depth. 12,000 IOPS ≈ 1 / 83 µs: a single synchronous stream at QD1. Little's law says the drive cannot be the limit.

  7. Why can't the bandwidth calculator's result be quoted as "the drive's throughput"?

    Answer

    It is the link's payload ceiling under stated assumptions. The device, the host, the workload and the other direction's traffic all reduce what an application sees.

  8. In the default latency budget, what is the largest QD1 IOPS gain from removing interrupt delivery entirely?

    Answer

    From 1/70.6 µs ≈ 14,160 to 1/68.6 µs ≈ 14,580 IOPS: about 3 %. Polling pays only when media latency is small.
