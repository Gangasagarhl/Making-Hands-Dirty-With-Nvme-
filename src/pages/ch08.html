The Link Training and Status State Machine (LTSSM) is the Physical Layer's brain. It decides whether anyone is on the other end of the wires, trains the lanes, agrees width and speed, runs the link, recovers it from errors, puts it to sleep and wakes it up. Every PCIe link problem you will ever debug happened in, or was caused by, one of its states.

Why this chapter exists

"The SSD is not detected", "the link keeps retraining", "the drive disappears after resume from sleep", "the link is stuck at Gen1": all of these are LTSSM stories. A protocol analyzer shows them as state names and ordered sets; Linux shows only their after-effects. If you know the states, their entry and exit conditions, and what each sends on the wire, you can read those stories directly.

This chapter teaches the state machine itself: every top-level state, its purpose, how it is entered and left, and how it fails. Chapter 9 then zooms into the training sequences (TS1/TS2), lane and link numbering, speed changes and equalisation.

Learning objectives

  • Name every top-level LTSSM state (Detect, Polling, Configuration, L0, Recovery, L0s, L1, L2, Hot Reset, Disabled, Loopback) and explain its purpose.
  • Draw the state diagram from memory with the main transitions.
  • List the main substates of Detect, Polling, Configuration and Recovery.
  • Explain which states are reached during normal bring-up, which during operation, which for power saving and which only on request.
  • Recognise LTSSM sequences in synthetic analyzer traces, including normal training to Gen4 x4 and a link that flaps through Recovery.
  • Map common failures (stuck in Detect, Polling loops, Configuration failures, Recovery storms, low-power exit failures) to likely causes.
  • Say what Linux can and cannot reveal about the LTSSM.

The story

Two friends use walkie-talkies to talk across a park.

  • First, Ana presses the button and listens: is anyone's walkie-talkie switched on at the other end? (Detect)
  • Then they both say "testing, testing" over and over until each can hear the other clearly. (Polling)
  • Then Ana says "I'm on channel 1, let's use channels 1 to 4", and Ben agrees. (Configuration)
  • Now they chat normally. (L0)
  • If the message gets crackly, they say "Sorry, say again, let's re-tune", and fix it quickly without hanging up. They also use this moment to switch to a faster talking speed. (Recovery)
  • During short silences, they take their thumb off the button (L0s); for longer breaks, they put the walkie-talkie down but keep it on (L1); at bedtime, they switch it to standby with only a tiny light on (L2).
  • Sometimes a grown-up says "start again from the very beginning" (Hot Reset), or "switch it off for now" (Disabled), or the friends play an echo game where Ben repeats everything Ana says, to test the line (Loopback).

An aircraft's flight follows fixed phases: pre-flight checks, taxi, take-off, climb, cruise, and procedures for turbulence, holding patterns, go-arounds and diversions. Each phase has entry criteria, a checklist and permitted next phases. Cruise is where the useful work happens, but most incidents happen in transitions. The LTSSM is the same: L0 is cruise; Detect, Polling and Configuration are the take-off; Recovery is the turbulence procedure (and the moment to climb to a higher flight level, a faster speed); L0s/L1/L2 are holding patterns and parking.

  • Walkie-talkies take turns. PCIe ports transmit and receive simultaneously; most LTSSM states involve both sides sending ordered sets at the same time.
  • Pilots decide by judgement. LTSSM transitions are defined by precise counts of received ordered sets and by timeouts; there is no judgement, only rules (plus implementation choices where the specification allows them).
  • The analogy suggests one state machine per link. In fact, each port runs its own LTSSM. The two machines usually move in step, but they can be in different substates momentarily, and a mismatch is a classic cause of training failures.
  • L0s is really per direction: one port's transmitter can be in L0s while the other direction carries data.

Beginner explanation

The LTSSM is a state machine inside the Physical Layer of every PCIe port. A state machine is a set of named modes plus rules for moving between them. After power-on and reset, each port's LTSSM goes through a fixed sequence:

  1. Detect: is there a receiver on the other end of each lane?
  2. Polling: send training patterns until each receiver locks onto them.
  3. Configuration: agree link width and lane numbers.
  4. L0: the link is up; the Data Link Layer can start and packets can flow.

From L0, the link can visit:

  • Recovery to fix problems or change speed, then return to L0;
  • L0s, L1, L2 to save power;
  • Hot Reset, Disabled, Loopback when software or a test asks for them.

The link always starts slow (2.5 GT/s). Reaching Gen4 speed means going through Recovery after the first arrival in L0. So a normal Gen4 link visits Recovery at least once during every boot. Recovery is not a sign of trouble by itself; Recovery again and again during operation is.

Engineering explanation

The state diagram

Who runs the LTSSM, and what it controls

Each port has its own LTSSM. The downstream port (Root Port or Switch downstream port) and the upstream port (the NVMe SSD) run the same state machine definition with a few role differences: the downstream port proposes link and lane numbers, normally initiates speed changes, and can direct its partner into Hot Reset, Disabled or Loopback. The LTSSM controls the transmitter (what to send: ordered sets, data, electrical idle), monitors the receiver (what has been received: counts of TS1/TS2, electrical idle, errors), programs the SerDes rate and equalisation, and reports to the Data Link Layer whether the physical link is up (LinkUp) or down.

Detect

Purpose: find out whether a partner is attached, and on which lanes.

  • Detect.Quiet: transmitter in Electrical Idle; rate forced to 2.5 GT/s; waits up to a timeout (commonly cited as 12 ms) or until the receiver sees the partner leave Electrical Idle.
  • Detect.Active: the transmitter performs receiver detection on each lane (a common-mode voltage step, sensing the charge time into the partner's termination). If receivers are found on all lanes, go to Polling with all of them. If on some lanes, the rules allow a retry and then proceed with the detected lanes. If none, return to Detect.Quiet.

What crosses the wire: no ordered sets; only the electrical detection pulse.

Failure examples: no partner power; missing REFCLK so the partner never enables termination; PERST# still asserted at the partner; broken AC coupling. Symptom: the port cycles Detect.Quiet ↔ Detect.Active forever, which an analyzer shows as silence with periodic detection pulses. Linux shows no device and, on a hot-plug port, "Presence Detect" without "Link Active".

Polling

Purpose: establish bit lock and symbol lock on each lane, fix polarity, and exchange initial training sets.

  • Polling.Active: send TS1 ordered sets (with Link and Lane numbers set to PAD) at 2.5 GT/s on all detected lanes. Move on when enough consecutive TS1 or TS2 have been received on the lanes and enough TS1 have been sent (the specification gives exact counts, commonly cited as at least 1024 TS1 sent and 8 consecutive received). Timeout commonly cited as 24 ms.
  • Polling.Configuration: send TS2; invert polarity on any lane where TS were received inverted; move to Configuration after enough consecutive TS2 are received and enough are sent in reply. Timeout (commonly cited as 48 ms) returns to Detect.
  • Polling.Compliance: transmit a compliance pattern for electrical testing. Entered when a partner is detected but does not respond with training sets in the expected way (for example a passive test load), or when directed by the Compliance Receive or Enter Compliance mechanisms. Not part of normal bring-up.

Failure examples: one lane never locks (signal integrity) → link may form with fewer lanes; all lanes fail → back to Detect, endless Detect–Polling loop; unexpected entry to Polling.Compliance on a production system (a known signature of a partner that is detected electrically but not sending TS, for example a device whose PCIe core is held in reset).

Exact counts and timeouts for every LTSSM substate are normative and defined in the Physical Layer Logical Block of the Base Specification. Values in this chapter marked "commonly cited" are given for orientation. Uncertain Always confirm against the revision you are validating.

Configuration

Purpose: agree the link number, link width and lane numbering; perform lane-to-lane deskew; then start the data stream.

  • Config.Linkwidth.Start / Config.Linkwidth.Accept: the downstream port sends TS1 with a link number and PAD lane numbers; the upstream port echoes the link number on the lanes it can use.
  • Config.Lanenum.Wait / Config.Lanenum.Accept: the downstream port proposes lane numbers; the upstream port accepts them or indicates reversed order.
  • Config.Complete: both send TS2 with the agreed numbers; deskew is established.
  • Config.Idle: both send idle data (Logical Idle at 8b/10b; an SDS ordered set followed by idle data at 128b/130b); after enough idle is received, enter L0.

Configuration is also the gateway to Loopback and Disabled during initial training (when TS1 control bits request them), and it is re-entered from Recovery when the link width must change (for example, re-forming with fewer lanes after a lane fails, or upconfiguring width).

Failure examples: the partners disagree on lane numbering (unsupported lane reversal), causing timeouts back to Detect; a lane drops out between Polling and Configuration, causing a narrower link; crosslinks (two downstream ports facing each other) handled only if supported.

L0

Purpose: normal operation. TLPs and DLLPs flow; SKP ordered sets are inserted periodically. The Physical Layer reports LinkUp to the Data Link Layer, which initialises flow control (DL_Init) and becomes DL_Active.

Exits: to Recovery (receiver errors such as loss of lock, receiving TS1/TS2 from the partner, a directed speed or width change, a retrain request from software via Link Control Retrain Link, or a Data Link Layer request after REPLAY_NUM rollover); to L0s (transmitter idle under Active State Power Management, ASPM); to L1 (ASPM or software-directed power management handshake); to L2 (power-down handshake).

Recovery

Purpose: re-establish bit/block lock and deskew without going through Detect, and the only path for speed changes, equalisation, exit from L1, and entry to Hot Reset, Disabled or Loopback from an active link.

  • Recovery.RcvrLock: send TS1; achieve lock on the received TS1/TS2.
  • Recovery.RcvrCfg: send TS2; confirm link and lane numbers are unchanged; check whether a speed change was requested by both sides.
  • Recovery.Speed: enter Electrical Idle, change the data rate, then go back to Recovery.RcvrLock at the new rate. If the new rate fails, revert to the previous (or a lower) rate.
  • Recovery.Equalization: at 8 GT/s and above, run the equalisation phases to tune transmitters and receivers (Chapter 9).
  • Recovery.Idle: send idle data; on receiving enough idle, return to L0. Also the branching point to Hot Reset, Disabled, Loopback or Configuration when directed.

A short Recovery (lock, confirm, idle) typically lasts microseconds; a Recovery with a speed change and equalisation can last many milliseconds. During Recovery, no TLPs flow; TLPs that were in flight are replayed by the Data Link Layer afterwards, so software normally sees no error.

Failure examples: Recovery entered repeatedly (a "flapping" link); Recovery.RcvrLock timing out at a high rate, leading to rate fallback; Recovery timing out completely → Detect, which drops the link (LinkUp false), causing Surprise Down errors, hot-plug link-down events, Completion Timeouts and NVMe controller resets.

L0s

Purpose: a light power-saving state entered autonomously by a transmitter whose direction has been idle for a short time (ASPM L0s). Each direction is independent: Tx_L0s on one side corresponds to Rx_L0s on the other.

  • Entry: the transmitter sends an Electrical Idle Ordered Set (EIOS) and goes electrically idle.
  • Exit: the transmitter sends Fast Training Sequence (FTS) ordered sets (the number needed, N_FTS, was advertised by the receiver in its training sets) plus, at 128b/130b rates, an EIEOS and SDS; the receiver re-locks and the direction returns to L0. If the receiver cannot re-lock in time, it moves to Recovery.

Failure examples: an N_FTS value too small for the real re-lock time causes frequent Recovery entries whenever traffic resumes; latency spikes for the first I/O after idle.

L0s is not supported in Flit Mode (Revision 6.0 and later at speeds where Flit Mode is in use). Flit Mode instead introduces L0p, in which a link can reduce its active width while staying in L0 to save power, and restore width without retraining. Version-dependent Many platforms disable L0s even on older links because the power saving is small and exit-latency effects are visible.

L1

Purpose: deeper power saving with the link idle in both directions. Entered by a handshake: either ASPM L1 (hardware-autonomous, negotiated with PM_Active_State_Request_L1 DLLPs) or software-directed when a device is put into a low-power device state (D3hot), using PM_Enter_L1 DLLPs. Both directions go electrically idle.

  • Exit: always through Recovery (L1 → Recovery → L0), triggered by either side needing to transmit.
  • L1 PM Substates (L1.1 and L1.2): optional deeper levels within L1 in which the reference clock can be stopped (using CLKREQ#) and, in L1.2, the receiver's common-mode voltage detection is turned off too. Exit latency grows with depth. These substates were added by an engineering change and are controlled by the L1 PM Substates extended capability. Version-dependent

Failure examples: the link fails to exit L1 (CLKREQ# not wired or not handled, REFCLK not restarted in time), leaving the device unreachable: Completion Timeouts on the first access after idle; NVMe command timeouts under light load but not under heavy load. A device advertising an L1 exit latency shorter than reality can break latency-sensitive workloads.

L2

Purpose: a very low power state in which main power is removed but auxiliary power remains, so the device can request wake-up (via the WAKE# signal or an in-band Beacon). Entered after the PM_Enter_L23 / PME_Turn_Off handshake that prepares the link for power removal (the "L2/L3 Ready" condition). When main power is restored, the LTSSM starts again from Detect. Without auxiliary power, the link simply goes to the off state (often called L3), which is not an LTSSM state as such.

Failure examples: device does not respond after system resume (link never re-enters Detect correctly, or PERST# sequencing on resume is wrong); the NVMe driver times out on its first command after resume.

Hot Reset

Purpose: an in-band reset of the device below a port without removing power. Software sets the Secondary Bus Reset bit in a downstream port's Bridge Control register; that port goes through Recovery and sends TS1 ordered sets with the Hot Reset training-control bit set; the partner enters Hot Reset as well. After a defined time (commonly cited as 2 ms), both go to Detect and the link trains again from scratch. A Hot Reset propagates down through Switches to everything below.

Failure examples: unexpected Hot Reset on a production link (a buggy driver or a hypervisor resetting a passed-through device); device that does not come back after Hot Reset (its firmware does not tolerate in-band reset); software issuing configuration requests too soon after the reset.

Disabled

Purpose: turn the link off on request. Software sets Link Disable in the downstream port's Link Control register; the port sends TS1 with the Disable Link bit, then EIOS, then electrical idle. The link stays down until the bit is cleared; then the port goes to Detect. Hot-plug software uses this to power down slots cleanly.

Failure examples: a link "mysteriously dead" because a tool left Link Disable set; a device that cannot retrain after being re-enabled.

Loopback

Purpose: testing. A loopback leader sends TS1 with the Loopback bit set; the follower enters Loopback and retransmits everything it receives (from the leader), so the leader can measure bit errors through the whole loop. Substates: Loopback.Entry, Loopback.Active, Loopback.Exit; exit goes to Detect. Used in validation and manufacturing tests, not in normal operation.

Failure examples: entering Loopback unintentionally (rare; a misconfigured test mode); a device that cannot exit Loopback without a fundamental reset.

LTSSM states versus device power states

Do not confuse link states (L0, L0s, L1, L2) with device power states (D0, D1, D2, D3hot, D3cold), which are a Function's power state controlled through the Power Management capability. They interact: putting a Function into D3hot causes the link to enter L1; D3cold means power removed (L2 with aux power, or off). A device can be in D0 with its link in L1 under ASPM. NVMe adds its own power states (PS0, PS1, …) on top, managed with Set Features (Chapter 50).

The central concept on the ladder: Recovery

"Sorry, say that again, let's re-tune the walkie-talkies" without hanging up, and the moment to agree to talk faster.

Recovery re-establishes lock and alignment on an existing link, changes speed, runs equalisation and exits L1, then returns to L0 with link and lane numbers unchanged.

The SerDes re-locks its CDR, the PCS re-aligns blocks and deskews lanes, PLLs switch rate in Recovery.Speed, and equaliser settings are updated in Recovery.Equalization. Meanwhile the Data Link Layer holds TLPs in its replay buffer.

Recovery and its substates (RcvrLock, RcvrCfg, Speed, Equalization, Idle) are normative LTSSM states in the Physical Layer Logical Block, with defined entry conditions, ordered-set counts and timeouts.

TS1 then TS2 ordered sets on all lanes; EIOS and Electrical Idle before a rate change; EIEOS at 8 GT/s and above; equalisation information carried in TS1 fields; then idle data and resumption of TLPs/DLLPs.

Analyzer: L0 traffic interrupted by TS1, a Recovery state band of microseconds (or milliseconds with speed change), then TLP replays. Counts of Recovery entries per second quantify link health.

Isolated Recovery at boot: normal (speed change). Periodic Recovery during operation: a marginal link, a low-power exit problem, or a Data Link Layer rollover. Correlate with RxErr, BadTLP, Rollover counters and with ASPM settings.

Hardware view

The LTSSM is digital logic in the PCIe controller core (sometimes partly in firmware running on a small microcontroller next to the SerDes). It drives the SerDes through control signals: transmit electrical idle, start receiver detection, select rate, load equaliser presets, report lock and electrical-idle detection. Around it sit several hardware dependencies that cause most LTSSM failures:

Many Root Ports and SSD controllers expose the current LTSSM state in a vendor-specific debug register, sometimes with a history buffer of recent transitions. These are invaluable in the lab but implementation-dependent: their location and encoding come from the component vendor's documentation, not the PCIe specification. Implementation-dependent

Retimers complicate the picture: each retimer participates in training (it has its own state machine that follows the ports' LTSSM progress and forwards ordered sets, adjusting some fields), so a link with a retimer has three or four agents involved in every transition, and a failure can originate in either channel segment.

Specification view

The LTSSM is defined in the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 — Physical Layer Logical Block — Link Training and Status State Rules, with state diagrams for each top-level state and its substates. Link power states and their handshakes are in — Power Management; the registers in — PCI Express Capability Structure; L1 PM Substates in its own extended capability description.

  • Top-level states: Detect, Polling, Configuration, Recovery, L0, L0s, L1, L2, Disabled, Loopback, Hot Reset.
  • Links begin training at 2.5 GT/s; speed changes occur through Recovery; L1 exits through Recovery.
  • Hot Reset, Disabled and Loopback are requested by training-control bits in TS1/TS2 ordered sets; their exit is to Detect.
  • Link Control contains Retrain Link and Link Disable; Link Status contains Link Training and Data Link Layer Link Active; Bridge Control contains Secondary Bus Reset.

Because every speed change needs Recovery, a Gen4 link must enter Recovery at least once per bring-up; Recovery counts at boot are therefore not evidence of a problem. Because L1 exits through Recovery, a system with aggressive ASPM L1 will log Recovery entries proportional to idle-to-busy transitions.

How fast a port moves through each substate within the allowed timeouts; whether it steps through rates or goes directly to the highest; retry policies after failed speed changes; when it initiates autonomous width or speed changes; which debug registers expose the LTSSM; ASPM policies chosen by platform firmware and OS.

Recovery.Equalization (Revision 3.0 and later), equalisation at 16 GT/s and 32 GT/s (4.0, 5.0), L1 PM Substates (added by engineering change to 3.x), L0p and the removal of L0s in Flit Mode (6.0), and Flit Mode negotiation during training (6.0) are all revision-dependent. Substate details have been refined in every revision.

  • The specification does not say how many Recovery entries per hour are acceptable.
  • It does not require a standard register exposing the current LTSSM state to software.
  • Confirm in the specification: every timeout value and ordered-set count; exact conditions for Polling.Compliance entry; which lane-number and link-number changes force Configuration instead of Recovery.

Never assume both ports are in the same substate at the same instant; never assume a link that reached L0 once will stay there; never assume Recovery entries are harmless because no error was reported; never assume the D-state and L-state of a device match.

Synthetic example for teaching.

Reasoning for the flapping link

  1. First abnormal event: row 2, the USP (SSD) receiver losing alignment on lane 1. It identifies a direction (host→device) and a lane.
  2. Periodicity (every ~50 ms) suggests something periodic in the system: a fan or voltage regulator modulating noise, a temperature control loop, a power-management timer, or crosstalk from a periodic aggressor. Random marginality usually looks random.
  3. Escalation: each short Recovery is harmless to software; the final failure at row 7 is not. Monitoring Recovery frequency would have predicted it.
  4. Discriminate: swap the SSD to another slot (does lane 1 follow the slot?), look at lane margining for lane 1 at the SSD receiver, check Root Port transmitter equalisation results for lane 1, check for a periodic noise source.
  5. Do not start from the NVMe timeouts: they are consequences, 0.85 s and many Recovery cycles after the first abnormal event.

Linux view

Linux has no standard window into the LTSSM state. It sees snapshots and events:

# Link state snapshot at the Root Port (downstream side of the link)
sudo lspci -vvv -s 00:01.1 | grep -E "LnkCtl:|LnkSta:|LnkSta2:|SltSta:"

# ASPM configuration in effect for the SSD's link
sudo lspci -vvv -s 01:00.0 | grep -E "LnkCap:|LnkCtl:"
cat /sys/module/pcie_aspm/parameters/policy

# L1 PM Substates capability and control, if present
sudo lspci -vvv -s 01:00.0 | sed -n '/L1 PM Substates/,/L1SubCtl2/p'

# Events: hot-plug link changes, DPC, AER, NVMe resets
sudo dmesg | grep -iE "link (up|down)|pciehp|DPC|AER|nvme.*(reset|timeout)"

What each tells you (shapes illustrative):

  • LnkSta: Speed 16GT/s, Width x4, TrErr- Train- SlotClk+ DLActive+ BWMgmt- ABWMgmt-: Train+ would mean the port is in training right now (rarely caught); DLActive+ means the Data Link Layer is up (if the port supports reporting it); BWMgmt+ means the link's speed or width changed without an autonomous reason since the bit was cleared, a strong hint of retraining due to problems.
  • LnkCtl: ASPM L1 Enabled; … CommClk+: which ASPM states are enabled and whether common clock is configured.
  • Kernel log lines from the PCIe hot-plug driver of the form "Slot(N): Link Down" / "Link Up", from DPC "containment event", and from the NVMe driver about controller resets, in that causal order, reconstruct a link-drop timeline.

Linux cannot count Recovery entries in general, cannot see L0s/L1 residency, and cannot see the substate where training failed. For that you need a protocol analyzer, vendor debug registers or vendor tools. Code / pseudocode

This script checks an LTSSM transition log (the kind you might export from an analyzer or a vendor debug buffer, here synthetic) against the top-level transitions in this chapter, then reports time in each state, Recovery entries outside bring-up, and the first suspicious event. The allowed-transition table is a simplification for teaching; substates are not modelled.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Validate and summarise a top-level LTSSM log (synthetic example built in)."""
from collections import defaultdict

ALLOWED = {
    "Detect":        {"Polling", "Detect"},
    "Polling":       {"Configuration", "Detect"},
    "Configuration": {"L0", "Detect", "Loopback", "Disabled"},
    "L0":            {"Recovery", "L0s", "L1", "L2"},
    "Recovery":      {"L0", "Configuration", "Detect", "Hot Reset", "Disabled", "Loopback"},
    "L0s":           {"L0", "Recovery"},
    "L1":            {"Recovery"},
    "L2":            {"Detect"},
    "Hot Reset":     {"Detect"},
    "Disabled":      {"Detect"},
    "Loopback":      {"Detect"},
}

LOG = """\
0        Detect
380      Polling
450      Configuration
470      L0      rate=2.5
500      Recovery reason=speed
9800     L0      rate=8
9900     Recovery reason=speed
21700    L0      rate=16
1012000  Recovery reason=error
1012006  L0      rate=16
1063000  Recovery reason=error
1063006  L0      rate=16
1115000  Recovery reason=error
1115006  L0      rate=16
1840000  Recovery reason=error
1864000  Detect
1865000  Polling
"""

def parse(text):
    rows = []
    for line in text.splitlines():
        if not line.strip():
            continue
        parts = line.split()
        t, state = int(parts[0]), parts[1]
        attrs = dict(p.split("=", 1) for p in parts[2:])
        rows.append((t, state, attrs))
    return rows

def analyse(rows):
    time_in = defaultdict(int)
    problems, error_recoveries, first_bad = [], 0, None
    reached_full_speed = False
    for (t0, s0, a0), (t1, s1, a1) in zip(rows, rows[1:]):
        time_in[s0] += t1 - t0
        if s1 not in ALLOWED.get(s0, set()):
            problems.append(f"t={t1} us: illegal transition {s0} -> {s1}")
            first_bad = first_bad or (t1, f"illegal {s0}->{s1}")
        if s1 == "Recovery" and a1.get("reason") != "speed":
            error_recoveries += 1
            first_bad = first_bad or (t1, "Recovery not caused by a speed change")
        if s0 == "L0" and a0.get("rate") == "16":
            reached_full_speed = True
        if s1 == "Detect" and reached_full_speed:
            problems.append(f"t={t1} us: link dropped to Detect after reaching L0 (link down)")
    print("time in state (us):", dict(time_in))
    print("Recovery entries not explained by speed change:", error_recoveries)
    for p in problems:
        print("PROBLEM:", p)
    if first_bad:
        print(f"first suspicious event: t={first_bad[0]} us ({first_bad[1]})")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    analyse(parse(LOG))

Time is in microseconds. The bring-up of the normal trace is encoded first; the steady-state workload of the flapping trace is placed to start at 1,000,000 µs (1 s), so its events appear at 1,012,000 µs, 1,063,000 µs and so on. Extend ALLOWED and add substates as you learn them in Chapter 9.

Worked example

Scenario. An NVMe SSD in a laptop-style platform works under benchmarks but the first command after a few seconds of idle sometimes takes 3 ms instead of 80 µs; occasionally the kernel logs an NVMe timeout and a controller reset after the machine has been idle.

  1. Hypothesis space by LTSSM state: the pattern "only after idle" points to low-power link states: L0s, L1 or L1 substates, plus NVMe autonomous power states.
  2. Read configuration: LnkCtl shows ASPM L1 enabled; L1 PM Substates capability shows L1.2 enabled; NVMe autonomous power state transitions are also enabled.
  3. Separate axes: the 3 ms delay could be NVMe power state exit latency (reported by the controller in its power state descriptors) or L1.2 exit. The timeout and reset are more serious: they suggest a failed L1 exit, where the link cannot return to L0 (Recovery fails) or REFCLK is not restored when CLKREQ# is asserted.
  4. Experiment: disable L1.2 only (leave L1) through the platform's settings or kernel parameters in a test environment; check whether timeouts disappear. Then, separately, disable NVMe autonomous power states and check whether the 3 ms delay disappears.
  5. Evidence to collect: an analyzer capture on the link around an idle-to-busy transition: L1 entry handshake, electrical idle, then exit via Recovery; any Recovery timeout → Detect is the failure.
  6. Likely conclusion (synthetic case): CLKREQ# routing on the board is missing for this slot, so L1.2 exit depends on a clock that never restarts reliably. Fix: platform should not enable L1.2 for this slot.

Common mistakes

  • Treating every Recovery entry as an error. Speed changes and L1 exits are Recovery too.
  • Believing the link goes back to Detect to change speed. It does not; Recovery handles speed changes on a live link.
  • Thinking L1 exits directly to L0. It goes through Recovery.
  • Confusing D3hot (device power state) with L1 or L2 (link states).
  • Assuming L0s is available on all links; it is often disabled and is not used in Flit Mode.
  • Thinking Hot Reset and a function-level reset are the same: Hot Reset is a link-level, in-band reset of everything below a port; Function Level Reset affects one function and does not retrain the link.
  • Starting a link-drop investigation from the NVMe timeout instead of the first LTSSM anomaly.

Senior engineer insight

A senior engineer treats Recovery frequency as a vital sign. Every healthy link has a characteristic count at bring-up and a near-zero rate in steady state (apart from L1 exits, if ASPM L1 is enabled). They ask for that number early, and if no tool can provide it, they ask for an analyzer capture over a long enough window to see the pattern.

They also respect the asymmetry of the LTSSM: either port can drag the link into Recovery, but the one that initiates it is usually the one that detected a problem. Identifying the initiator from the first TS1 after L0 points straight at a receiver and thus a direction.

Finally, they know that LTSSM bugs are frequently interaction bugs: two individually compliant ports with slightly different timing interpretations, or a retimer that handles a corner case differently. Interoperability testing with many partners is how such bugs are found before customers find them.

Lab: Reconstruct a link's life from an LTSSM log

Objective

Use ltssm_check.py and a live system's Link registers to reason about LTSSM history, and identify the first abnormal event in a synthetic log.

Skills

LTSSM state knowledge, transition validation, separating bring-up Recovery from error Recovery, reading LnkSta/LnkCtl for ASPM and training flags.

Safety

Safe lab Python on a synthetic log; read-only lspci. Do not use Retrain Link, Link Disable or Secondary Bus Reset on a machine with mounted filesystems on the device.

Hardware required

Any computer; a Linux machine with an NVMe SSD for steps involving lspci.

Software required

Python 3, pciutils.

Input

The built-in synthetic log; your own variations; your system's link registers.

Expected output

A timeline with bring-up phase, steady-state phase, the first abnormal event and its timestamp, and a statement of what Linux on your system can confirm about the current link state.

Background

Bring-up visits Detect, Polling, Configuration, L0, then Recovery for speed changes. Steady-state Recovery not explained by speed change or L1 exit is suspicious.

Step 1 — Run the checker

Run python3 ltssm_check.py. Note the time in each state and the first suspicious event.

Step 2 — Break it

Edit the log: add an illegal transition (for example L1 directly to L0) and a Recovery with reason=l1exit after an L1 entry. Adjust the script so L1-exit Recovery is not counted as an error.

Step 3 — Compare with a live system

Read LnkSta, LnkCtl and the ASPM policy on your system. Is ASPM L1 enabled? Is BWMgmt set on the Root Port?

Observation

Record what you can and cannot conclude about LTSSM history from the live system.

Questions

  1. In the synthetic log, how long did bring-up to 16 GT/s take, and which phase dominated?
  2. What is the first abnormal event, and why is it not the Detect at 1,864,000 µs?
  3. If ASPM L1 is enabled on your system, what Recovery rate would you expect during light I/O?

Challenge

Add a "flap rate" metric to the checker: error Recoveries per second over a sliding window, with a threshold that raises an alarm before the link drops.

Hint

Keep a deque of error-Recovery timestamps; drop entries older than the window; alarm when its length exceeds the threshold.

Solution

1: From 0 to 21.7 ms; the two Recovery-with-equalisation phases (0.5–9.8 ms and 9.9–21.7 ms) dominate. 2: The first abnormal event is the Recovery at 1,012,000 µs (12 ms into the steady-state workload) that was not caused by a speed change; the Detect at 1,864,000 µs is the final consequence, 852 ms and several Recovery cycles later. 3: Roughly one Recovery per idle-to-busy transition: with light, bursty I/O this can be many per second, and it is expected. A flap-rate alarm must therefore exclude L1-exit Recovery or be calibrated per platform.

What a senior engineer notices

  • The log's timestamps are from one port's perspective; the partner's log may differ by microseconds.
  • Whether the steady-state Recoveries are initiated by the same port each time.
  • Whether the link came back at the same width and speed after the drop.

Failure variants

  • A log with Configuration entries during operation: a width change (lane failure or autonomous width management).
  • A log with Hot Reset entries: someone reset the link; find who (driver, hypervisor, hot-plug).
  • A log that shows L2 then nothing: power was not restored, or wake was not delivered.

Extension

Combine the checker with AER counter snapshots taken every second (read-only sysfs) to correlate error Recoveries with RxErr and BadTLP increments on a real system, if your hardware exposes an LTSSM history via vendor tools.

Answers

2: Detect (Quiet, Active) → Polling (Active, Configuration) → Configuration (Linkwidth, Lanenum, Complete, Idle) → L0 @2.5 → Recovery (RcvrLock, RcvrCfg, Speed, RcvrLock, Equalization, RcvrCfg, Idle) → L0 @8 → Recovery (same, at 16 GT/s) → L0 @16. 3: Hot Reset (in-band reset), Disabled (turn off the link), Loopback (testing); Polling.Compliance can also be entered on request for electrical compliance testing. 4: In L1 both directions are idle, receivers may have lost lock entirely and (with L1 substates) clocks may be stopped; a full lock and confirmation sequence (Recovery) is needed. In L0s only one direction is idle for a short time and the receiver stays close to lock, so FTS suffices. 5: The link reached 16 GT/s at boot; later, errors caused Recovery at 16 GT/s; lock could not be re-achieved, so the ports performed a speed change down to 8 GT/s within Recovery and returned to L0; the hardware set Link Bandwidth Management Status because the speed changed for a reason other than autonomous power management.

Quiz

  1. In which state does a transmitter check for a receiver termination?

    • Detect.Active
    • Polling.Active
    • Configuration
    • Recovery.Speed
    Answer and reasoning

    Detect.Active performs receiver detection per lane.

  2. Which state is used to change link speed?

    • Detect
    • Configuration
    • Recovery
    • L1
    Answer and reasoning

    Recovery (via Recovery.Speed).

  3. How does a link leave L1?

    • Directly to L0 with FTS
    • Through Recovery
    • Through Detect
    • Through Configuration
    Answer and reasoning

    L1 always exits through Recovery.

  4. Where do Hot Reset, Disabled and Loopback exit to?

    • L0
    • Recovery
    • Configuration
    • Detect
    Answer and reasoning

    All three end in Detect, followed by full training.

  5. Which statement about L0s is true?

    • Both directions must enter it together
    • Each direction enters and exits independently
    • It requires the reference clock to be stopped
    • It is mandatory in Flit Mode
    Answer and reasoning

    L0s is per direction; it is not supported in Flit Mode.

  6. At what data rate do Polling and Configuration take place during initial training?

    • 2.5 GT/s
    • The highest common rate
    • 8 GT/s
    • The rate used before the last reset
    Answer and reasoning

    Initial training is at 2.5 GT/s.

  7. Why is a single Recovery entry during boot not evidence of a problem?

    Model answer

    Any link that operates above 2.5 GT/s must enter Recovery to change speed (and to equalise at 8 GT/s and above), so Recovery entries at bring-up are normal.

  8. A device works under heavy load but times out after idle. Which LTSSM states do you suspect first, and why?

    Model answer

    L1 and its substates (and L0s): entered during idle, exited when traffic resumes. A failed or slow exit (clock request handling, exit latency, Recovery failure) explains failures that appear only after idle.

  9. What does the Data Link Layer do while the LTSSM is in Recovery?

    Model answer

    It keeps LinkUp (unless Recovery fails to Detect), holds unacknowledged TLPs in the replay buffer, and replays as needed after L0 returns; no TLPs are lost.

Interview questions

  1. (Beginner) Name the LTSSM states a link passes through at power-on.

    Model answer

    Detect, Polling, Configuration, L0; then Recovery for each speed change, returning to L0.

  2. (Beginner) What is the difference between L0s and L1?

    Model answer

    L0s is a light, per-direction idle state exited with FTS; L1 idles both directions, needs a handshake to enter, saves more power and exits through Recovery with higher latency.

  3. (Intermediate) What triggers Recovery from L0?

    Model answer

    Receiving TS1/TS2 from the partner; receiver errors such as lost lock; a directed speed or width change; software Retrain Link; the Data Link Layer after REPLAY_NUM rollover; L1 exit (from L1, not L0).

  4. (Intermediate) How does software cause a Hot Reset, and what does the device experience?

    Model answer

    Setting Secondary Bus Reset in the downstream port's Bridge Control; the port sends TS1 with the Hot Reset bit; the device enters Hot Reset, resets its state (like a conventional reset, except sticky bits and aux-powered state), then the link returns to Detect and retrains; software must wait before accessing configuration space.

  5. (Senior) A link drops to Detect after hours of operation. How do you build the timeline?

    Model answer

    Gather timestamps from all sources: analyzer (if armed with a long trigger on Recovery/Detect), AER logs (correctables preceding the drop), hot-plug/DPC events, NVMe driver logs. Find the earliest abnormal physical event (first error Recovery, first RxErr) and the trend of Recovery frequency before the drop. Correlate with environment (temperature, power events, periodic workloads). Determine which port initiated each Recovery to identify the failing direction and lane. Then test hypotheses by swapping positions and margining.

  6. (Senior) Why might two compliant ports fail to train together?

    Model answer

    Timing interpretations inside allowed ranges that interact badly (one moves on just as the other times out), optional features negotiated inconsistently (lane reversal, equalisation bypass modes), retimer handling of corner cases, signal integrity marginal at one combination of transmitter presets and receiver adaptation, or bugs in rarely exercised substates. Interoperability testing and analyzer captures of both directions reveal which side deviates.

Model explanation (junior engineer)

After PERST# goes away, the Root Port checks each lane for a receiver and finds four. Both sides send TS1 at 2.5 GT/s until each receiver locks, then TS2 to confirm. The Root Port proposes link 0; the SSD accepts on all four lanes; lane numbers 0–3 are agreed; after TS2 and idle, the link is in L0 at 2.5 GT/s and the Data Link Layer exchanges InitFC. Then the Root Port asks for a faster speed in Recovery; both switch to 8 GT/s and tune their transmitters and receivers (equalisation), come back to L0, and repeat for 16 GT/s. The final state is L0 at 16 GT/s x4.

Chapter summary

  • Each port runs an LTSSM: Detect → Polling → Configuration → L0 at power-on; Recovery for errors, speed changes, equalisation and L1 exit.
  • Power states: L0s (per direction, FTS exit, not in Flit Mode), L1 (with L1.1/L1.2 substates, exits through Recovery), L2 (aux power, wake to Detect); L0p in Flit Mode.
  • On request: Hot Reset, Disabled, Loopback; all exit to Detect.
  • Recovery at bring-up is normal; repeated Recovery in steady state is a vital sign of trouble.
  • Linux sees only results and events; analyzers and vendor registers see the LTSSM itself.
