PCIe link, physical layer, LTSSM and equalization

Interviewers use these questions to see whether you can reason about a link that trains, but not the way it should: wrong speed, wrong width, too many recoveries, errors in one direction only. Name the state, the register and the experiment.

  1. [Trace] A root port's LTSSM history buffer (a vendor feature) shows the excerpt below for the Lab SSD after a cold boot. Why does the link reach L0 at 2.5 GT/s before it ever runs at 16 GT/s, and why does equalization appear inside Recovery rather than inside Polling or Configuration?

    #Time (ms)StateRateMeaning
    10.0Detect.Active2.5 GT/sreceivers detected on lanes 0–3
    20.4Polling.Active → Polling.Configuration2.5 GT/sbit and symbol lock, TS1/TS2 exchange
    31.1Configuration → L02.5 GT/sx4, lane numbers agreed
    41.3Recovery.RcvrLock → RcvrCfg → Speed2.5 → 8 GT/sspeed change requested
    53.9Recovery.Equalization Ph0–Ph38 GT/scoefficients chosen for 8 GT/s
    66.2Recovery … Equalization Ph0–Ph316 GT/scoefficients chosen for 16 GT/s
    79.0Recovery.Idle → L016 GT/snormal operation
    Model answer

    Every PCIe link starts training at 2.5 GT/s with 8b/10b encoding, because that is the only rate both ends are guaranteed to share. Detect finds receivers, Polling gets bit and symbol lock while the TS1/TS2 ordered sets advertise each port's supported data rates, and Configuration agrees width and lane numbers. Only after L0 at 2.5 GT/s does each side know what the other supports, so a higher rate is reached by a deliberate excursion through Recovery: RcvrLock and RcvrCfg agree to change speed, Recovery.Speed switches the rate, and the link relocks. Equalization for 8 GT/s and above belongs in Recovery because transmitter coefficients only matter at the new rate; the link must already be running at that rate to evaluate them. In the common flow the link equalizes at 8 GT/s and then at 16 GT/s, as in rows 5–6. Version-dependent Later revisions of the PCI Express Base Specification add optional ways to skip intermediate equalization; whether they are used depends on what both ports advertise.

    A strong answer includes: the 2.5 GT/s start as the common rate; rate advertisement in TS ordered sets; Recovery as the "change things without losing configuration" path; equalization per rate; that the history buffer is vendor-specific.

    Common weak answer: "It negotiates the highest speed in Polling." Speed is advertised in Polling but changed in Recovery.

  2. [Troubleshoot] Equalization at 16 GT/s reported all phases successful. Over the next hour the Lab SSD, but not the root port, logs correctable Receiver Errors, all on lane 2. Which equalization phase chose the transmitter settings that lane 2 is now receiving, and what do you check?

    Model answer

    The SSD's receiver is listening to the root port's transmitter. In equalization, Phase 2 is where the Upstream Port (the SSD) evaluates what it receives and asks the Downstream Port (the root port) to adjust its transmitter coefficients; Phase 3 is the reverse direction. So the settings behind lane 2's errors were chosen by the SSD's own evaluation in Phase 2, together with the SSD receiver's adaptation (continuous-time linear equalizer and decision feedback equalizer). "Successful" only means the evaluation criteria were met at that moment; margin can be thin and shrink with temperature. Check: whether the errors correlate with temperature or load; per-lane error status in the physical-layer extended capabilities where implemented; Lane Margining at the Receiver on the SSD side for lane 2 against lanes 0, 1 and 3; whether lane 2 has a longer trace, a connector or a via stub; and whether forcing re-equalization (retrain with equalization requested) picks different coefficients and moves the errors.

    A strong answer includes: direction (whose transmitter, whose receiver); Phase 2 versus Phase 3 ownership; margin versus pass/fail; a lane-specific physical check.

    Common weak answer: "The SSD is faulty." The SSD is the detector here, and the transmitter is the root port's.

    Learn it in: Chapter 54, Module 09, Module 07.

  3. [Troubleshoot] The output below is from lspci -vvv for the Lab SSD (illustrative and abridged). What has happened to the link, and what is your next step?

    LnkCap:  Port #0, Speed 16GT/s, Width x4, ASPM L1
LnkSta:  Speed 8GT/s (downgraded), Width x4
LnkCtl2: Target Link Speed: 16GT/s
LnkSta2: Equalization Complete+ Phase1+ Phase2+ Phase3+
    Model answer

    The SSD supports 16 GT/s and its Target Link Speed allows 16 GT/s, but the link runs at 8 GT/s with full width. The 8 GT/s equalization completed, so the link reached 8 GT/s properly. Either the 16 GT/s change was never attempted, or it was attempted and the link fell back. First read the other end: the root port's Link Capabilities, Supported Link Speeds and its own Target Link Speed (firmware often caps a slot's speed), then the 16.0 GT/s physical-layer capability on both ports for the 16 GT/s equalization-complete and phase bits. Link Bandwidth Management Status and Link Autonomous Bandwidth Status in Link Status tell you whether hardware changed the speed and why. Then retrain deliberately (set Retrain Link on the root port) and watch whether it reaches 16 GT/s and stays there. A speed that is reached and then lost points to signal integrity; one that is never attempted points to configuration.

    A strong answer includes: checking both ends; separating "never tried" from "tried and fell back"; the per-rate equalization status; a controlled retrain. Note that the "(downgraded)" annotation depends on the lspci version.

    Common weak answer: "Update the SSD firmware." That skips the root port, which is the most common place a speed cap lives.

    Learn it in: Chapter 9, Chapter 69, Module 09.

  4. [Performance] On the Lab SSD's Gen4 x4 link, how long does one 256-byte MWr64 take to serialize, and what is the theoretical ceiling on data bandwidth with MPS 256 before Data Link Layer Packets (DLLPs) and SKP ordered sets are counted?

    Model answer

    Raw rate: 16 GT/s × 4 lanes × 128/130 = 63.0 Gb/s ≈ 7.877 GB/s. An MWr64 with 256 bytes of payload occupies roughly 4 bytes of framing token (STP), 16 bytes of header, 256 bytes of data and 4 bytes of Link Cyclic Redundancy Check (LCRC) ≈ 280 bytes. 280 B ÷ 7.877 GB/s ≈ 35.5 ns per TLP, so at most about 28 million such TLPs per second, carrying 256/280 ≈ 91.4% data, or about 7.2 GB/s of payload. Ack and UpdateFC DLLPs, SKP ordered sets and any optional End-to-end CRC (ECRC) take a few percent more. Derived The framing overhead differs at 8b/10b rates (Gen1/Gen2) and in Flit mode, so recompute for those.

    A strong answer includes: encoding efficiency; per-TLP overhead in bytes; the division; the caveat that real throughput is lower again.

    Common weak answer: "16 GT/s × 4 = 64 Gb/s = 8 GB/s." That ignores encoding and packet overhead.

    Learn it in: Chapter 6, Mathematics for Storage and Links, Performance Engineering.

  5. [Troubleshoot] After moving the Lab SSD to a riser card, the link trains at 16 GT/s but only x2. Retraining does not change it. Where in link training was the width decided, and how do you localize the fault?

    Model answer

    Width is settled in Detect (which lanes have a receiver detected) and Configuration (link-width and lane-number negotiation through TS1/TS2). x2 from an x4 device means lanes 2 and 3 either did not detect a receiver or did not achieve lock and correct lane numbers, so Configuration built a narrower link. A retrain through Recovery normally keeps the width; widening needs upconfiguration support, so retraining is not a fix. Evidence: Link Capabilities x4 versus Link Status x2 on both ends; Linux's boot message that the available PCIe bandwidth is limited by an x2 link (paraphrased); vendor LTSSM logs if the root port has them. Localize by substitution: the same SSD directly in the slot (riser suspected), another SSD in the riser (SSD suspected), and checking the slot's bifurcation setting in firmware. If the riser reverses lane order, the device must support lane reversal or the link narrows.

    A strong answer includes: Detect/Configuration as the place width is decided; why retrain does not widen; substitution to split riser, slot and device; lane reversal and bifurcation as specific suspects.

    Common weak answer: "x2 is fine, Gen4 x2 is still fast." The performance cost is real (half the bandwidth) and the root cause is a physical defect that may get worse.

    Learn it in: Chapter 5, Chapter 8, Module 07.

  6. [Concept] A vendor counter on the root port shows the Lab SSD's link entering Recovery about twice per second on an almost idle system with Active State Power Management (ASPM) L1 enabled. AER shows nothing. Is that a problem?

    Model answer

    Probably not. Exit from L1 goes through Recovery (L1 → Recovery → L0), so every ASPM L1 exit adds one to a Recovery counter. Two exits per second on an idle system with periodic housekeeping traffic is plausible. Recoveries that matter are the ones caused by trouble: after Receiver Errors, after a REPLAY_NUM rollover, or when the partner starts sending TS1s from L0 unexpectedly. To tell them apart, correlate the counter with ASPM residency and with AER correctable counts; disable ASPM briefly as an experiment and see whether the count drops to nearly zero; on an analyzer, check whether each Recovery follows an electrical idle exit (normal) or arrives in the middle of traffic (suspicious). Implementation-dependent Recovery counters are vendor features, not standard registers, so read the vendor's definition of what they count.

    A strong answer includes: L1 exit via Recovery; "error-driven versus power-driven" Recovery; one experiment that separates them.

    Common weak answer: "Recovery means the link is unstable."

    Learn it in: Chapter 8, Chapter 50, Module 08.

  7. [Troubleshoot] On a new server the SSD sits on a cabled backplane with its own reference clock generator, and spread-spectrum clocking (SSC) is on. The SSD only supports common-clock operation. What symptoms do you expect and why?

    Model answer

    With separate reference clocks, each end's bit rate differs. Without SSC the difference is small (each reference within ±300 ppm), and SKP ordered sets inserted at the normal interval let the elastic buffers absorb it. With independent SSC, each clock wanders by up to about 0.5% (5000 ppm down-spread) on its own schedule, so the difference becomes far larger than the SKP interval was designed for. That separate-reference, independent-spread configuration is what Separate Reference clocks with Independent Spread (SRIS) mode exists for, with more frequent SKP ordered sets. A device without SRIS support in that setup sees elastic-buffer overflow or underflow: Receiver Errors, Bad TLP and replays, Recovery loops, sometimes training failures or links that drop under load. Evidence: errors that vanish when SSC is disabled in firmware or when a common clock is supplied. Fixes: common clock, SSC off for that path, or SRIS-capable devices on both ends with SRIS enabled.

    A strong answer includes: ppm arithmetic; SKP's purpose; SRIS as the matching mode; an on/off experiment with SSC.

    Common weak answer: "Clock problems would stop the link training at all." Often it trains and fails later under traffic.

    Learn it in: Chapter 5, Module 07.

  8. [Trace] The link is in ASPM L1. At t = 0 the CPU writes the SQ1 tail doorbell. The analyzer shows the doorbell MWr32 on the wire only at t = 18.4 µs. Describe the L1 entry handshake that put the link there, why the doorbell was delayed, and which fields bound that delay.

    #DirTime (µs)TypeMeaning
    1Dev→RP−950.0DLLP PM_Active_State_Request_L1 (repeated)SSD asks to enter L1
    2RP→Dev−949.6DLLP PM_Request_Ack (repeated)root port accepts

    Model answer

    ASPM L1 entry is requested by the downstream component (the SSD): it stops scheduling new TLPs, waits until its transmitted TLPs are acknowledged, then repeatedly sends PM_Active_State_Request_L1 DLLPs. The root port, if it can accept, blocks its own TLP scheduling, waits for its TLPs to be acknowledged and answers with PM_Request_Ack DLLPs; both sides then send Electrical Idle Ordered Sets (EIOS) and go idle. If the root port cannot accept, it rejects with a PM_Active_State_Nak Message. When the CPU's write arrives, the root port must bring the link out of L1 through Recovery before any TLP can be sent; the 18 µs gap is that exit latency (plus any L1 substate exit if L1.1/L1.2 were in use). The device advertises its L1 Exit Latency in Link Capabilities and its tolerance in Device Capabilities (Endpoint L1 Acceptable Latency); system software is meant to enable ASPM L1 only where the path's exit latency fits the tolerance.

    A strong answer includes: which side requests L1; the DLLP names; the Nak message path; exit through Recovery; the capability fields that bound exit latency.

    Common weak answer: "The doorbell was slow because the SSD was busy." The SSD never saw it until the link woke.

    Learn it in: Chapter 50, Module 42, Module 08.

  9. [Spec] L1 PM Substates are enabled on both ends, yet the Lab SSD's link never enters L1.2, only L1.1. The SSD reports a Latency Tolerance Reporting (LTR) value of 30 µs for both snoop and no-snoop; LTR_L1.2_THRESHOLD is programmed to 55 µs. Explain.

    Model answer

    L1.2 turns off more of the link's circuitry (including the common-mode voltage keepers) and so costs more time to exit. The LTR_L1.2_THRESHOLD represents that cost. When LTR is enabled, L1.2 entry is only permitted if the device's most recent reported latency tolerance is at least the threshold; otherwise the port may use L1.1 at most. Here the SSD says "I can tolerate only 30 µs", which is below the 55 µs exit cost, so L1.2 is not allowed. That is correct behaviour, not a bug. Next checks: why the SSD reports 30 µs while idle (many NVMe controllers report a short LTR while queues are active and a long one when idle; firmware decides); how the threshold was computed (Linux and platform firmware derive it from T_POWER_ON and Common_Mode_Restore_Time); and that CLKREQ# is wired, which L1 substates need. Confirm the exact comparison rule (which LTR values, greater-or-equal) in the L1 PM Substates section of the PCI Express Base Specification.

    A strong answer includes: threshold as an exit-cost budget; LTR as the device's tolerance; who sets each; that firmware policy decides the reported LTR.

    Common weak answer: "The platform does not support L1.2." It does; the device's own numbers forbid it.

    Learn it in: Chapter 50, Module 42.

  10. [Architecture] A Gen5 design adds a retimer on the riser between the root port and the SSD. What changes for link training, latency, error reporting and where you put the analyzer?

    Model answer

    A retimer is a Physical Layer participant: it recovers the bits, retransmits them, takes part in the LTSSM through its two pseudo-ports, and splits the channel into two link segments, each equalized separately. Training takes longer and has more ways to fail. The retimer adds latency in each direction (tens of nanoseconds is common; implementation-dependent), which adds to the Ack/Nak round trip and to every read's latency. It adjusts SKP ordered sets to absorb clock differences, so an analyzer on one side sees a different SKP pattern from the other. It does not take part in the Data Link Layer: it does not check LCRC, send Ack/Nak or log AER errors. So a corrupted bit on segment A or segment B both appear as the same Bad TLP at the receiving port; telling the segments apart needs the retimer's vendor diagnostics or an analyzer on each segment. Link Status 2 reports retimer presence on supporting ports.

    A strong answer includes: two segments, separate equalization; Physical-only role; added latency; why AER cannot locate the segment; where to probe.

    Common weak answer: "A retimer is just an amplifier." That describes a redriver, which does not retime or participate in training.

    Learn it in: Chapter 5, Chapter 54, Module 07.

  11. [Concept] In PCIe 6.x Flit mode, what replaces the per-TLP sequence number and LCRC, and how does that change what you read on an analyzer when a link has a bit error?

    Model answer

    In Flit mode the unit of protection and retry is a fixed-size flit, not a TLP. A 256-byte flit is commonly described as 236 bytes of TLP content, 6 bytes of Data Link Layer payload (which carries flit sequence and acknowledgement information), 8 bytes of CRC and 6 bytes of forward error correction (FEC). TLPs are packed into flits and may span them. Most bit errors are corrected by FEC and never cause a retry; the CRC catches what FEC cannot, and the receiver requests a retry of flits, not of a TLP. On an analyzer you therefore look for FEC correction counts, flit sequence numbers and flit retries instead of per-TLP Nak and replay. TLP headers also use a different Flit-mode header format, so a non-Flit decoder will mis-decode them. Flit mode is required at 64 GT/s with PAM4 signalling and, once negotiated, is used at lower rates too. Version-dependent Confirm byte counts and retry rules in the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x or later.

    A strong answer includes: flit as retry unit; FEC plus CRC; corrected versus retried errors; header format change; impact on tooling.

    Common weak answer: "Gen6 is just faster Gen5 with PAM4."

    Learn it in: Chapter 6, Module 07.

  12. [Troubleshoot] A loose cable drops the Lab SSD's link for about 30 ms, then it retrains on its own. List every place this event can leave a record, on both sides, and what state the NVMe controller is in afterwards.

    Model answer

    Root port side: Link Status Data Link Layer Link Active goes 0 then 1; on a hot-plug capable slot, Slot Status records a Data Link Layer State Changed event; if the port reports Surprise Down errors, its AER Uncorrectable Error Status records Surprise Down (fatal by default); if DPC is enabled, the fatal error triggers containment and the port holds the link down until software releases it, so "retrained on its own" would not happen. Linux logs pciehp link-down/link-up and possibly removes and re-adds the device; AER or DPC messages appear. Device side: when an Upstream Port's Data Link Layer goes down, the component is reset, so the SSD comes back with its configuration space at defaults (BARs unassigned, Bus Master Enable clear) and the NVMe controller disabled (CSTS.RDY = 0). Every in-flight command is lost. The driver sees MMIO reads returning all ones, then I/O timeouts, then a reset and re-initialization, if the device was not removed. The SSD's own logs may count an unexpected link loss.

    A strong answer includes: DLLLA; Surprise Down; DPC changing the outcome; "link down resets the device"; the driver's view.

    Common weak answer: "The link retrained, so nothing was lost."

    Learn it in: Chapter 49, Chapter 55, Module 41.

  13. [Spec] Software sets Secondary Bus Reset in the root port's Bridge Control register, holds it for a few milliseconds and clears it. Walk through what the SSD's LTSSM does and what software must do before touching the SSD again.

    Model answer

    Setting Secondary Bus Reset makes the root port send TS1 ordered sets with the Hot Reset bit. The SSD's port, on receiving them, enters the Hot Reset state, and when the hot reset ends the link goes to Detect and trains again from 2.5 GT/s. Hot Reset is a form of Conventional Reset: the SSD's functions return to their default configuration state (non-sticky registers cleared, BARs unassigned, Memory Space and Bus Master Enable clear) and the NVMe controller is disabled. Everything below that bridge is reset, including every function of a multi-function device. Before touching the device, software must wait for readiness: the required delay after reset (for ports above 5 GT/s, measured from link training completing, which DLL Link Active reporting can signal), then accept that configuration reads may return Configuration Request Retry Status for a while. Then it restores configuration space (Linux saves and restores it around the reset) and re-initializes the NVMe controller.

    A strong answer includes: TS1 Hot Reset bit; Detect and full retrain; what is reset; the readiness wait; restoring configuration.

    Common weak answer: "Hot reset only resets the NVMe controller." It resets the whole PCIe function and the link.

    Learn it in: Chapter 49, Module 08, Module 41.

  14. [Coding] Write a function that decodes the Link Capabilities and Link Status registers into speed and width and flags a link that trained below its capability. Use LnkCap = 0x0000_0844 and LnkSta = 0x3043 as the test.

    SPEED = {1: "2.5", 2: "5.0", 3: "8.0", 4: "16.0", 5: "32.0", 6: "64.0"}

def decode_link(lnkcap, lnksta):
    cap_speed, cap_width = lnkcap & 0xF, (lnkcap >> 4) & 0x3F
    sta_speed, sta_width = lnksta & 0xF, (lnksta >> 4) & 0x3F
    dllla = (lnksta >> 13) & 1
    issues = []
    if sta_speed < cap_speed:
        issues.append("speed below capability")
    if sta_width < cap_width:
        issues.append("width below capability")
    return (f"cap {SPEED.get(cap_speed, '?')} GT/s x{cap_width}, "
            f"now {SPEED.get(sta_speed, '?')} GT/s x{sta_width}, DLLLA={dllla}",
            issues)

print(decode_link(0x0000_0844, 0x3043))
# ('cap 16.0 GT/s x4, now 8.0 GT/s x4, DLLLA=1', ['speed below capability'])
    Model answer

    The code above is the expected shape. Max Link Speed is bits 3:0 of Link Capabilities and Maximum Link Width bits 9:4; Current Link Speed is bits 3:0 of Link Status, Negotiated Link Width bits 9:4, and Data Link Layer Link Active is bit 13. 0x0844 decodes to speed code 4 (16 GT/s) and width 4; 0x3043 to speed code 3 (8 GT/s), width 4, with DLLLA and Slot Clock Configuration set. Two subtleties a strong candidate mentions: strictly, the speed codes index the Supported Link Speeds Vector in Link Capabilities 2 rather than naming a generation, which works out the same for every real device; and comparing only the endpoint's capability is not enough, because the root port's capability may be lower, so a real tool compares against the minimum of both ends.

    A strong answer includes: correct bit fields; masking and shifting; the Supported Link Speeds Vector subtlety; comparing against both ends.

    Common weak answer: hard-coding "4 means Gen4" without knowing why, and flagging a downgrade that is really the root port's limit.

    Learn it in: Chapter 18, Chapter 69, Config Space and NVMe Register Decoder.

  15. [Troubleshoot] Lane Margining on a pre-production board shows lane 2 of the SSD's link with about 30% less timing margin than lanes 0, 1 and 3. The link has run error-free for a week. Do you sign off?

    Model answer

    Not on this evidence alone. Lane Margining at the Receiver (introduced with 16 GT/s in PCIe 4.0) lets software move a receiver's sampling point in time and voltage and count errors, so you can see how close to failure a lane is while it still runs clean. A lane with much less margin than its neighbours is the one that will fail first as temperature, supply voltage, aging or a different unit pushes it. Next steps: repeat across several boards and SSDs to see whether the weak lane follows the board or the drive; repeat hot and cold; inspect lane 2's routing (length, vias, connector pins); compare against the platform's margin pass criteria, which are set by the platform or OEM rather than by the base specification's mechanism. Margining itself can cause errors on the lane being margined, so run it on test systems, not in production.

    A strong answer includes: margin as a predictor; "follows board or follows drive"; environmental corners; where the pass limits come from; safety of margining.

    Common weak answer: "No errors, so it passes."

    Learn it in: Chapter 54, Validation Engineering, Module 09.

  16. [Troubleshoot] After a link-down event, the NVMe driver reads CSTS and gets 0xFFFF_FFFF. One version of the driver then tries to collect a telemetry log because "CFS is set"; another declares the controller ready. Explain both bugs and the right check.

    Model answer

    When a memory read to a device that is gone or not responding fails (Unsupported Request or Completion Timeout at the root complex), many platforms complete the CPU's load with all ones; that behaviour is platform- and implementation-dependent, but common. All ones in CSTS sets every defined bit: RDY = 1, CFS = 1, SHST = 11b (a reserved encoding), NSSRO = 1 and so on. A driver that tests only CFS sees a "fatal status" and talks to a device that cannot answer; one that tests only RDY thinks the controller is ready. The right check is to treat an all-ones register read as "device not reachable" first, before interpreting any bit, then confirm with configuration space (a Vendor ID of 0xFFFF means the function is not responding) and with the root port's Link Status. Recent Linux NVMe drivers, for example, check that the PCI device is still present (a configuration-space read) before trusting CSTS bits; the exact checks are version-dependent. Only once the device is reachable again does CFS mean anything.

    A strong answer includes: where all ones come from; why it is ambiguous; order of checks; the configuration-space confirmation.

    Common weak answer: "0xFFFFFFFF means the controller had a fatal error."

TLPs, completions and tags

These questions test whether you can decode a header by hand, compute Byte Count and Lower Address, and reason about what a requester is waiting for. Have the TLP header layout in your head; the interviewer will not give it to you.

  1. [Trace] Decode this header captured on the Lab SSD's link, and say what NVMe activity it most likely belongs to: DW0 0x6000_0040, DW1 0x0100_00FF, DW2 0x0000_0002, DW3 0x0000_1000.

    Model answer

    DW0: Fmt = 011b (4DW header, with data), Type = 00000b, so a 64-bit Memory Write (MWr64); Traffic Class 0, no attributes, TD = 0 (no ECRC), EP = 0 (not poisoned), Length = 0x040 = 64 DW = 256 bytes, exactly MPS. DW1: Requester ID 0x0100 (bus 1, device 0, function 0: the Lab SSD), Tag 0x00 (carries no matching meaning for a posted write), Last DW Byte Enables 1111b and First DW Byte Enables 1111b. DW2–DW3: address 0x0000_0002_0000_1000. The 4DW form is correct because the address is above 4 GiB.   15. [Architecture] What MRRS would you choose for an NVMe controller's DMA engine, and what does the choice affect beyond the number of read requests?

    Model answer

    MRRS bounds the size of each memory read. A larger MRRS (1024 to 4096 bytes) means fewer read requests, fewer tags for the same bytes in flight, and less upstream header overhead. But it changes behaviour on shared paths: one large read produces a long burst of completions that can delay completions for other devices behind the same switch or root port, adding latency to their reads, which is why some platforms cap MRRS for fairness. It does not change downstream efficiency much, because the root complex splits completions by MPS and often at RCB boundaries anyway. A smaller MRRS (256 to 512 bytes) needs more tags and more requests but keeps bursts short. For the Lab SSD, 512 with 8-bit tags covers Gen4 x4 comfortably (see the tag arithmetic). The value is set by system software in Device Control, and the controller's internal maximum may be smaller still; the controller must honour whichever is lower. Choose by measuring with the platform's real completion behaviour.

Ordering questions are where interviewers find out whether you know which agent creates order, which agent preserves it and which agent only observes it.   2. [Concept] The PCIe ordering table forbids some passing (for example, a read must not pass an earlier posted write) and requires other passing to be possible (posted writes must be able to pass non-posted requests). Why is the second kind of rule needed?

    Model answer

    The "must not pass" rules give producer-consumer correctness: a read must not overtake an earlier write in the same direction, so reading a flag after writing data sees the data. The "must be able to pass" rules exist to prevent deadlock. Buffers are finite. Imagine a bridge whose non-posted queue is full of reads that cannot make progress until their completions return, while those completions, or the buffer space they need, wait for posted writes that are stuck behind the reads in a single queue. Nothing moves. Requiring posted requests to be able to pass non-posted requests (and completions to be able to pass non-posted requests) guarantees that posted writes and completions can always drain, which frees the resources reads are waiting for. That is why PCIe keeps posted, non-posted and completion traffic in separate flow-control classes with separate credits. A candidate who can say which rules serve correctness and which serve progress understands the table rather than memorizing it.

    A strong answer includes: two purposes of the table; a concrete circular-wait scenario; separate credit classes as the mechanism.

    Common weak answer: "Writes are more important than reads."

    Learn it in: Chapter 14, Chapter 51, Module 14.

  3. [Architecture] An SSD writes read data peer-to-peer into a GPU's BAR through a PCIe switch, then writes the CQE to host memory and sends MSI-X. The host then tells the GPU to process the data, and occasionally the GPU sees old data. Explain, and say whose job the fix is.

    Model answer

    PCIe ordering holds along a path. The data writes leave the switch through the GPU's downstream port; the CQE and MSI-X leave through the upstream port toward the root complex. Once the paths diverge, nothing orders a write still queued toward the GPU against a write that has already reached host memory. The CPU can see the CQE, handle the interrupt and ring the GPU while the last data writes are still in the switch's buffers or the GPU's ingress queue. The producer must make sure the data has arrived before announcing completion. One method: after the last data write to the GPU, the SSD issues a read (a zero-length read is enough) to the GPU's address range. Its completion cannot return until the earlier posted writes on that path have been delivered ahead of it, and only then does the SSD write the CQE. The alternative is a consumer-side fence on the GPU. Whether a given controller does this is implementation-dependent, so peer-to-peer designs must check it explicitly.

    A strong answer includes: per-path ordering; divergence at the switch; read-after-write flush by the producer; that the fix belongs to the device or the protocol design, not to the host.

    Common weak answer: "PCIe guarantees writes from one device are ordered."

    Learn it in: Chapter 51, Module 14, Module 03.

  4. [Concept] Two SSDs sit behind one switch upstream port. What does ID-based Ordering (IDO) allow there, and why does it not break NVMe's "data before CQE" guarantee for either SSD?

    Model answer

    Normally a posted write must not pass an earlier posted write in the same direction, even if the two came from different devices. At a shared switch port, a write from SSD A that is stuck (for example, the next hop has no posted credits for it yet) then blocks SSD B's writes, even though B's data has nothing to do with A's. IDO, when enabled and set in the TLP's attributes, relaxes ordering between TLPs with different Requester IDs, so B's writes may pass A's. Ordering between TLPs with the same Requester ID is unchanged, so each SSD's data still precedes its own CQE and MSI-X. Software enables it with IDO Request Enable and IDO Completion Enable in Device Control 2, and the switch and root complex must support it. The benefit is less head-of-line blocking between independent devices. The risk is software that wrongly relies on ordering between two different devices' writes, which is rare and worth asking about.

    A strong answer includes: head-of-line blocking at a shared port; relaxation only across Requester IDs; enable bits; the one case it would break.

    Common weak answer: "IDO is another name for Relaxed Ordering."

    Learn it in: Chapter 51, Module 14.

  6. [Architecture] A driver places SQ1 in the controller memory buffer (CMB), mapped write-combining, and writes the doorbell through the normal uncached BAR0 mapping. What ordering problem must the driver handle, and what must the controller handle?

    Model answer

    With the SQ in the CMB, the 64-byte SQE travels to the device as posted MMIO writes, then the doorbell follows as another posted write to the same device. On the link, posted writes from the root complex to the same device stay in order, so if the SQE writes leave the CPU first, the controller receives them before the doorbell. The danger is in the CPU: write-combining buffers may hold, merge or flush SQE stores in any order and at any time, so the SQE bytes can still be in a write-combining buffer when the uncached doorbell store goes out. The driver must issue a store fence (a write memory barrier) after filling the SQE and before the doorbell. The controller must cope with the SQE arriving as several TLPs of unusual sizes and byte enables, and must not start fetching the slot from its own internal memory until earlier writes to the CMB have landed internally; the link order gives it that guarantee only if its internal paths keep it too.

    A strong answer includes: link order versus CPU write-combining order; the fence; split SQE writes; the controller's internal ordering duty.

    Common weak answer: "PCIe keeps writes in order, so nothing is needed."

  7. [Concept] "When the interrupt handler sees the CQE, the read data is in DRAM." Correct this statement.

    Model answer

    Ordering guarantees visibility, not location. When the CPU observes the CQE, any CPU that then reads the data buffer sees the new data, because the root complex makes the earlier writes visible in the coherence domain before the later CQE write. Where the bytes physically are is a platform detail: on many servers inbound DMA writes are placed directly in the last-level cache and reach DRAM only on eviction. That matters in three cases: on a non-coherent platform, the driver must invalidate CPU caches before reading DMA data; for host-side persistence (for example, when the buffer is persistent memory), "visible" is not "durable" and needs its own flush; and for performance, data landing in cache is why a quick consumer sees low latency. The precise statement: after observing the CQE, with proper barriers, the CPU will read the new data; the specification makes no claim that it is in DRAM.

    A strong answer includes: visibility versus location; cache placement of DMA writes; the non-coherent and persistence exceptions.

    Common weak answer: agreeing with the statement.

    Learn it in: Chapter 20, Module 01, Module 36.

  8. [Concept] Older drivers using legacy INTx interrupts read a device register at the start of their interrupt handler "to flush DMA". Why was that needed, and why does an MSI-X driver not need it?

    Model answer

    With INTx, the interrupt reaches the CPU through the interrupt controller, a path separate from the memory writes carrying DMA data (in conventional PCI, a physical wire). Nothing orders the interrupt against the device's earlier DMA writes, so the handler could run while data was still in flight. Reading a device register fixes this: the read's completion travels back toward the CPU through the same path as the device's earlier posted writes, and a completion must not pass posted writes in the same direction, so by the time the read returns, the earlier DMA writes have been pushed ahead of it. An MSI-X interrupt is itself a posted memory write from the device, sent after the data and the CQE, in the same ordered stream, so it cannot arrive before them. The read is unnecessary and costs a full round trip, a few hundred nanoseconds to microseconds of CPU stall on every interrupt.

    A strong answer includes: separate path for INTx; completion-pushes-posted rule; MSI-X as an in-band write; the cost.

    Common weak answer: "Reading the register acknowledges the interrupt." Sometimes it does that too, but that is not why it flushes DMA.

    Learn it in: Chapter 42, Chapter 43, Module 19.

  9. [Troubleshoot] An application occasionally reads old data from a buffer immediately after an NVMe Read completes. The analyzer is not yet attached. List hypotheses in the order you would test them and the cheapest discriminating evidence for each.

    Model answer

    (1) Application or driver reads the buffer before the request has completed (wrong buffer reused, asynchronous I/O misuse): check whether the buffer is reused across requests; reproduce with a fresh buffer per request.   11. [Concept] An engineer says, "Our device issues a zero-length read after its data writes, so the data is flushed, just like the NVMe Flush command." Separate the two ideas.

    Model answer

    They work at different layers and promise different things. A PCIe zero-length read is an ordering tool: because a read (and its completion) cannot pass earlier posted writes from the same requester on the same path, the completion's return proves that those writes have been delivered to and made visible at the completer. It says nothing about non-volatile media. The NVMe Flush command (opcode 00h) is a storage durability tool: it makes data that the controller has accepted into a volatile write cache non-volatile, so it survives power loss. It says nothing about PCIe ordering. A host that wants durable data after a Write needs Flush, or Force Unit Access on the Write, or a controller without a volatile write cache. A device that wants to know its DMA writes are visible before signalling something on another path uses ordering, possibly a zero-length read. Mixing the two leads to designs that are either slow (flushing media to fix an ordering problem) or unsafe (relying on ordering for durability).

    A strong answer includes: layer of each; what each proves; when each is the right tool.

    Common weak answer: "Both make sure data is written."

    Learn it in: Chapter 34, Chapter 14, Module 32.

  12. [Spec] A controller's CQE writes come from one internal engine and its MSI-X writes from another. Under load, the analyzer occasionally shows the MSI-X write on the link before the CQE write it announces. Can a PCIe switch or root complex be blamed?

    Model answer

    No. The ordering rules say how the fabric must treat TLPs relative to the order in which they were transmitted; they cannot restore an order the device never produced. If the analyzer, sitting on the SSD's link, sees MSI-X before the CQE, the device sent them in that order, and the fault is in the device's internal arbitration between the two engines. The device must serialize so that the CQE write is issued to its transaction layer before the interrupt write for that queue, with neither carrying Relaxed Ordering. The host consequence: the interrupt handler finds no new CQE (phase unchanged) and returns; if no further interrupt arrives, the command appears lost until a timeout or until the next interrupt on that vector happens to pick it up. That pattern, timeouts that cure themselves, is the signature in the field. Fix in firmware or RTL; a driver workaround is a final poll of the CQ before re-enabling the interrupt.

    A strong answer includes: fabric preserves, device creates; probe point proves transmit order; host symptom; where the fix belongs.

    Common weak answer: "The root complex reordered them."

Data Link Layer, replay and flow control

The Data Link Layer is where "no errors" and "slow" live side by side. Expect questions that ask you to count sequence numbers, decode credits and turn error counts into rates.

  1. [Trace] The SSD sends TLPs with sequence numbers 0x101 to 0x105. The root port's Ack for 0x105 is corrupted on the way back. The SSD's replay timer expires and it replays 0x101 to 0x105. What does the root port do with the replayed TLPs, and what does each side log?

    Model answer

    The root port already accepted 0x101 to 0x105, so its next expected sequence number is 0x106. A received TLP whose sequence number is earlier than expected is a duplicate: the receiver discards it without passing it to the transaction layer and schedules an Ack carrying the last good sequence number (0x105). That is not an error at the receiver. On the SSD's side, the corrupted Ack DLLP failed its 16-bit CRC and was discarded, which the SSD logs as a Bad DLLP (correctable); the replay timer expiry is logged as Replay Timer Timeout (correctable) and increments REPLAY_NUM. When the new Ack 0x105 arrives, the SSD purges those TLPs from its replay buffer and REPLAY_NUM resets. On an analyzer you see the same five TLPs twice with identical sequence numbers; that is replay, not duplicated data in host memory.

    A strong answer includes: duplicate handling and the Ack it triggers; who logs what; replay is invisible to the transaction layer.

    Common weak answer: "The host memory gets written twice."

  2. [Troubleshoot] The SSD's AER Correctable Error Status shows Replay Timer Timeout, and the counters show three timeouts, then one REPLAY_NUM Rollover, then the link enters Recovery. There are no Naks in the capture. What does that sequence tell you?

    Model answer

    REPLAY_NUM is a 2-bit counter that increments on every replay of the same unacknowledged TLPs and resets when an Ack acknowledges new ones. When it rolls over, the transmitter stops replaying and asks the Physical Layer to retrain the link (Recovery), logging REPLAY_NUM Rollover. Three timer-driven replays followed by a rollover mean the SSD got no acknowledgement at all for several replay periods. No Naks means the root port never saw a TLP it could identify as bad. So either the root port did not receive the TLPs in a form it could even frame (severe corruption in the SSD-to-root-port direction, where framing errors appear as Receiver Errors at the root port), or it received them and Acked, and the Acks were lost in the root-port-to-SSD direction (the SSD would then log Bad DLLP or Receiver Errors). Check both ends' Receiver Error and Bad DLLP counts to tell which direction is failing.

    A strong answer includes: REPLAY_NUM mechanics; rollover leads to Recovery; "timeouts, no Naks" means nothing usable got through in one direction; checking both ends to find which.

    Common weak answer: "The root port is too slow to Ack."

  4. [Performance] The Lab SSD's link is Gen4 x4. The time from transmitting a TLP to receiving and processing its Ack averages 1.0 µs, and the SSD's replay buffer holds 4 KiB. Is the replay buffer a bottleneck? What changes when a retimer adds latency?

    Model answer

    A transmitter must keep every TLP in its replay buffer until it is acknowledged, so it cannot have more unacknowledged bytes in flight than the buffer holds. To keep the link busy it needs link rate × Ack round trip: 7.877 GB/s × 1.0 µs ≈ 7.9 KB. With a 4 KiB buffer, the ceiling is about 4096 B ÷ 1.0 µs ≈ 4.1 GB/s, roughly half the link, with no errors logged anywhere: the transmitter simply stalls waiting for Acks. A retimer adds its latency twice to the round trip (once for the TLP, once for the Ack), raising the requirement; so do Ack coalescing delays at the receiver. Replay buffers must be sized for the highest link rate and the worst Ack latency the design supports, including retimers. On an analyzer, the symptom is gaps in transmission that end exactly when an Ack arrives. Derived

    A strong answer includes: bandwidth-delay product applied to the replay buffer; the 4.1 GB/s ceiling; retimer and coalescing effects; the trace signature.

    Common weak answer: "Replay buffers only matter when there are errors."

  5. [Concept] In a capture, the root port sends one Ack for every five or six TLPs from the SSD. Is that efficient or a problem, and what bounds it?

    Model answer

    Ack is cumulative: an Ack carrying sequence number N acknowledges every TLP up to and including N. A receiver therefore does not need to Ack each TLP; it may coalesce, which saves bandwidth in the reverse direction. One Ack per five or six TLPs is normal and efficient. The limit is the receiver's Ack latency timer: it must send an Ack within a bounded time after receiving a TLP that needs acknowledging, and the specification gives guideline values that depend on link width, speed and maximum payload. Too sparse and the transmitter's replay buffer fills or its replay timer expires (see the previous question); too dense wastes reverse-direction bandwidth, which matters on a link that is busy in both directions. When analysing, measure the time from a TLP to the first Ack that covers it, not the Ack count.

    A strong answer includes: cumulative Acks; the latency bound; trade-off between reverse bandwidth and replay-buffer pressure; measuring latency rather than counting.

    Common weak answer: "Every TLP must be Acked individually."

    def can_send(credit_limit, credits_consumed, needed, field_bits, infinite=False):

        return True
    mod = 1 << field_bits
    return (credit_limit - (credits_consumed + needed)) % mod <= mod // 2

  8. [Performance] The root port advertises 512 posted data credits (8 KiB) and returns posted credits about 1.5 µs after it receives each TLP. The SSD is on Gen4 x4. Which NVMe workload is limited by this, Reads or Writes, and to roughly what throughput?

  9. [Concept] DLLPs are not replayed. If an UpdateFC DLLP is corrupted and discarded, are those credits lost forever?

    Model answer

    No. UpdateFC carries the receiver's current credit limit as an absolute, modular counter value, not an increment. The next UpdateFC of the same type carries a value at least as large, which includes everything the lost one would have told the transmitter. Receivers also send UpdateFC periodically even when no new credits have been freed, under a timer in the tens of microseconds (check the exact value in the specification), so a transmitter starved by a lost update recovers within that interval. The corrupted DLLP is logged as a Bad DLLP (correctable) by the port that received it. The same design (cumulative values, periodic resend) is why Ack loss is also survivable: the next Ack covers the earlier one. That is the general pattern in the Data Link Layer: unprotected-by-replay messages carry state, not deltas.

    A strong answer includes: absolute values; periodic UpdateFC; Bad DLLP logging; the "state, not delta" design principle.

    Common weak answer: "The link must retrain to recover credits."

  10. [Spec] The root port expects sequence number 0x200 next. It receives a TLP with sequence number 0x202 and a correct LCRC. What does it do?

    Model answer

    A good LCRC proves the TLP arrived intact, but the sequence number shows that 0x200 and 0x201 never arrived at all (lost entirely, for example because framing was destroyed). A TLP whose sequence number is neither the expected one nor an earlier duplicate is handled as a bad TLP: it is discarded, a Nak is scheduled (unless one is already outstanding) carrying the last good sequence number, 0x1FF, and Bad TLP is logged. The transmitter, on the Nak, replays from 0x200, so 0x200, 0x201 and 0x202 arrive in order. The transaction layer never sees a gap or out-of-order TLP. The interview point: LCRC validity and acceptance are different checks, and the Data Link Layer delivers TLPs strictly in sequence.

    A strong answer includes: lost versus corrupted; the in-sequence delivery rule; the Nak's sequence number; replay from the gap.

  11. [Trace] The analyzer decodes a TLP from the SSD as perfectly good, but the root port answers with a Nak immediately afterwards. How can both be true?

  13. [Spec] Which Data Link Layer error is uncorrectable, what triggers it, and why is it treated so much more severely than a Bad TLP?

    Model answer

    Data Link Protocol Error, an uncorrectable error with fatal default severity. It is raised when a port receives an Ack or Nak DLLP whose sequence number corresponds neither to an unacknowledged TLP nor to the most recently acknowledged one: the two ends disagree about the state of the sequence space. A Bad TLP is a corrupted packet that replay repairs; the protocol is working as designed.   16. [Concept] What happens to the contents of the replay buffer when the link goes through Recovery, and when the link goes down? What does each mean for an NVMe command in flight?

    Model answer

    Recovery retrains the physical link while the Data Link Layer stays up (DL_Active). The replay buffer is kept, and unacknowledged TLPs are replayed after the link returns to L0, so nothing is lost: an NVMe command in flight simply takes longer, perhaps by microseconds to milliseconds. When the link goes down (DL_Down), the Data Link Layer is reset: the replay buffer is discarded, sequence numbers restart, and any TLP not yet acknowledged is gone. Posted writes are lost silently (data, CQEs, MSI-X), non-posted requests from either side end in completion timeouts, and on the device side link-down resets the component. For NVMe that means every command in flight is lost and the controller must be re-initialized; the driver sees timeouts or a dead device. That is why "Recovery count" and "link down count" must never be merged into one metric.

    A strong answer includes: DL_Active versus DL_Down; kept versus discarded replay buffer; NVMe consequences of each.

    Common weak answer: "Both lose the in-flight TLPs."

  4. [Spec] Bus Master Enable exists in the SSD's Command register and in the root port's Command register. What does each one control, and which would stop a misbehaving SSD that ignores its own?

  12. [Architecture] Two NVMe SSDs behind the same PCIe switch end up in one IOMMU group, so you cannot pass them through to two different virtual machines. Why, and what changes that?

    Model answer

If A and B belonged to different VMs, A could reach B. The kernel therefore puts every device that cannot be proven isolated into one IOMMU group, and VFIO assigns groups, not devices. Without ACS in the hardware, the options are to accept the shared group, move devices to separate root ports, or use a kernel override that gives up the isolation guarantee, which is not acceptable for untrusted guests. Peer-to-peer DMA performance is the price of redirecting through the root complex.

  13. [Architecture] You port an NVMe driver to a system-on-chip whose PCIe DMA is not cache-coherent. What breaks if you change nothing, and what does the driver need?

    Model answer

  15. [Spec] Enable Relaxed Ordering and Enable No Snoop in the SSD's Device Control register default to 1 after reset. Who is supposed to change them, and does "enabled" mean the device sets those attributes on every TLP?

    Model answer

    Both bits are permissions granted by system software, not instructions. With Enable Relaxed Ordering set, the function is permitted to set the RO attribute on TLPs where the function's own design says it is safe (for example data writes but not CQE or MSI-X writes); with Enable No Snoop set, it is permitted to set No Snoop where the function knows the target memory does not need cache snooping. Defaulting to 1 means "permitted unless software says otherwise". System software clears them where the platform cannot handle the attribute correctly; Linux, for example, has quirks that clear Relaxed Ordering for devices below certain root ports with known problems. The device remains responsible for using the attributes only where its producer–consumer guarantees allow it. So a bug where the device sets No Snoop on CQE writes into cacheable memory is the device's, even though the enable bit was set.

    A strong answer includes: permission versus behaviour; defaults; platform quirks; device responsibility.

    Common weak answer: "When the bits are set, every TLP is relaxed and non-snooped."

  16. [Troubleshoot] An AER event names device 0000:01:00.0. Six months later you need to know whether the same physical drive is still failing. Which identifiers do you record at the time of the event, and why not the BDF?

    Model answer

    A Bus:Device.Function (BDF) is a position in the hierarchy at one moment: it changes when cards move, BIOS settings change, devices are added or bus numbers are reassigned. /dev/nvme0n1 depends on probe order. At the time of the event, record the mapping from the BDF to stable identities: the NVMe controller's Serial Number and Model Number from Identify Controller and its firmware revision; the NVM subsystem NVMe Qualified Name (NQN); the namespace identifiers (Namespace Globally Unique Identifier (NGUID), IEEE Extended Unique Identifier (EUI-64) or namespace UUID); and, if present, the PCIe Device Serial Number capability. Linux exposes these through sysfs and nvme-cli. Six months later, search for the serial number or NQN, not the BDF. For fleet tools, write the identity snapshot into the same record as the error, because after a reset or replacement the BDF may point to a different drive.

    A strong answer includes: why BDF and device names are unstable; the stable identifiers and where they come from; capturing the mapping at event time.

    Common weak answer: "Look up 01:00.0 again."

    Learn it in: Chapter 70, Chapter 58, Module 23.
