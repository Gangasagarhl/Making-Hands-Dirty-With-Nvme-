Chapter 16 taught you what a credit is. This chapter teaches you to do arithmetic with credits: how many a link needs, how scaling stretches the counters for fast links, how a starved credit type quietly caps throughput, how to size the ingress buffers that credits describe, and how to recognise each of these in a trace.

Why this chapter exists

Most engineers meet PCI Express (PCIe) flow control as a correctness mechanism: "the transmitter never sends a packet the receiver has no room for." That is true, and Chapter 16 covered it. But in the field, flow control almost never shows up as a correctness bug. It shows up as a performance ceiling that nobody can explain: a Gen5 x4 solid-state drive (SSD) that reads at 9 GB/s on one platform and 13 GB/s on another, a switch port that loses half its throughput when a second device starts writing, a link that is perfectly healthy in every error register and still idles 40% of the time.

Those are credit problems. Credits are the receiver's statement of how much buffer it has, and buffer, round-trip latency and bandwidth are tied together by one simple inequality. Once you can do that arithmetic, you can look at a trace, count credits, and say "this link cannot go faster than X with these buffers" — and be right.

Learning objectives

  • Compute the credits a link needs to run at full rate from its bandwidth and its credit-loop latency (the bandwidth–delay product).
  • Explain the six credit types (PH, PD, NPH, NPD, CplH, CplD), their units, and which NVMe traffic consumes which.
  • Apply the transmitter gating rule with modular counters, and explain why at most about half the counter range can be outstanding.
  • Explain Scaled Flow Control: why Gen4 and later links need it, and what HdrScale/DataScale do.
  • Recognise header-credit starvation, data-credit starvation, and completion-buffer starvation, and separate them from tag exhaustion and media latency.
  • Size an ingress buffer (posted, non-posted, completion) for a target throughput, and explain the cost of over- and under-sizing.
  • Read UpdateFC DLLPs in a synthetic trace and identify the first credit-blocked event.

The story

Imagine you are passing buckets of water to your friend across a garden, but your friend has only a few empty barrels. Every time your friend empties a barrel into the pond, they shout "one more barrel free!" You are only allowed to throw a bucket if you know there is a free barrel.

If the garden is small, the shout reaches you quickly and you can keep throwing. If the garden is huge, the shout takes a long time to arrive, and you stand there waiting even though your friend's barrels are actually empty. The bigger the garden and the faster you throw, the more barrels your friend needs so you never have to wait.

Think of a busy restaurant kitchen and a pass (the counter where finished plates wait for servers). The kitchen may only put a plate on the pass if there is an empty spot. A server takes plates away and taps a bell for each spot freed. The pass has two kinds of spots: small spots for the order tickets and large trays for the food. A kitchen can be blocked because it ran out of ticket spots even though trays are free, or the other way round. And if the servers walk slowly back to tap the bell, the kitchen idles even though the dining room is coping fine.

What it hides: PCIe credits are counted with wrap-around counters, not individually announced; an UpdateFC DLLP carries a running total, not "one more free." Losing an UpdateFC therefore does not lose credits permanently — the next one corrects the count. The bell analogy suggests the opposite.

Where it misleads: there are not two kinds of spots but six (header and data for each of posted, non-posted and completion), per virtual channel. And some receivers advertise infinite credits for a type, which has no restaurant equivalent: it means "I promise I always have room; never count."

Beginner explanation

Every PCIe link has two independent directions. In each direction, the receiving side owns some memory — its ingress buffer — where arriving Transaction Layer Packets (TLPs) wait until the receiver's logic can process them. Flow control is the agreement that the transmitter never sends a TLP unless the receiver's buffer has room for it.

The receiver describes its buffer in credits. There are two units:

  • A header credit is room for one TLP header (whatever its size, including an optional TLP Prefix in non-flit mode — check the base specification for the exact inclusion rules for your revision).
  • A data credit is room for 16 bytes (four doublewords, 4 DW) of payload.

Separate pools exist for the three traffic classes of flow control: Posted (memory writes and messages), Non-Posted (memory reads, configuration and I/O requests, and some atomics) and Completions. Two units times three classes gives the six credit types you will see everywhere: PH, PD, NPH, NPD, CplH, CplD.

When the link comes up, the Data Link Layer exchanges InitFC1 and InitFC2 DLLPs to announce initial credit counts. Afterwards, the receiver periodically sends UpdateFC DLLPs carrying its running credit limit. The transmitter subtracts what it has consumed from what it has been allowed, and sends only if enough remains.

Here is the one new idea this chapter adds: credits take time to come back. A credit freed by the receiver is not usable by the transmitter until an UpdateFC reaches it. During that round trip, the transmitter keeps sending only if it still has credits left from before. So the number of credits a receiver advertises must cover everything the transmitter can send during one round trip, or the link sits idle while the credits are "in the mail."

Credits bound throughput like a window in TCP: throughput ≤ credits in bytes ÷ credit round-trip time. That one line explains most flow-control performance bugs.

Infinite credits

An InitFC value of zero in a header or data field means infinite credits: the receiver promises to always accept that type, and the transmitter never gates on it. Once advertised as infinite, the receiver does not send UpdateFC for that type (or sends it with zero fields — check the rules for your revision).

Infinite credits are most commonly seen for completions at a requester. A device that issues reads must already have reserved space for the data it asked for, because the completer has no way to throttle completions without risking deadlock. Endpoints and root complexes therefore typically advertise infinite CplH/CplD, and the burden moves to the requester: it must not issue a read unless it has buffer for the whole completion. Uncertain on the exact normative wording and its exceptions (for example, for switches and for some root complex configurations) — read the flow-control rules on completion credit advertisement in the PCI Express Base Specification before relying on it in a design review.

"Infinite completion credits" does not remove the buffer; it moves the throttle. The SSD's maximum outstanding read bytes are now limited by its own completion reorder buffer and by tags, not by the root port. When an NVMe WRITE workload is slow and every credit counter looks generous, look at the device's internal completion buffer — the limit is invisible on the wire.

The credit loop and the bandwidth–delay product

The time between "the transmitter sends a TLP" and "the transmitter learns, through an UpdateFC, that the receiver has freed that TLP's space" is the credit loop latency. It has several parts:

  1. Transmitter queuing and serialization of the TLP.
  2. Flight time across the channel (short on a motherboard; longer through retimers and cables).
  3. Receiver Physical and Data Link Layer processing (deskew, LCRC/FEC checks).
  4. Time the TLP sits in the ingress buffer before the receiver's consumer drains it — usually the dominant term, and the one that changes under load.
  5. Receiver's UpdateFC scheduling delay (receivers batch updates rather than sending one per TLP).
  6. UpdateFC serialization and flight back, possibly queued behind outgoing TLPs.
  7. Transmitter processing of the UpdateFC.

The central concept on the ladder

You can only throw a bucket when you know a barrel is empty, and the "barrel is empty" shout takes time to arrive. More distance or faster throwing needs more barrels.

The receiver advertises buffer space as credits; credits come back after a round trip. Throughput is capped at credits ÷ round-trip time.

The ingress buffer is SRAM inside the receiving port, partitioned into posted, non-posted and completion queues, each split into header slots and data storage. Its consumer is the port's internal fabric (a switch crossbar, the root complex's coherent fabric, the SSD's DMA engine).

The PCI Express Base Specification defines the six credit types, the 4 DW data credit unit, InitFC1/InitFC2/UpdateFC DLLPs, the modular gating rule, infinite credit semantics, Scaled Flow Control (from the 4.0 revision onward) and the rules for when UpdateFCs must be sent.

InitFC1-P, -NP, -Cpl then InitFC2-P, -NP, -Cpl per VC at link-up; UpdateFC-P/NP/Cpl afterwards, carrying HdrFC and DataFC running totals (and scale bits). TLPs are gated per type.

An analyzer shows UpdateFC DLLPs interleaved with TLPs. Many analyzers can compute and plot available credits per type. A credit stall shows as a gap in one TLP type while another type keeps flowing, ending right after an UpdateFC for the starved type.

"Throughput is low and there are no errors": compute the bandwidth–delay product from the trace, compare to advertised credits, find which type hits zero first, then ask why the receiver's consumer is slow (the usual root cause) rather than why the buffer is small.

Scaled Flow Control

As data rates rose, the bandwidth–delay product outgrew the 8-bit and 12-bit counters. Gen5 x16 moves roughly 63 GB/s; with a 1 µs credit loop that is 63 KB in flight — about 3,900 data credits, nearly double the 2,048 that an unscaled 12-bit counter allows outstanding.

Scaled Flow Control, introduced in the 4.0 revision of the base specification, adds two-bit HdrScale and DataScale fields to InitFC and UpdateFC DLLPs. Both sides indicate support during initialization; if both support it, each credit value in the DLLP is interpreted in multiples of the scale factor and the counters widen accordingly. The commonly described encodings are: 00b = scaling not supported, 01b = factor 1, 10b = factor 4, 11b = factor 16. Version-dependent Uncertain on the exact widened counter widths and on whether scaling is required at particular data rates — verify in the flow-control section of the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 4.0 or later.

Flit mode (Revision 6.0 and later). In Flit mode, DLLPs travel inside flits rather than as separate framed packets, and the specification adds options for shared credit pools across VCs alongside dedicated per-VC credits. The six-type concept and the bandwidth–delay arithmetic still apply, but the encoding and the pool structure differ. Treat every bit-level statement in this chapter as non-flit unless stated otherwise, and read the Flit-mode flow-control rules in the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 before analysing a Gen6 or Gen7 trace.

Credit starvation patterns

Credit starvation means a transmitter has a TLP ready but cannot send it because one credit type is exhausted. There are four distinct patterns. Learn to name them precisely; each points to a different fix.

Only the first three are flow control in the strict sense. The fourth is listed because it is the most common misdiagnosis: engineers see a slow write workload, assume credits, and spend a week staring at UpdateFCs that are perfectly healthy.

How ordering interacts with starvation

Credit types are separate so that one class cannot block another in a way that deadlocks. The ordering rules (Chapter 51) let posted requests pass non-posted requests and completions in specific cases, precisely so that a full non-posted buffer cannot prevent posted writes from draining. But the converse is also a performance trap: a non-posted read can wait behind a stalled posted stream when ordering requires it. A completion with Relaxed Ordering cleared may not pass earlier posted writes in the same direction, so a posted-credit stall at the root port can delay completions too.

If posted credits stall at the root port, the SSD cannot write read data, cannot write CQEs, and cannot send MSI-X interrupts — all three use PH/PD. Software sees "commands time out," not "posted credits are exhausted." Always check posted credit flow first when interrupts appear late.

Ingress buffer sizing

A designer choosing how much SRAM to give each class must answer four questions:

  1. What throughput must each class sustain? Posted at a root port must absorb the full device-to-host read stream; non-posted at the root port carries MRd headers only (small); completions at the endpoint must hold the write data it requested.
  2. What is the worst realistic credit loop? Not the idle loop. Include consumer latency under load and UpdateFC batching.
  3. What TLP size mix? Header credits are sized for the smallest common TLP size, not the largest.
  4. How many VCs? Most NVMe systems use only VC0; buffering VC1 that nobody uses is wasted area.

Over-sizing costs silicon area and power, and does nothing if the consumer is the bottleneck (a deeper buffer just fills and stays full). Under-sizing creates a hard throughput ceiling that no software tuning can fix. Because the loop latency includes the consumer's drain time, the honest design rule is: size for the loop latency at the throughput you need, then make sure the consumer can actually drain at that throughput.

How a port partitions its SRAM, whether it shares a pool across types internally, how often it sends UpdateFC, and how much it advertises are implementation choices. Two compliant root ports can differ by a factor of four in posted credits. Never assume a credit count — read it from the InitFC DLLPs in a trace.

UpdateFC timing

The specification requires receivers to send UpdateFC DLLPs when credits are freed, within limits, and also periodically even without new frees, so that a lost UpdateFC (DLLPs are not replayed) is repaired. It gives recommended maximum UpdateFC transmission latencies that depend on link width, MPS and data rate, and describes an optional receiver-side check that flags a protocol error if no UpdateFC arrives for a long time. Uncertain on the exact timer values for each revision; consult the "UpdateFC transmission latency" guidance and the flow-control timer rules in the base specification.

Because DLLPs are not acknowledged or replayed, a corrupted UpdateFC (bad DLLP CRC) is simply dropped. This is harmless for correctness but shows up as a momentary credit stall. A link with frequent Bad DLLP correctable errors can have "mysterious" throughput dips caused entirely by lost UpdateFCs. That links flow control to signal integrity — the subject of Chapter 54.

The 16 questions, for credits

1. What is it?
A per-type, per-VC count of receiver buffer space, expressed in header units and 16-byte data units.
2. Why was it created?
So a link never drops a TLP for lack of buffer, without needing a retransmit-on-overflow scheme.
3. What problem does it solve?
Lossless transport over a point-to-point link with independent buffers per traffic class, without deadlock between classes.
4. What existed before?
Parallel PCI used a shared bus with wait states and retry/disconnect: a target that was not ready simply told the initiator to try again, wasting bus time.
5. Why is the current design better?
The transmitter knows in advance whether it may send, so no bandwidth is wasted on rejected attempts, and classes are isolated.
6. Who creates it?
The receiver's Transaction Layer decides allocation; its Data Link Layer sends InitFC/UpdateFC.
7. Who consumes it?
The transmitter's Transaction Layer gating logic.
8. Where does it live?
Counters in both ports' link logic; the buffer itself in the receiver.
9. What memory/registers?
Ingress SRAM; credit counters are internal and not exposed in standard configuration space. Some vendors expose them in debug registers.
10. What happens before?
Link training reaches L0 and DL_Init begins (Chapter 8).
11. What happens after?
The Data Link Layer reports DL_Active and TLPs may flow.
12. What if it is wrong?
Too few: throughput ceiling. Over-advertised: Receiver Overflow (an uncorrectable error) and possibly a Flow Control Protocol Error.
13. What does the analyzer show?
InitFC1/InitFC2 at link-up, UpdateFC during traffic, and computed credit balance.
14. What does Linux show?
Nothing directly. Indirectly: AER correctable/uncorrectable errors (Receiver Overflow, Flow Control Protocol Error), and throughput.
15. What would validation test?
Minimum and maximum advertised credits, infinite credit behaviour, scaled FC negotiation, recovery after lost UpdateFCs, overflow detection.
16. What would a senior suspect?
A slow consumer first, small buffers second, lost UpdateFCs from signal integrity third — and the requester's own completion buffer whenever reads from a device are slow.

Hardware view

Inside a real port, the ingress buffer is usually a single SRAM carved into regions, with header slots kept separately so the receiver can parse headers quickly. Some designs share the data SRAM dynamically between classes and advertise conservatively; others allocate statically. Neither is visible on the wire — only the advertised numbers are.

The consumer differs by device type:

  • Root port: the root complex's internal fabric toward memory and the CPU caches. Posted writes go through the IOMMU (address translation can stall on page walks) and into the coherent memory system.
  • Switch port: the crossbar toward an egress port. If the egress port is itself credit-blocked by its downstream receiver, the stall propagates backward hop by hop. This is called head-of-line blocking through a switch.
  • NVMe SSD: the controller's front-end that decodes doorbell writes and handles completions for its own reads (SQE fetches, PRP lists, write data). SSD ingress traffic is light except for completions of its own read requests.

Retimers between ports are transparent to flow control: they do not terminate the Data Link Layer and keep no credits, but they add flight time in both directions, lengthening the credit loop. A board that adds two retimers to reach a far slot can lower the throughput ceiling of a marginally-buffered link.

Specification view

Flow control is defined in the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 — Transaction Layer Specification, Flow Control and the Data Link Layer chapter's description of InitFC and UpdateFC DLLPs and the DL_Init state machine. The PCI Express Base Specification is a PCI-SIG members' document. NVMe specifications say nothing about credits: the NVMe over PCIe Transport Specification, Revision 1.4 relies on PCIe for transport and does not define flow control.

What is verified? The six credit types; the 4 DW (16-byte) data credit unit; one header credit per TLP; InitFC1 → InitFC2 → UpdateFC sequence; non-flit DLLP type encodings for VC0; zero meaning infinite at initialization; modular counters with a gating rule that limits outstanding credits to half the counter range; Scaled Flow Control introduced in Revision 4.0.

What is derived? The bandwidth–delay formulas, the throughput ceiling, and all the sizing guidance in this chapter. They follow from the gating rule and Little's Law, not from normative text.

What is implementation-dependent? Advertised credit counts, buffer partitioning, UpdateFC batching policy (within the specification's limits), whether CREDITS_RECEIVED overflow checking is implemented, and the size of an endpoint's internal completion buffer.

What is version-dependent? Scaled Flow Control (4.0 and later), Flit-mode flow control and shared credit pools (6.0 and later), and the recommended UpdateFC latency tables, which were extended as data rates were added.

What must be checked in the specification? The exact rules for when finite completion credits are permitted; the widened counter sizes under scaling; UpdateFC timing requirements and the optional timeout check; the precise conditions for Receiver Overflow and Flow Control Protocol Error. The specification also does not say how many credits a port should advertise — only the minimums and the encoding.

What should never be assumed? That two root ports from the same vendor advertise the same credits; that "infinite" means "unlimited throughput"; that a missing UpdateFC is an error; that credits explain a slowdown before you have measured the loop latency.

Protocol view

At link-up, after the Physical Layer reaches L0, the Data Link Layer runs flow-control initialization for VC0 (and for other enabled VCs later):

Key protocol facts for analysis:

  • Credit information flows in the opposite direction to the TLPs it governs. To understand why the SSD stopped sending MWr, look at DLLPs going to the SSD.
  • UpdateFC carries a running total (CREDITS_ALLOCATED modulo the field size), so the difference between consecutive UpdateFCs is the credits freed in between.
  • UpdateFCs are not acknowledged, have no sequence numbers and are never replayed. TLPs are replayed, and a replayed TLP does not consume credits a second time (credits are consumed once, when the TLP is first sent by the Transaction Layer).
  • Each VC has its own credits. A device using Traffic Class mapping onto VC1 has an independent set.

Errors related to flow control are reported through Advanced Error Reporting (AER, Chapter 46): Receiver Overflow (a TLP arrived with no buffer for it — the transmitter violated its credits or the receiver advertised more than it had) and Flow Control Protocol Error (illegal flow-control behaviour, such as advertising more than the allowed maximum, or violating initialization rules). Both are uncorrectable; their default severity and the conditions that trigger them are defined in the base specification.

Transmitter gating (pseudocode)

/* Per VC, per type (P, NP, Cpl): header and data counters. F = field size. */
bool may_send(fc_t *hdr, fc_t *data, unsigned payload_bytes) {
    unsigned need_h = 1;
    unsigned need_d = (payload_bytes + 15) / 16;          /* round up to 4 DW */
    if (!hdr->infinite &&
        ((hdr->limit - (hdr->consumed + need_h)) & hdr->mask) > (hdr->mask + 1) / 2)
        return false;                                     /* header-blocked */
    if (need_d && !data->infinite &&
        ((data->limit - (data->consumed + need_d)) & data->mask) > (data->mask + 1) / 2)
        return false;                                     /* data-blocked */
    return true;
}

void on_send(fc_t *hdr, fc_t *data, unsigned payload_bytes) {
    hdr->consumed  = (hdr->consumed + 1) & hdr->mask;
    data->consumed = (data->consumed + (payload_bytes + 15) / 16) & data->mask;
}

void on_updatefc(fc_t *hdr, fc_t *data, unsigned hdrfc, unsigned datafc) {
    hdr->limit  = hdrfc;   /* running totals, not increments */
    data->limit = datafc;
}

Credit loop simulator (Python, runnable)

This simulator models one posted credit loop. It is a simulation of the arithmetic, not of any real device. Run it with python3 credit_loop_sim.py; it uses only the standard library.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""credit_loop_sim.py - synthetic model of one PCIe posted-credit loop.

Teaching simulation only: it models one credit type pair (PH/PD) in one
direction, with a fixed per-TLP overhead and a periodic UpdateFC. Real links
have more effects (DLLP bandwidth, replay, ordering, other traffic).
"""
import math

def simulate(link_bytes_per_ns, mps, ph, pd, flight_ns, drain_bytes_per_ns,
             update_period_ns, n_tlps=4000, overhead=24):
    ser_ns = (mps + overhead) / link_bytes_per_ns      # time on the wire per TLP
    d_per_tlp = math.ceil(mps / 16)                    # data credits per TLP
    window = min(ph, pd // d_per_tlp)                  # TLPs the credits allow in flight
    if window == 0:
        raise ValueError("credits too small for even one TLP")
    visible = []          # time the transmitter sees TLP i's credits returned
    tx_free = 0.0
    drain_done = 0.0
    stalled_ns = 0.0
    for i in range(n_tlps):
        ready = tx_free
        if i >= window:                                # must wait for TLP i-window's credits
            ready = max(ready, visible[i - window])
            stalled_ns += max(0.0, visible[i - window] - tx_free)
        arrive = ready + ser_ns + flight_ns
        drain_done = max(arrive, drain_done) + mps / drain_bytes_per_ns
        # credits freed at drain_done, announced at the next UpdateFC tick
        tick = math.ceil(drain_done / update_period_ns) * update_period_ns
        visible.append(tick + flight_ns + ser_ns * 0.1)  # DLLP is short
        tx_free = ready + ser_ns
    gbps = n_tlps * mps / drain_done                   # bytes/ns == GB/s
    return gbps, window, stalled_ns / drain_done

def main():
    link = 16e9 * 128 / 130 * 4 / 8 / 1e9              # Gen4 x4: ~7.877 bytes/ns
    print(f"Synthetic Gen4 x4 link, {link:.3f} GB/s raw after 128b/130b")
    print(f"{'PH':>4} {'PD':>5} {'window':>6} {'flight':>7} {'drain':>6} {'GB/s':>6} {'stalled':>8}")
    cases = [
        (32, 512, 100, 20.0), (8, 512, 100, 20.0), (32, 128, 100, 20.0),
        (16, 512, 400, 20.0), (64, 2048, 400, 20.0), (64, 2048, 100, 5.0),
    ]
    for ph, pd, flight, drain in cases:
        gbps, w, st = simulate(link, 256, ph, pd, flight, drain, update_period_ns=200)
        print(f"{ph:>4} {pd:>5} {w:>6} {flight:>5}ns {drain:>6.1f} {gbps:>6.2f} {st:>7.0%}")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Worked example

All numbers are a synthetic example for teaching; the formulas are Derived.

Example 1 — How many posted credits does the root port need for the Lab SSD at Gen4 x4?

  1. Link rate. 16 GT/s × 4 lanes × 128/130 ÷ 8 = 7.877 GB/s on the wire.
  2. Payload efficiency. A 256-byte MWr with a 4 DW header (64-bit address) costs about 16 (header) + 256 + 4 (framing token) + 4 (LCRC) = 280 bytes. Ignoring DLLP and SKP overhead: 256 ÷ 280 = 91.4% → about 7.2 GB/s payload.
  3. Loop latency. Suppose: 36 ns serialization, 20 ns flight each way, 150 ns receiver pipeline and drain, up to 200 ns waiting for the next UpdateFC, 20 ns transmitter processing. Worst case ≈ 450 ns.
  4. Data credits. 7.2 GB/s × 450 ns = 3,240 bytes → ⌈3,240 ÷ 16⌉ = 203 PD.
  5. Header credits. 3,240 ÷ 256 = 12.7 → 13 PH. But CQE and MSI-X writes are 16 and 4 bytes: if 10% of TLPs are small, size headers for the mix, and add margin: ~20 PH.
  6. Margin. Designers commonly add 50–100% for loop variance. A root port advertising 32 PH / 512 PD (Trace 1) has about 2.5× margin.

Example 2 — Why does Gen5 x16 need Scaled Flow Control?

  1. 32 GT/s × 16 × 128/130 ÷ 8 = 63.0 GB/s; payload at 91.4% ≈ 57.6 GB/s.
  2. A switch-to-root-port loop under load of 1 µs (switch crossbar plus root complex fabric) needs 57.6 KB in flight.
  3. 57,600 ÷ 16 = 3,600 data credits. The unscaled maximum outstanding is 2,048 (half of the 12-bit range).
  4. Without scaling, the ceiling is 2,048 × 16 B ÷ 1 µs = 32.8 GB/s — barely half the link. With DataScale factor 4, the same DataFC field can express up to four times as many credits, which covers the need.

Example 3 — Is the NVMe WRITE slowdown credits or tags?

The Lab SSD fetches write data with MRd of MRRS = 512 bytes. Measured round trip from MRd to the last CplD: 1.6 µs (host memory latency through the IOMMU). By Little's Law (L = λ × W):

Bread = Nout × MRRS ÷ Trt
Bread
achievable data-fetch throughput, bytes per second
Nout
number of read requests outstanding at once — the minimum of free tags, root-port NPH credits, and the SSD's completion-buffer capacity divided by MRRS
MRRS
bytes requested per MRd (Max Read Request Size)
Trt
round-trip time from MRd to the last completion, seconds
  • To reach 7.2 GB/s: Nout = 7.2 GB/s × 1.6 µs ÷ 512 B = 22.5 → 23 outstanding reads, 11.5 KiB of completion buffer.
  • Root port NPH = 48 (Trace 1): not the limit. Tags (256 with 8-bit tags): not the limit.
  • If the SSD's completion buffer is 8 KiB, Nout ≤ 16 → 16 × 512 ÷ 1.6 µs = 5.1 GB/s. The limit is the requester's own buffer, invisible as credits because the SSD advertised infinite Cpl.

Example 3 is the most common real case. Credits on the wire looked fine; the engineer who only checks UpdateFCs would declare "not flow control" and be technically right but practically stuck. The completion buffer is flow control's hidden twin.

Common mistakes

  • Reading UpdateFC values as increments. They are running totals modulo the field size. Subtract consecutive values (with wrap-around) to get credits freed.
  • Looking at credits in the wrong direction. The SSD's MWr are governed by DLLPs the root port sends to the SSD.
  • Forgetting rounding. A 4-byte doorbell or MSI-X payload consumes a full 16-byte data credit; a 260-byte payload consumes 17.
  • Treating infinite as unbounded. Infinite completion credits move the limit into the requester.
  • Sizing for idle latency. The loop that matters is the loaded loop, dominated by consumer drain time.
  • Blaming credits for a consumer stall. If the buffer is full and not draining, a bigger buffer only delays the stall.
  • Ignoring header credits. Small-TLP workloads (4 KiB random I/O with CQE and MSI-X traffic, 64-byte SQE fetches) are header-bound long before they are data-bound.
  • Assuming scaled flow control is negotiated. Both sides must support it; check the scale fields in InitFC.
  • Counting replayed TLPs twice. Replay happens below the credit accounting; credits were consumed once.

Senior engineer insight

  • Credits are a symptom carrier, not usually the root cause. A credit stall tells you where back-pressure surfaced. The question that finds the bug is "why did the consumer stop?"
  • Compute before you capture. Five minutes with the bandwidth–delay product tells you whether credits could be the limit. If the advertised window supports 3× the link rate, look elsewhere.
  • Separate classes are your best diagnostic tool. If one class stalls and another flows, the link, the Physical Layer and the Data Link Layer are exonerated in one observation.
  • Repeated identical UpdateFC values are evidence. They prove the receiver is alive and its buffer is not draining. Missing UpdateFCs prove something else (loss or a stuck Data Link Layer).
  • Validation must include the loaded loop. Many credit bugs only appear with IOMMU enabled, with virtualization, or with peer-to-peer traffic through a switch — none of which a simple loopback test exercises.
  • For design reviews: ask for the assumed loop latency behind every credit number, and for how the endpoint's completion buffer was sized against MRRS × outstanding requests.

Background

The simulator implements the window formula: the transmitter may have at most min(PH, ⌊PD ÷ credits per TLP⌋) TLPs unreturned. Credits return at the next UpdateFC tick after the consumer drains each TLP.

Step 1 — Predict before you run

For each case in main(), compute window bytes = window × 256 and estimate loop latency ≈ 2 × flight + 36 ns + 13 ns + 100 ns (average UpdateFC wait). Predict throughput = min(7.2 GB/s, window bytes ÷ loop, drain rate).

Step 2 — Run and compare

Run the simulator and fill in a predicted-vs-simulated table. Note where your prediction is off by more than 10% and explain why (hint: the UpdateFC tick is not always half a period away).

Step 3 — Find the knee

Modify main() to sweep PH from 4 to 48 with PD = 2048 and flight 400 ns. Plot or print throughput vs PH. Find the smallest PH that reaches 95% of the link payload rate.

Step 4 (optional) — Real parameters

Run sudo lspci -vvv -s <your SSD> and record link speed, width, MaxPayload and MaxReadReq. Re-run Step 1's arithmetic for your system with an assumed 500 ns loop. State clearly that the credit values are assumptions because Linux cannot show them.

Observation

  2. Why does case 6 not improve when you raise PH and PD to 128/4096?

Challenge

Extend the simulator with a second traffic stream of 16-byte posted writes (CQEs) at one per 16 data TLPs, sharing the same PH/PD pool. Show how the header knee moves.

Hints

Step 3: the knee is where window bytes ÷ loop ≈ 7.2 GB/s; with a ~950 ns loop that is about 6.8 KB → ~27 headers. For the challenge, give each TLP its own payload size and compute its data credits individually; the window becomes a running sum, not a fixed count.

Solution

Q1: both reduce the window to 8 TLPs (8 headers; or 128 ÷ 16 = 8 TLPs of data). The window, not which credit type defines it, sets throughput. Only a trace of which counter hit zero distinguishes them.

Q2: the consumer drains at 5 GB/s, below the link rate. The buffer fills regardless of size; throughput equals drain rate. This is a consumer stall.

Q3: UpdateFC-P DLLPs keep arriving (#4, #8) with unchanged totals. Lost DLLPs would produce an absence, not repeats.

Q4: 8× more data per header credit; if the loop is unchanged and the link permits, the fetch rate rises from ~2.3 GB/s toward the link limit (~7 GB/s), i.e. about 3× in practice, because the link saturates first.

Step 3: with flight 400 ns the simulator's knee lies in the high twenties of PH; the exact value depends on tick alignment. Changing MPS to 128 roughly doubles the headers needed for the same throughput.

What a senior engineer notices

That the simulator's "stalled" percentage is the same for header starvation and for a slow consumer — so the metric alone is not diagnostic. They also notice the UpdateFC period is a significant part of the loop, and that a receiver which batches updates less aggressively can fix a ceiling without adding any SRAM.

Failure variants

  • Set PD below 16 (less than one TLP): the simulator raises an error — the equivalent of a receiver advertising less than the minimum required for MPS-sized TLPs.
  • Set update_period_ns to 2,000: observe a severe ceiling with otherwise generous credits.
  • Set drain to 50 GB/s and flight to 2,000 ns (a long cable or many retimers): the bandwidth–delay product dominates.

Exercises

  1. A root port sends UpdateFC-P with DataFC = 0xFF8, then 200 ns later DataFC = 0x038. How many data credits were freed? (Remember the 12-bit wrap.)
  2. Compute PH and PD needed for 14 GB/s of 256-byte writes with a 700 ns loop. Is scaling needed?
  3. The Lab SSD wants to fetch 4 KiB of write data in one MRd. Explain why MRRS forbids this on the lab system and how many requests the 4 KiB fetch becomes.
  4. Explain, using credit types, why an NVMe completion queue entry can be delayed behind read data.
  5. Design question: a root port has 64 KiB of SRAM for VC0 ingress. Propose a split among P, NP and Cpl for a platform whose devices are mostly NVMe SSDs, and justify it.
Answers

1. (0x038 − 0xFF8) mod 0x1000 = 0x040 = 64 credits = 1 KiB.

2. 14 GB/s × 700 ns = 9,800 B → 613 PD and 39 PH (plus margin). 613 is below 2,048, so no scaling is needed for data; headers below 128 are fine too.

3. MRRS = 512 bytes on the lab system, so a 4 KiB fetch becomes 8 MRds of 512 bytes (8 NPH credits). Each may be returned as multiple completions split at the 64-byte Read Completion Boundary.

4. CQE writes and data writes are both posted MWr from the SSD, in the same VC; posted requests stay ordered relative to each other (unless Relaxed Ordering permits passing), so the CQE waits for preceding data and for PH/PD credits like any data TLP.

5. Most traffic into a root port from NVMe SSDs is posted (read data, CQEs, MSI-X) and non-posted headers (MRd). Completions toward the root port (responses to CPU MMIO reads) are small. A reasonable answer gives most data SRAM to posted (e.g. 40–48 KiB), plenty of NP headers but little NP data, and modest completion space (or infinite completion advertisement with reservation for its own MMIO reads). Any answer that sizes from bandwidth × loop is acceptable.

Quiz

  1. How many data credits does a memory write with a 100-byte payload consume?

    • 5
    • 6
    • 7
    • 100
    Answer and reasoning

    ⌈100 ÷ 16⌉ = 7. Data credits are 4 DW (16 bytes) each, rounded up.

  2. An SSD's DMA writes of read data into host memory consume which credits at the root port?

    • PH and PD
    • NPH and NPD
    • CplH and CplD
    • None; DMA bypasses flow control
    Answer and reasoning

    Memory writes are posted. The SSD sends MWr toward the root port and must have PH and PD credits from the root port.

  3. What does an InitFC value of zero for CplD mean?

    • The receiver has no completion buffer and cannot accept completions
    • Infinite credits: the transmitter does not gate completions on data credits
    • The value will be sent later in InitFC2
    Answer and reasoning

    Zero at initialization means infinite. The receiver guarantees it can always accept that type, typically because it reserved space before issuing requests.

  4. Why can at most half the counter range of credits be outstanding?

    Model answer

    Counters wrap. The gating rule decides "ahead" vs "behind" by checking whether the modular difference is at most half the range. If more than half could be outstanding, the transmitter could not distinguish a large allowance from having overrun the limit.

  5. Consecutive UpdateFC-P DLLPs carry identical HdrFC and DataFC for 30 µs. What does this indicate?

    • UpdateFCs are being lost
    • The receiver is alive but has freed no posted buffer space — its consumer is stalled
    • The link is in L1
    • The transmitter has stopped sending
    Answer and reasoning

    Periodic UpdateFCs with no change prove the Data Link Layer is alive and nothing was freed. If the transmitter had simply stopped, the receiver would still free what it had — totals would rise once and then stay flat, but the transmitter would show no queued TLPs.

  6. Which revision of the PCI Express Base Specification introduced Scaled Flow Control?

    • 2.0
    • 3.0
    • 4.0
    • 6.0
    Answer and reasoning

    Revision 4.0, alongside 16 GT/s, because the bandwidth–delay product of fast wide links exceeded what unscaled counters could express.

  7. A replayed TLP (after a NAK) consumes credits again. True or false?

    • True
    • False
    Answer and reasoning

    False. Credits are consumed when the Transaction Layer first sends the TLP. Replay is a Data Link Layer retransmission of the same TLP; the receiver did not keep the bad copy.

  8. An NVMe WRITE workload is slow; root-port NPH credits never reach zero; tags are plentiful. Name the most likely limit and how you would confirm it.

    Model answer

    The SSD's internal completion buffer (it advertised infinite Cpl credits, so it must reserve space before each MRd). Confirm by measuring outstanding read bytes in the trace: if outstanding MRd bytes plateau at a fixed value (for example 8 KiB) and the gap between MRds equals the completion round trip, the device is self-throttling.

  9. A link shows rising Bad DLLP correctable errors and periodic throughput dips but no other errors. Connect the two.

    Model answer

    Corrupted DLLPs are dropped; some of them are UpdateFCs. The transmitter's credit view lags until the next UpdateFC, producing momentary stalls. The root cause is signal integrity (see Chapter 54), not buffer sizing.

  10. Which error does a receiver report when a TLP arrives with no buffer space for it?

    • Completion Timeout
    • Unsupported Request
    • Malformed TLP
    • Receiver Overflow
    Answer and reasoning

    Receiver Overflow, an uncorrectable error reported through AER.

Interview questions

  1. Beginner: What are the six credit types and their units?

    Model answer

    PH, PD, NPH, NPD, CplH, CplD — header credits count one TLP header each; data credits count 16 bytes (4 DW) of payload. They are advertised per VC by the receiver.

  2. Beginner: Which DLLPs carry flow-control information and when?

    Model answer

    InitFC1 and InitFC2 during DL_Init after link training; UpdateFC during normal operation, periodically and when credits are freed.

  3. Intermediate: How do credits limit throughput, quantitatively?

    Model answer

    Throughput ≤ (credits in bytes) ÷ (credit loop latency), separately for headers (credits × average payload per TLP) and data (credits × 16). The loop includes the receiver's drain time and UpdateFC batching, so it is the loaded latency that matters.

  4. Intermediate: Why do endpoints usually advertise infinite completion credits, and what is the consequence?

    Model answer

    Because a completer cannot be made to wait on completions without risking deadlock, the requester should reserve buffer before requesting. Advertising infinite moves throttling into the requester: it limits outstanding reads to its completion-buffer size. Performance problems then appear as low outstanding read counts, not as credit stalls.

  5. Advanced: Explain Scaled Flow Control and when it is needed.

    Model answer

    Added in Revision 4.0, it lets InitFC/UpdateFC credit fields be multiplied by a negotiated scale factor (1, 4 or 16), widening the effective counters. It is needed when the bandwidth–delay product exceeds what 128 headers or 2,048 data credits outstanding can cover, as on Gen5 x16 links with microsecond-scale loops. Both sides must support it.

  6. Advanced: You see a posted credit stall. How do you decide between "buffer too small" and "consumer stalled"?

    Model answer

    Watch the UpdateFC totals during the stall. A small buffer produces regular returns with the window limiting throughput smoothly; a stalled consumer produces repeated unchanged totals for a long period followed by a large jump. Also vary the load: a small buffer gives a fixed ceiling, a consumer stall gives intermittent, cause-correlated dips.

  7. Senior: A Gen5 SSD reaches 13 GB/s on platform A and 9 GB/s on platform B with identical link speed and width. Walk through your investigation.

    Model answer

    Establish workload direction (reads stress posted credits at the root port; writes stress NPH and the SSD's completion buffer). Capture link-up on both to compare InitFC values and scaling. Under load, find whether any type hits zero on B. If yes, determine buffer size vs consumer stall from UpdateFC patterns; on B check IOMMU configuration, memory topology (remote NUMA node), and root complex settings. If no type hits zero, look at outstanding requests, MRRS/MPS differences, Relaxed Ordering, interrupt and CPU behaviour. Write down the bandwidth–delay arithmetic for both to show whether credits can explain a 30% gap.

  8. Senior: In a design review, what would you ask about an endpoint's flow-control design?

    Model answer

    The assumed loop latency behind each advertised value; whether the advertisement can ever exceed the physical buffer (overflow risk); the UpdateFC policy and how it meets the specification's latency guidance; completion-buffer sizing vs MRRS × target outstanding reads; behaviour with scaled FC both enabled and disabled; behaviour in Flit mode for Gen6 designs; and validation coverage for loaded loops with IOMMU and virtualization.

Teach-back challenge

  • To a child: explain why your friend across a very big garden needs more barrels than a friend across a small one, even if both empty barrels equally fast.
  • To a junior engineer: explain why an NVMe READ and an NVMe WRITE stress different credit types at the root port, and what infinite completion credits mean for the SSD.
  • To a senior engineer: present Trace 2 and argue, from the UpdateFC pattern alone, that enlarging the posted buffer would not fix the problem, then propose the next measurement.
Model explanations

Child: "The 'barrel is empty' shout takes longer to cross a big garden. While it travels you can't throw, unless you still have spare barrels. So the bigger the garden, the more spare barrels you need to keep throwing all the time."

Junior: "On a READ, the SSD pushes data into memory with posted writes, so the root port's posted credits matter. On a WRITE, the SSD pulls data with read requests — those use the root port's non-posted header credits — and the data comes back as completions. The SSD says 'infinite completion credits', which means it promises to have room; so it only sends as many reads as fit in its own completion buffer. That buffer becomes the limit."

Senior: "UpdateFC-P kept arriving every 200 ns with identical totals for 37 µs, then jumped by exactly the full window. That is a receiver whose Data Link Layer is healthy and whose buffer is full and not draining. A larger buffer fills in proportionally longer and stalls the same way. Next: correlate the stall periods with root complex events — IOMMU invalidations, memory throttling, or contention from another device — using host-side tracing and timestamps."

Chapter summary

Credits describe receiver buffer per class and per unit; they are consumed once per TLP, returned by UpdateFC running totals, and gated with modular arithmetic that allows at most half the counter range outstanding. Their performance meaning is a window: throughput is bounded by credit bytes divided by the loaded credit loop latency. Scaled Flow Control (Revision 4.0 onward) stretches the window for fast links; Flit mode (6.0 onward) changes encoding and adds shared pools. Starvation comes in header, data, consumer-stall and requester-self-throttle forms, and each has a distinct trace signature. Over-advertising credits produces Receiver Overflow; losing UpdateFCs produces dips that point to signal integrity.

Do not move on until you can:

  • compute the credits needed for a given throughput and loop latency, and the ceiling for given credits
  • name which credit types NVMe READ and WRITE traffic consumes at the root port and at the SSD
  • apply the modular gating rule and compute credits freed between two UpdateFCs with wrap-around
  • explain why Scaled Flow Control exists and what must be negotiated
  • classify a stall as header, data, consumer or requester-buffer starvation from a trace
  • size an ingress buffer for a target throughput and explain the cost of oversizing
  • explain what Receiver Overflow and Flow Control Protocol Error mean and which side is usually wrong
