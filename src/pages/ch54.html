From 8 GT/s upward, a PCI Express link cannot simply be switched on: each lane must first tune its transmitter and receiver to the particular piece of copper it drives. This chapter explains why, walks through the four equalization phases, translates presets and coefficients into plain numbers, introduces Lane Margining at the Receiver, and teaches you to recognise a marginal channel from the symptoms it causes higher up the stack.

Why this chapter exists

"The SSD trains at Gen4 on one slot and Gen3 on another." "The link is fine for hours, then logs a burst of correctable errors." "It only fails when the chassis is warm." These are some of the most common PCI Express (PCIe) field complaints, and nearly all of them trace back to the analog channel and to link equalization — the process that adapts each lane to its channel.

Protocol engineers sometimes treat the Physical Layer as someone else's problem. That is a mistake: the Physical Layer's failures arrive at your desk dressed as Data Link Layer replays, Transaction Layer completion timeouts, NVMe command timeouts and "the drive disappeared." You do not need to design a serializer/deserializer (SerDes) to debug them, but you must know what equalization does, where its results are recorded, and how to measure margin.

Learning objectives

  • Explain inter-symbol interference (ISI) and why it grows with data rate and channel length.
  • Describe transmitter FIR equalization (pre-cursor, main cursor, post-cursor), and receiver CTLE and DFE, at a conceptual level.
  • Convert a preset (de-emphasis and preshoot in dB) into normalized coefficients, and explain FS and LF.
  • Walk through equalization Phases 0, 1, 2 and 3: who adjusts whom, at which data rate, and what is exchanged.
  • Explain why Gen4, Gen5 and Gen6 make equalization harder, and what changes with PAM4 and Flit mode.
  • Use Lane Margining at the Receiver concepts to quantify margin per lane, and know its safety implications.
  • Map signal-integrity failures to their link-level symptoms and to the registers and logs that record them.

The story

Imagine shouting a message to a friend across a big echoing hall. If you speak slowly, each word fades before the next one, and your friend hears you clearly. If you speak fast, the echo of one word smears into the next, and the words blur together.

So you and your friend practise. You try saying the first sound of each word a little louder and the rest softer. Your friend tries cupping their ears in different ways. Your friend tells you "a bit louder at the start… no, too much… that's perfect!" Then you swap: your friend shouts and you coach them. Once you have both found the best way, you can talk fast without blurring. And every hall needs its own practice, because each one echoes differently.

A sound engineer setting up a concert in a new venue plays test tones, listens from the audience area, and adjusts the mixing desk's equalizer to cut the frequencies the room exaggerates and boost the ones it swallows. Then a second engineer at the back of the room radios suggestions back to the desk. The settings are specific to that room; a different venue needs a new sound check.

What it hides: a PCIe link has many lanes, each its own "room," tuned independently and simultaneously; and the tuning is repeated for every data rate above 5 GT/s. The listener in PCIe also has its own equalizer (the receiver's CTLE and DFE) that adapts without being told.

Where it misleads: the coaching in PCIe is not free-form. The receiver can only request coefficient settings within limits the transmitter advertised (FS and LF), using a defined handshake in training sets, under time limits. And the result is not judged by ear: each receiver uses its own implementation-specific figure of merit, which is why two receivers may choose different settings on the same channel.

Beginner explanation

A PCIe lane is a differential pair of copper traces, plus connectors, vias, sometimes cables and retimers. To a fast signal, that channel behaves like a low-pass filter: it lets slow changes through but attenuates and smears fast ones. A single 1 bit sent down the line arrives not as a crisp pulse but as a rounded bump with a long tail. That tail lands on top of the following bits — inter-symbol interference (ISI).

At 2.5 and 5 GT/s, the tail is short relative to a bit, and a fixed transmitter de-emphasis (−3.5 dB or −6 dB, chosen by a register) is enough. At 8 GT/s and above, bits are so short that the tail of one bit can span several following bits, and the right correction depends on the specific channel. So from Gen3 onward, PCIe adds an adaptive procedure: the two ends of each lane try transmitter settings, the receiver judges the result, and they converge on a good setting. This is equalization.

Equalization happens in two places:

  • Transmitter equalization: the transmitter pre-distorts the signal with a small filter, boosting transitions and reducing long runs, to cancel the channel's smearing in advance. This is what PCIe's presets and coefficients control, and what the link negotiates.
  • Receiver equalization: the receiver amplifies high frequencies (Continuous-Time Linear Equalizer, CTLE) and subtracts the predicted tail of previous bits (Decision Feedback Equalizer, DFE). This is internal to the receiver and not negotiated.

If equalization succeeds, the link runs at the target speed with a very low bit error rate (BER). If it does not, the link may fall back to a lower speed, use fewer lanes, or run at full speed with too many errors — which the Data Link Layer then hides behind retries until it cannot.

Equalization is per lane, per data rate, per direction. An x4 Gen4 link has done (up to) two independent equalization procedures — at 8 GT/s and at 16 GT/s — each covering four lanes in each direction.

Engineering explanation

Two facts make each generation harder. First, copper loss grows roughly with frequency (skin effect) and faster still in the dielectric, so doubling the rate more than doubles the dB of loss for the same trace. Second, faster edges are more sensitive to reflections at every impedance discontinuity (vias, connector pins, package balls). Retimers exist because beyond a certain length no equalization can recover the signal; a retimer is a full receiver and transmitter in the middle of the channel, and each half of the channel is equalized separately.

Transmitter FIR: three taps

A PCIe transmitter at 8 GT/s and above is a three-tap finite impulse response (FIR) filter. Each transmitted bit's voltage is the weighted sum of the previous, current and next bits:

V(n) = C−1 · b(n+1) + C0 · b(n) + C+1 · b(n−1)
V(n)
output voltage for bit n, normalized to full swing
b(n)
bit n, mapped to +1 or −1
C−1
pre-cursor coefficient (applied to the next bit; zero or negative) — produces preshoot
C0
main cursor coefficient (positive)
C+1
post-cursor coefficient (applied to the previous bit; zero or negative) — produces de-emphasis

The sum of magnitudes is constrained to the transmitter's full swing: |C−1| + C0 + |C+1| = FS. Each transmitter advertises two numbers during equalization:

  • FS (Full Swing): the total of coefficient magnitudes, in the transmitter's own integer units (a maximum of 63 in the 6-bit coefficient fields).
  • LF (Low Frequency): the minimum allowed C0 − |C−1| − |C+1|, which bounds how much the low-frequency level may be reduced.

A receiver requesting coefficients must stay within those limits; a request outside them is rejected by the transmitter (it sets a Reject Coefficient Values bit in its reply training sets). Verified as concepts; consult the Physical Layer "Transmitter Coefficients" and "Equalization" sections of the base specification for the exact field widths and rules.

Receiver equalization (not negotiated)

The receiver's CTLE is an analog high-pass boost with a few selectable gain/peaking settings. Its DFE remembers recently decided bits and subtracts their expected tails from the incoming signal. A DFE cannot correct pre-cursor ISI (energy that arrives before the main pulse), which is one reason transmitter preshoot exists. The receiver adapts CTLE and DFE continuously or during equalization; this is entirely Implementation-dependent, and its settings are not visible in standard registers.

The four phases

Equalization happens inside the LTSSM's Recovery.Equalization substate, after the link has trained at 2.5 GT/s and decided to change speed. Terminology: the Downstream Port (DSP) is the port facing away from the root (a root port or switch downstream port); the Upstream Port (USP) is the port facing the root (the SSD, or a switch's upstream port).

Rates above 8 GT/s

For 16 GT/s, the link first equalizes at 8 GT/s, then performs a separate equalization at 16 GT/s, with presets communicated in equalization training sets sent at 8 GT/s. Gen5 repeats again at 32 GT/s. Because walking up rate by rate is slow, the 5.0 revision added options to skip directly to the highest rate ("equalization bypass to highest rate") and to indicate that no equalization is needed when both sides agree. Gen6 adds equalization at 64 GT/s with PAM4 signalling. Version-dependent — each option is advertised in training sets and in the rate-specific Physical Layer capability structures; confirm support bits and rules in the revision you target.

Why Gen5 and Gen6 depend on equalization

  • Gen5 (32 GT/s NRZ): the loss at 16 GHz Nyquist is large enough that an un-equalized eye is completely closed on typical server channels. Retimers become common for slots more than a few inches from the root complex.
  • Gen6 (64 GT/s PAM4): keeps the 16 GHz Nyquist of Gen5 by sending two bits per symbol on four voltage levels. Each PAM4 eye has about one third of the NRZ eye height (roughly 9.5 dB less signal-to-noise for the same swing), so the raw error rate is much higher. Gen6 accepts a first-bit error rate around 10−6 and corrects it with a lightweight Forward Error Correction (FEC) code and a CRC inside 256-byte flits, retrying flits that FEC cannot fix.

Flit mode, PAM4 and FEC belong to Revision 6.0 and later; Revision 7.0 doubles again to 128 GT/s with PAM4. The equalization concepts carry forward, but training-set formats, precoding options and margining parameters differ by rate. Treat every Gen6+ statement in this chapter as conceptual and confirm details in the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0.

Lane Margining at the Receiver

Equalization finds a setting; it does not tell you how close to failure the link is. Lane Margining at the Receiver (LMR), introduced in Revision 4.0 and required for ports that support 16 GT/s and above, lets software measure that margin on a live link without external equipment.

Software instructs a receiver (in a port, or in a retimer, through the link) to move its sampling point away from the optimum — left or right in time, up or down in voltage — in steps, and to count errors at each step. The step at which errors exceed a limit marks the edge of the eye as seen by that receiver after its own equalization.

The capability is the Lane Margining at the Receiver Extended Capability, with a port-level capabilities/status pair and per-lane control and status registers. Commands include reporting capabilities (number of voltage and timing steps, maximum offsets, sampling rate, whether independent left/right or up/down margining is supported), setting an error-count limit, stepping timing or voltage, reporting the error count, returning to normal settings and clearing the error log. Commands to the USP or to retimers travel in-band in Control SKP Ordered Sets; this is why margining works while the link is in L0. Uncertain on exact encodings — see the "Lane Margining at the Receiver" sections of the Physical Layer and the extended-capability chapter.

Margining a receiver deliberately degrades its sampling. Errors during margining are expected and should be confined to margining, but a receiver that is already marginal can drop the link. Margin a production system only with the workload stopped and data backed up. This makes any margining lab a Caution lab.

Physical errors climb the stack in a predictable order: bit errors → LCRC failures (Bad TLP) and DLLP CRC failures (Bad DLLP) → NAKs and replays → Replay Timer Timeouts → REPLAY_NUM Rollover → Recovery entries → link retrains at lower speed or width → link down → Surprise Down / DPC → completion timeouts and NVMe command timeouts. A senior engineer reading an NVMe timeout first asks how far up this ladder the evidence goes.

The central concept on the ladder

Two friends practise talking fast across an echoing hall, coaching each other until each can understand the other.

Each lane tunes its transmitter's filter, guided by the far receiver's judgement, so fast bits survive the channel's smearing.

A 3-tap FIR in each transmitter (C−1, C0, C+1, bounded by FS and LF), and CTLE plus DFE in each receiver, adapted per lane and per rate.

The PCI Express Base Specification defines presets P0–P10, the coefficient rules, the Recovery.Equalization substate with Phases 0–3, the equalization status bits, the Lane Equalization Control registers and the Lane Margining at the Receiver capability.

EQ TS2s at the lower rate carry presets (Phase 0); TS1s at the new rate carry the equalization control (EC) phase field, the preset or coefficients in use, FS/LF, and requests and rejections (Phases 1–3).

An analyzer with LTSSM decode shows Recovery.Equalization entry, TS1 EC transitions 01→10→11, coefficient requests per lane, and time spent per phase. Configuration space shows completion and success bits afterwards.

Downtraining, Recovery loops and correctable-error bursts are first checked against equalization status and per-lane errors, then measured with margining, then confirmed by swapping slot, cable or retimer.

Hardware view

What a protocol engineer should know about the hardware:

  • Firmware sets the starting point. Platform firmware programs the downstream port's Lane Equalization Control registers with initial transmitter presets for both ends, per lane, per rate. Board designers tune these tables per slot. A wrong table entry is a classic cause of a slot that "never reaches Gen4."
  • Receivers judge with private metrics. How a receiver decides one coefficient set is better than another (eye height estimate, error count, internal adaptation metrics) is implementation-specific.
  • Retimers split the problem. A retimer has its own receivers and transmitters; with one retimer there are two equalized segments per direction. Standard registers report whether retimers were detected, and LMR can address retimer receivers.
  • Temperature and voltage move the optimum. Equalization runs at link-up; the channel then warms. Good receivers keep adapting CTLE/DFE; transmitter settings stay fixed until the next equalization.

M.2 and U.2/U.3/E3.S NVMe drives sit at the end of channels that include a connector, sometimes a cable or backplane, and often a retimer on the backplane. When the same drive model behaves differently in two bays, the channel is the first suspect.

Specification view

Equalization is defined in the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 — Physical Layer Logical Block: Link Equalization Procedure, the LTSSM: Recovery.Equalization substate, the Electrical sub-block: Transmitter presets and coefficients, and the extended capabilities chapter (Secondary PCI Express Extended Capability, Physical Layer 16.0 GT/s, 32.0 GT/s and 64.0 GT/s Extended Capabilities, Lane Margining at the Receiver Extended Capability). Form-factor channel budgets come from the PCI Express Card Electromechanical (CEM) Specification and related form-factor specifications. NVMe specifications are silent on equalization; an NVMe SSD's Physical Layer obeys PCIe rules only.

What is verified? Adaptive equalization from 8 GT/s; four phases (0–3) in Recovery.Equalization; Phase 2 tunes the Downstream Port's transmitter, Phase 3 the Upstream Port's; three-tap transmitter FIR; presets P0–P10; FS and LF; equalization status bits in Link Status 2 (8 GT/s) and in the rate-specific Physical Layer capabilities; Lane Margining at the Receiver introduced in Revision 4.0; Gen6 uses PAM4, Flit mode and FEC.

What is derived? The coefficient values computed from preset dB targets; the escalation ladder of symptoms; margin interpretation rules of thumb such as "a lane with less than 60% of the median eye width is suspect."

What is implementation-dependent? Receiver CTLE/DFE design and adaptation; the receiver's figure of merit and search algorithm in Phases 2/3; whether the DSP skips Phases 2/3; initial preset tables (platform firmware); margining step sizes and maximum offsets (reported by the receiver); pass/fail criteria for margins (set by platform validation teams, not the specification).

What is version-dependent? 16 GT/s equalization and LMR (4.0); 32 GT/s, equalization bypass and "no equalization needed" options (5.0); 64 GT/s PAM4, precoding, Flit mode, FEC (6.0); 128 GT/s (7.0). Register layouts for each rate live in rate-specific capabilities.

What must be checked in the specification? Exact preset tolerances and P10's definition; FS/LF field widths and rules; Phase 2/3 timeouts; which LMR commands are mandatory vs optional; how margining addresses retimers; the conditions under which a port requests re-equalization.

What the specification does not say: how good a margin is "good enough" for a product; which preset is best for a given channel; how a receiver should evaluate coefficients.

What should never be assumed? That "Equalization Complete" means the link has good margin; that a link at full speed has no physical problem; that margining results from one receiver describe the opposite direction; that two drives in two slots share the same equalization outcome.

Protocol view

What crosses the link during equalization is entirely ordered sets — no TLPs or DLLPs flow while the LTSSM is in Recovery.

The fields that matter in equalization TS1s, by name: the Equalization Control (EC) field (which phase the sender is in), Transmitter Preset, Use Preset (whether the request is a preset or explicit coefficients), the pre-cursor, cursor and post-cursor coefficients (or FS and LF in Phase 1), and Reject Coefficient Values. Uncertain on symbol and bit positions — use your analyzer's decode or the TS1/TS2 ordered set tables in the base specification rather than raw byte offsets.

Equalization can be re-requested after link-up: a port can set a Link Equalization Request bit, and software can set Perform Equalization in Link Control 3 and then retrain the link. Re-equalizing a live link interrupts traffic while the LTSSM is in Recovery; TLPs waiting in buffers are delayed, not lost, provided the link returns to L0 before higher-level timers (completion timeout, NVMe command timeout) expire.

The link equalized successfully at boot, but the chosen settings leave too little margin once the channel warms (loss increases with temperature). Evidence to collect: AER correctable counters for Receiver Error, Bad TLP, Bad DLLP on the root port (the receiver of this direction); LMR at cool and warm temperatures; whether forcing re-equalization when warm (Perform Equalization + retrain) reduces errors. Corrective options: platform preset table change, retimer, better airflow, or a channel redesign. A software timeout increase only hides it.

Preset to coefficient converter (runnable)

This script converts preset dB targets into normalized coefficients and checks the inverse. It is executable with the Python standard library.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""preset_coeffs.py - convert transmitter preshoot/de-emphasis (dB) into
normalized 3-tap FIR coefficients (C-1, C0, C+1), with |C-1| + C0 + |C+1| = 1.

Definitions used (voltage levels of a 3-tap FIR driver):
  Vd = C0 + |C-1| + |C+1|  (= 1, full swing)
  Va = C0 - |C-1| - |C+1|  (steady-state level, long run of same bits)
  Vb = C0 - |C-1| + |C+1|  (first bit after a transition)
  Vc = C0 + |C-1| - |C+1|  (bit just before a transition)
  preshoot     = 20*log10(Vc/Va)
  de-emphasis  = 20*log10(Va/Vb)   (zero or negative)
Teaching tool: the preset dB values below are the commonly published
PCIe 8.0 GT/s+ preset targets; confirm them in the specification.
"""
import math

PRESETS = {  # name: (de-emphasis dB, preshoot dB)
    "P0": (-6.0, 0.0), "P1": (-3.5, 0.0), "P2": (-4.4, 0.0), "P3": (-2.5, 0.0),
    "P4": (0.0, 0.0),  "P5": (0.0, 1.9),  "P6": (0.0, 2.5),  "P7": (-6.0, 3.5),
    "P8": (-3.5, 3.5), "P9": (0.0, 3.5),
}

def coeffs(deemph_db, preshoot_db):
    d = 10 ** (deemph_db / 20)        # Va/Vb, <= 1
    p = 10 ** (preshoot_db / 20)      # Vc/Va, >= 1
    u = 1 / (p * d + 1 - d)           # u = 1 - 2|C-1| = Vb
    pre = (1 - u) / 2                 # |C-1|
    post = u * (1 - d) / 2            # |C+1|
    main = 1 - pre - post             # C0
    return -pre, main, -post

def check(cm1, c0, cp1):
    a, b = abs(cm1), abs(cp1)
    va, vb, vc = c0 - a - b, c0 - a + b, c0 + a - b
    return 20 * math.log10(va / vb), 20 * math.log10(vc / va), va

def z(x):
    return 0.0 if abs(x) < 5e-4 else x

def main():
    print(f"{'preset':6} {'deemph':>7} {'presh':>6} {'C-1':>7} {'C0':>6} {'C+1':>7} {'Va/Vd':>6}")
    for name, (de, ps) in PRESETS.items():
        cm1, c0, cp1 = coeffs(de, ps)
        de2, ps2, va = check(cm1, c0, cp1)
        assert abs(de2 - de) < 1e-6 and abs(ps2 - ps) < 1e-6
        print(f"{name:6} {de:>6.1f}dB {ps:>4.1f}dB {z(cm1):>7.3f} {c0:>6.3f} {z(cp1):>7.3f} {va:>6.3f}")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Margining result analysis (runnable, synthetic data)

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""margin_report.py - turn Lane Margining at the Receiver step results into
margins, and flag weak lanes. Input data are SYNTHETIC (teaching example).

For each lane we record the last timing step (left/right) and voltage step
(up/down) reached before the receiver's error count exceeded the limit.
The receiver reports how many steps it supports and the maximum offset
those steps span (Report Num/Max ... commands); we convert steps to %UI / mV.
"""

# Synthetic receiver capabilities (as if read back with the "Report" commands)
NUM_TIMING_STEPS = 16        # steps from centre to the max timing offset
MAX_TIMING_OFFSET_UI = 0.50  # max offset reached at the last step, in UI
NUM_VOLTAGE_STEPS = 64
MAX_VOLTAGE_OFFSET_MV = 200

# Synthetic results for the Lab SSD's x4 link at 16 GT/s (root-port receiver)
RESULTS = {  # lane: (left_steps, right_steps, up_steps, down_steps)
    0: (9, 10, 22, 21),
    1: (9, 9, 21, 22),
    2: (4, 5, 9, 8),      # weak lane
    3: (10, 9, 23, 22),
}

# Illustrative pass criteria (a platform team would set its own)
MIN_EYE_WIDTH_UI = 0.30
MIN_EYE_HEIGHT_MV = 30

def main():
    t_step = MAX_TIMING_OFFSET_UI / NUM_TIMING_STEPS
    v_step = MAX_VOLTAGE_OFFSET_MV / NUM_VOLTAGE_STEPS
    widths = {}
    print("lane  left(UI) right(UI) width(UI)  up(mV) down(mV) height(mV)  verdict")
    for lane, (l, r, u, d) in sorted(RESULTS.items()):
        width = (l + r) * t_step
        height = (u + d) * v_step
        widths[lane] = width
        ok = width >= MIN_EYE_WIDTH_UI and height >= MIN_EYE_HEIGHT_MV
        print(f"{lane:>4}  {l*t_step:8.3f} {r*t_step:9.3f} {width:9.3f}  "
              f"{u*v_step:6.1f} {d*v_step:8.1f} {height:10.1f}  {'pass' if ok else 'FAIL'}")
    med = sorted(widths.values())[len(widths) // 2]
    for lane, w in widths.items():
        if w < 0.6 * med:
            print(f"lane {lane}: eye width {w:.3f} UI is < 60% of the median "
                  f"{med:.3f} UI -> suspect lane-specific channel or equalization")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Equalization phase logic for a downstream port (pseudocode)

/* Conceptual DSP behaviour for one rate. Not normative; timers and exits per spec. */
phase0_at_lower_rate:   send EQ TS2 with usp_tx_preset[lane] from Lane Equalization Control
change_rate();
phase1:                 tx_use(dsp_tx_preset[lane]); exchange TS1 EC=01 (FS, LF, preset)
                        if (!all_lanes_locked()) goto fail_rate;
                        if (policy_skip_phase23) goto done;
phase2:                 /* USP drives requests; DSP applies within FS/LF or rejects */
                        while (usp_requests() && !timeout(PHASE2))
                            apply_or_reject(request);
phase3:                 /* DSP's receiver searches USP Tx settings */
                        for each lane: best = search(candidates, figure_of_merit, PHASE3 timer);
                        request(best);
done:                   record status bits (complete, phase1/2/3 successful)
                        go to Recovery.RcvrLock;
fail_rate:              record status; fall back to a lower rate

Worked example

Example 1 — Coefficients in transmitter units

A transmitter advertises FS = 48, LF = 16. The receiver wants a setting close to P7 (C−1 = −0.100, C0 = 0.701, C+1 = −0.200 normalized).

  1. Scale by FS: |C−1| = 4.8 → 5; C0 = 33.6 → 34; |C+1| = 9.6 → 10. Sum 49 > 48, so round C0 down to 33 to satisfy the full-swing rule: 5 + 33 + 10 = 48.
  2. Check LF: C0 − |C−1| − |C+1| = 33 − 5 − 10 = 18 ≥ 16. Valid.
  3. A request of 8 / 30 / 10 would give 30 − 8 − 10 = 12 < LF = 16: the transmitter rejects it.

Derived: the rounding is illustrative; the exact validity rules (including the pre-cursor limit relative to FS) are in the base specification.

Example 2 — Why PAM4 needs FEC

For the same peak-to-peak swing, PAM4 stacks three eyes where NRZ has one, so each eye is about one third as tall: 20 log10(3) ≈ 9.5 dB less vertical margin. Even with equalization, the raw error rate rises by many orders of magnitude. Gen6 therefore targets a first-bit error rate around 10−6 and relies on FEC plus CRC-protected flit retry to deliver an effective error rate comparable to earlier generations. Derived from the signalling, with the BER figure as commonly described for Revision 6.0.

Example 3 — Reading margins

From the margin_report.py output: lanes 0, 1 and 3 have eye widths around 0.56–0.59 UI at the receiver; lane 2 has 0.28 UI. At 16 GT/s, one UI is 62.5 ps, so lane 2's eye is about 17.5 ps wide versus about 37 ps for the others. Same receiver design, same SSD, same firmware: the difference is the channel. Combined with Trace 2, the conclusion is lane 2 of that bay. Confidence: medium-high; confirm by moving the drive.

Common mistakes

  • Blaming the SSD for a downtrained link without checking the slot, riser, cable, retimer and the other end's status bits.
  • Reading Phase 2 and Phase 3 the wrong way round. Phase 2 tunes the downstream port's transmitter (host→device direction); Phase 3 tunes the upstream port's (device→host).
  • Trusting LnkSta2 alone for Gen4/Gen5. Link Status 2 equalization bits describe 8 GT/s; 16 and 32 GT/s have their own status in rate-specific capabilities.
  • Treating "Equalization Complete" as "healthy." Completion says the procedure finished, not that margin is adequate.
  • Margining a production system under load. It can drop a marginal link.
  • Reading correctable errors on the wrong device. Errors are logged by the receiver; SSD→host problems appear at the root port.
  • Ignoring temperature. Equalization happens once; the channel changes afterwards.
  • Equating link speed with performance. A link at Gen4 with a NAK storm can be slower than a clean Gen3 link.

Senior engineer insight

  • Localize along four axes: direction, rate, lane, and location (drive vs slot). Most physical problems collapse to one cell of that grid, and the cell tells you who owns the fix.
  • Correctable errors are a budget, not noise. A healthy Gen4 link should log essentially none over hours. A steady trickle is a margin problem waiting for a hot day.
  • Margining makes arguments short. "Lane 2 has half the eye width of its neighbours at the same receiver" ends debates between board, drive and platform teams.
  • Firmware preset tables are code. They need version control, per-slot validation, and regression testing when a board revision changes.
  • For Gen6, read FEC statistics. Corrected FEC symbols are normal; the trend and the uncorrectable flit rate are what matter.
  • Never let a timeout increase close a physical-layer bug. It moves the failure to a worse place.

Background

Errors are reported by the receiver of the failing direction; margining measures one receiver at a time; equalization status records the last procedure's outcome per rate. Each source covers a different part of the grid.

Step 1 — Inventory your link

Run the Linux view commands for your SSD and its root port. Record capable vs current speed and width, LnkSta2 bits, and whether lspci decodes Physical Layer 16.0 GT/s and Lane Margining capabilities.

Step 2 — Analyze synthetic margins

Run margin_report.py. Then change lane 2 to (8, 9, 20, 21) and lane 0 to (3, 3, 6, 6) and re-run. Write down which lane fails and why the median rule flags it.

Step 3 — Correlate with traces

Using Trace 2 (Phase 3 fails on lane 2 at 16 GT/s) and the original margin data (measured at the root port's receivers at 16 GT/s on another, healthier unit of the same model), write a localization statement.

Step 4 (optional, Caution) — Real margining

On a test system only, with I/O stopped, run pcilmr against your root port per its manual. Record per-lane timing and voltage margins and compare lanes. Do not change any thresholds or keep margining running in the background.

Record the margin spread across lanes, any lane-specific outlier, and whether your real link's error counters are zero.

What a senior engineer notices

That margining at the root port says nothing about the SSD's receiver; that the synthetic step sizes come from the receiver's own report (so different receivers' numbers are not comparable); and that a single outlier lane is far more informative than an overall low margin.

Failure variants

  • All lanes equally low: suspect the channel length or a missing retimer, not a defect.
  • Timing margin fine, voltage margin poor: suspect amplitude loss (connector contact) or noise coupling.
  • Margins good, errors still present: suspect reference clock issues or crosstalk under traffic (margining may run with different traffic patterns).

Exercises

  1. Compute the unit interval and Nyquist frequency for 32 GT/s NRZ and for 64 GT/s PAM4.
  2. A transmitter advertises FS = 40, LF = 10. Is C−1 = 6, C0 = 26, C+1 = 8 valid? Is 4 / 22 / 14?
  3. Explain why a DFE cannot replace transmitter preshoot.
  4. Your link is at Gen4 x4 with EqualizationPhase3+ in LnkSta2, yet it fails at 16 GT/s with errors. What did you forget to check?
  5. Describe the evidence that distinguishes a lane that failed Detect from a lane that failed 16 GT/s equalization.
Answers

1. 32 GT/s NRZ: UI = 31.25 ps, Nyquist = 16 GHz. 64 GT/s PAM4: 32 Gbaud, symbol interval 31.25 ps, Nyquist 16 GHz.

2. 6 + 26 + 8 = 40 = FS; 26 − 6 − 8 = 12 ≥ 10: valid. 4 + 22 + 14 = 40; 22 − 4 − 14 = 4 < 10: invalid (rejected).

3. A DFE subtracts tails of bits already decided (post-cursor ISI). Pre-cursor ISI comes from the next, not-yet-decided bit; only a feed-forward filter (transmitter pre-cursor or receiver FFE) can address it.

4. LnkSta2 reports 8 GT/s equalization. 16 GT/s status is in the Physical Layer 16.0 GT/s capability.

5. A Detect failure: the lane never participates in Polling/Configuration at 2.5 GT/s (seen from power-on capture; link width already reduced before any speed change). An equalization failure: the lane participates at lower rates and drops only during or after Recovery.Equalization at the higher rate, with phase status bits showing failure.

Quiz

  1. In which equalization phase does the Upstream Port tune the Downstream Port's transmitter?

    • Phase 0
    • Phase 1
    • Phase 2
    • Phase 3
    Answer and reasoning

    Phase 2. The USP's receiver judges the DSP's transmitter. Phase 3 is the mirror (DSP tunes USP transmitter).

  2. Phase 0 takes place at which data rate?

    • The lower, current rate, before switching
    • The new, higher rate
    • Always 2.5 GT/s
    Answer and reasoning

    Phase 0 communicates presets in EQ TS2s at the current rate so the USP can start the new rate with a chosen preset. For 8 GT/s that is 2.5 or 5 GT/s; for 16 GT/s the presets travel at 8 GT/s.

  3. Which coefficient produces preshoot?

    • C−1 (pre-cursor)
    • C0 (main cursor)
    • C+1 (post-cursor)
    Answer and reasoning

    The pre-cursor tap, applied to the upcoming bit, raises the level of the bit just before a transition.

  4. Which revision introduced Lane Margining at the Receiver?

    • 3.0
    • 4.0
    • 5.0
    • 6.0
    Answer and reasoning

    Revision 4.0, required for ports supporting 16 GT/s and higher.

  5. Errors in the SSD→host direction are logged as correctable errors by which device?

    • The SSD
    • The root port
    • Both equally
    Answer and reasoning

    The receiver detects the error; for SSD→host traffic, that is the root port.

  6. Why does Gen6 use FEC when earlier generations did not?

    Model answer

    PAM4 has roughly a third of the NRZ eye height per eye, so the raw bit error rate at 64 GT/s is far higher than NRZ links could tolerate. Gen6 accepts a higher raw error rate and corrects it with lightweight FEC inside fixed-size flits, retrying flits that FEC cannot fix.

  7. A link shows EqualizationComplete+ and full speed but logs Bad TLP correctable errors every few seconds. Is equalization "fine"?

    Model answer

    No. Completion says the procedure ended, not that margin is adequate. The errors show the receiver is operating near its limit. Measure margins and correlate with temperature, traffic and the neighbouring links.

  8. What does an LF advertisement constrain?

    • The maximum preshoot in dB
    • The number of presets supported
    • The minimum of C0 − |C−1| − |C+1|, i.e. how far the low-frequency level may be reduced
    • The lowest data rate supported
    Answer and reasoning

    LF bounds the steady-state level; requests below it are rejected.

  9. Name three link-level symptoms of a marginal channel, in increasing severity.

    Model answer

    Correctable errors with replays (Bad TLP/Bad DLLP/Receiver Error); Recovery entries and possibly Replay Timer Timeout / REPLAY_NUM Rollover; link retraining to lower speed or width; finally link down with Surprise Down, DPC or device loss.

Interview questions

  1. Beginner: Why is equalization needed at 8 GT/s but not at 2.5 GT/s?

    Model answer

    At 8 GT/s the bit time is 125 ps and channel loss at 4 GHz smears each bit across several neighbours; fixed de-emphasis cannot correct channel-specific ISI. At 2.5 GT/s, bits are long enough that fixed de-emphasis suffices.

  2. Beginner: What is a preset?

    Model answer

    One of eleven defined transmitter settings (P0–P10), each a target de-emphasis and preshoot, used as starting points and common results of equalization.

  3. Intermediate: Walk through Phases 0–3.

    Model answer

    Phase 0 at the lower rate: DSP tells USP which transmitter preset to use. Phase 1 at the new rate: both transmit with initial presets and exchange FS/LF, reaching enough quality to communicate. Phase 2: USP requests settings for the DSP transmitter until satisfied. Phase 3: DSP requests settings for the USP transmitter. Then Recovery completes and the link returns to L0.

  4. Intermediate: How do you tell which direction of a link has a problem?

    Model answer

    Check which receiver logs errors (AER on the receiving device), which equalization phase failed (2 = host→device, 3 = device→host), and margin each receiver separately.

  5. Advanced: What does Lane Margining at the Receiver measure, and what are its limits?

    Model answer

    It measures how far a receiver's sampling point can be moved in time and voltage before errors exceed a limit, per lane, on a live link, after the receiver's own equalization. Limits: it measures one receiver at its current settings and conditions; step sizes are receiver-specific; it does not show the eye at the pins; it can disturb a marginal link.

  6. Advanced: Why does Gen6 keep the Gen5 Nyquist frequency?

    Model answer

    By using PAM4 (2 bits per symbol) at 32 Gbaud, Gen6 doubles throughput without doubling channel frequency content, avoiding much higher loss; the cost is reduced eye height, mitigated by FEC and flit-level retry.

  7. Senior: A fleet shows NVMe timeouts on drives in bay 7 of one server model only. How do you investigate?

    Model answer

    Collect AER history (root port correctable errors before each event), link speed/width history, DPC/Surprise Down events, temperature. Compare equalization status and margins for bay 7 vs other bays on several units. Swap drives between bays to separate drive from slot. If it follows the bay, review the backplane channel, retimer config and preset table for bay 7; correlate with airflow. Propose corrective action (preset change, retimer firmware, hardware fix) and a regression test with margining at temperature.

  8. Senior: What equalization-related items do you put in a validation plan for a new SSD?

    Model answer

    Equalization success at each supported rate across worst-case and best-case channels, with and without retimers, across temperature and voltage corners; response to all preset requests and coefficient requests including rejection of illegal ones; re-equalization on request; LMR command support and accuracy; interoperability with multiple root complexes and switches; error-free soak with correctable error counters monitored.

Teach-back challenge

  • To a child: explain why two friends need to practise before they can talk fast across an echoing hall, and why they take turns coaching.
  • To a junior engineer: explain what Phase 2 and Phase 3 do, and how to tell from Linux which direction is having errors.

Model explanations

Child: "In an echoing hall, fast words blur. So you practise: your friend tells you how to say things so they come out clear, then you help your friend. Every hall echoes differently, so you practise again in every new hall."

Junior: "Phase 2: the SSD listens to the root port and asks it to adjust its transmitter, fixing the host-to-SSD direction. Phase 3: the root port listens to the SSD and adjusts the SSD's transmitter, fixing SSD-to-host. In Linux, errors are counted by the receiver: if the root port's AER correctable counters rise, the SSD-to-host direction is the problem."

Senior: "Phase 3 at 16 GT/s failed only on lane 2, and Phase 2 succeeded on all lanes — so it is SSD Tx → root port Rx, lane 2, at 16 GT/s. Margins at the same root port receiver show lane 2 at half the eye width of its neighbours. Firmware tables are shared across lanes, so the lane-specific failure points to the physical path. Confidence medium; moving the drive to another bay and seeing the failure stay with bay 7 lane 2 would make it high."

Chapter summary

Above 5 GT/s, each lane's channel smears bits into each other. PCIe answers with a 3-tap transmitter FIR whose settings are negotiated per lane, per rate and per direction in four phases: presets announced at the lower rate (Phase 0), a working link at the new rate (Phase 1), the upstream port tuning the downstream transmitter (Phase 2) and the downstream port tuning the upstream transmitter (Phase 3). Presets P0–P10 are named combinations of de-emphasis and preshoot; FS and LF bound the coefficients. Gen4 and Gen5 repeat the procedure at each new rate; Gen6 switches to PAM4 with FEC in Flit mode. Lane Margining at the Receiver quantifies how much margin is left. Signal-integrity problems climb the stack from correctable errors to replays, Recovery loops, downtraining and link loss — and must be localized by direction, rate, lane and location.

Do not move on until you can:

  • explain ISI and why it worsens with each generation
  • convert a preset to coefficients and check a coefficient request against FS and LF
  • state which port tunes which transmitter in Phases 2 and 3
  • find equalization status for 8, 16 and 32 GT/s in configuration space
  • explain what Lane Margining at the Receiver measures and why it is a caution-level operation
  • map a symptom (downtrain, Recovery loop, correctable errors, link drop) to its likely physical cause and the next experiment
