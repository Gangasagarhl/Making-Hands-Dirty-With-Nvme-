Why this part exists

A senior engineer is not just someone who knows more. They are someone whose knowledge others can rely on: they explain clearly to whoever is listening, review designs by asking the right "why not?", reason with explicit models rather than intuition alone, protect hardware and data, and refuse conclusions the evidence cannot carry. Each of those is a learnable practice. This part teaches sections 76 to 90 of the course's master specification as practices you rehearse.

The story

A doctor finds out why a child has a tummy ache. To the child she says, "Your tummy had a little fight with some old milk, and it's winning." To the parents she says what to give and when to call back. To another doctor she says the test results and the medicine dose. Same tummy ache, three explanations — and none of them is a lie.

A structural engineer on a bridge project switches between a ten-second answer to the mayor ("safe, on schedule"), a load calculation for a colleague, and a trade-off memo comparing steel and concrete. They also carry formal models (load paths, failure modes) and safety rules (never remove a support to "see what happens") that everybody on site follows.

Bridges change slowly and their physics is public. Storage systems change with every firmware release, and much of their behaviour is proprietary and invisible. Your models therefore need explicit edges ("this is a teaching model", "this is implementation-dependent") that a bridge engineer's models rarely need.

Communication skill: one bug, five audiences

The facts never change between audiences; the level of detail, the vocabulary and the decision being supported do. Here is the split-completion bug from the forensic report lab, explained five ways.

To a manager — 30 seconds

"Firmware 2.7 on our SSDs rejects some writes when the data sits at certain memory alignments; it affects our database hosts. We have a safe workaround — staying on firmware 2.6 — and the vendor has our evidence. Risk is contained if we hold the rollout; I'll update you when the vendor confirms the fix."

Contains: impact, scope, action, decision needed (hold rollout). Omits: every protocol term.

To a firmware engineer — 2 minutes

"Your requester logic flags an Unexpected Completion on the third Completion of a split read. Repro: Memory Read, 512 bytes from an address at offset 0x20 — the root complex splits it at the 64-byte Read Completion Boundary into 32, 256 and 224 bytes, Byte Count 512, 480, 224. The third completion (Tag 0x33, Byte Count 224, Lower Address 0x40) is the one in your Header Log. Every misaligned buffer fails, every aligned one passes, and 2.6 has no failures. My guess is that tag retirement or remaining-byte tracking mis-handles a first fragment shorter than the boundary. Trace and AER snapshot attached."

Contains: exact reproduction, field values, the hypothesis about their code, evidence. Omits: business impact.

To a PCIe engineer — 5 minutes

Adds: why the split is legal (completion boundary rules, Byte Count and Lower Address semantics, MPS 256 limiting each completion), why the Unexpected Completion is the device's detection rather than a root-complex fault, how error messaging depends on the advisory non-fatal rules, and the experiment that would confirm: drive the same read from an exerciser with different split patterns.

To a storage architect — 10 minutes

Adds: blast radius (which hosts allocate unaligned buffers — direct I/O users, some databases, user-space drivers), why kernel page-cache I/O is unaffected (page-aligned buffers), whether data integrity is at risk (no: the command fails loudly; no silent corruption observed), the qualification gap that let it through (no test varied buffer alignment), and the process change (add alignment sweep to firmware qualification).

To a customer — without unnecessary protocol detail

"We identified an issue in SSD firmware version 2.7 where certain write operations from some applications fail with an error. No data is silently corrupted: affected writes report an error to the application. Please remain on firmware 2.6, which is not affected. We are working with the drive vendor on a corrected release and will notify you when it is qualified."

The customer version is not the manager version with fewer words. It answers their questions — is my data safe, what should I do, when will it be fixed — and promises only what you control.

Write the five versions for the lost-interrupt case in the root-cause template section. Check that every fact in the short versions is supported by the long ones.

Model answer (manager and customer)

Manager: "About once a day, one storage request on the database hosts stalls for 30 seconds because a power-saving setting interferes with how the drive signals 'done'. The data is safe. Turning off that setting stops it; we're confirming the exact cause with one more test this week." Customer: "Occasionally a storage operation may pause for up to 30 seconds and then complete normally. Design review training: "Would you design it this way?"

A design review is not a hunt for mistakes; it is a structured search for the alternatives the author did not consider, and an honest comparison. The habit: for each design decision, name at least one alternative and ask what it would cost and buy.

Worked review: a low-latency key-value cache on local NVMe

"Why not?" training

For every design, ask "why not the obvious alternative?". Try each before opening the answer.

  1. Why not SATA?

    Answer

    SATA's 6 Gb/s link and the AHCI host model with a single command queue of 32 entries cannot feed modern flash; NVMe over PCIe gives many deep queues and a multi-lane link. SATA remains fine for cost-sensitive capacity where bandwidth is not the limit. See From SATA/SAS to NVMe.

  2. Why not AHCI (with a faster link)?

    Answer

    AHCI was designed for rotating disks: one queue, register-heavy command issue, uncacheable register reads in the path. The bottleneck is the host interface model, not just the link.

  3. Why not polling everywhere?

    Answer

    It burns CPU and power when idle and prevents CPUs sleeping; it wins only under sustained load with latency targets.

  4. Why not interrupts everywhere?

    Answer

    At millions of IOPS, per-completion interrupts cost significant CPU and add latency; coalescing or polling help.

  5. Why not deeper queues?

    Answer

    Beyond the depth that saturates the device, extra depth only adds waiting time (Little's law: more in flight at the same throughput means longer latency) and memory.

  6. Why not one queue?

    Answer

    All CPUs would contend for one tail pointer and doorbell, requiring locks; no isolation between workloads.

  7. Why not one interrupt?

    Answer

    All completions would land on one CPU, destroying locality and creating a bottleneck; MSI-X allows a vector per queue for that reason.

  8. Why not TCP (for local storage)?

    Answer

    A local device needs no network; TCP adds latency and CPU. TCP is the answer when storage must be remote and the network is ordinary IP.

  9. Why not RDMA (everywhere)?

    Answer

    It needs special NICs and careful network configuration; operational complexity and cost must be justified by latency/CPU needs.

  10. Why not PCIe x16 for an SSD?

    Answer

    A single SSD's media and controller usually cannot use x16 bandwidth; lanes are a scarce platform resource; connectors and form factors (M.2, U.2/U.3, E1/E3) are typically x4. Exceptions exist for special accelerators.

  11. Why not x4 for everything?

    Answer

    Some devices (GPUs, large-scale accelerators, multi-controller cards) need more bandwidth than x4 at the available generation; and x2/x1 can suffice for boot devices, saving lanes.

  12. Why not larger transfers?

    Answer

    Transfers are capped by MDTS; larger I/Os increase latency for each request, can hurt fairness, need bigger PRP lists, and may not improve throughput once the link or media saturates.

Ten formal mental models

A formal mental model is a model you can write down: its stages, its invariants (things that must always be true), the evidence each stage leaves, and the signature of each failure. Intuition is fast; models are checkable. The ten below are teaching models Derived: they are consistent with the specifications but are not themselves normative state machines.

Model answer (model 6, reset)

Trigger fails: CC.EN write never seen on the wire (driver bug). Quiesce fails: controller continues DMA after EN=0 (late CQE in old queue memory). State clearing incomplete: RDY never goes 0 within CAP.TO. Re-initialisation fails: admin commands time out (wrong ASQ/ACQ, stale IOMMU mapping). Resume fails: I/O queues re-created but old commands not requeued (lost I/O).

The command lifecycle

This is a teaching model. The NVMe specifications do not define these eleven stages as a controller state machine, and a controller may overlap, reorder or merge stages internally (for example starting data transfer before the media operation completes for a write into a cache). Use it to organise evidence, not to claim compliance.

A letter's journey: you write it, put it in the outbox, ring the bell, the postman collects it, reads the address, does the job, writes back, puts the reply in your inbox and knocks; you read it and throw away the envelope.

A command is built, placed in a submission queue, announced with a doorbell, fetched, executed, completed with a completion queue entry, and signalled with an interrupt.

The CPU writes 64 bytes into DRAM and a 32-bit register on the SSD; the SSD's DMA engine reads the 64 bytes, moves data, writes 16 bytes back to DRAM and a 32-bit interrupt message to the interrupt controller.

The NVMe Base Specification defines the SQE and CQE formats, queue semantics and doorbells (with the PCIe Transport specification); the command set specification defines the command; the PCI Express Base Specification defines MSI-X. The eleven stages are a derived model over those definitions.

On the wire: MWr (doorbell) → MRd + CplD (fetch) → data TLPs → MWr (CQE) → MWr (MSI-X) → MWr (CQ head doorbell).

In an analyzer, each stage after "Submitted" corresponds to a recognisable TLP except Executing, Media and Completed, which appear only as time gaps.

Debugging: find the last stage with evidence; the failure lies in the next stage or in the agent responsible for it.

The PCIe transaction lifecycle

A conceptual model, not a literal internal implementation. Real devices pipeline, buffer and reorder within the rules; the root complex may split one CPU access into several TLPs or combine accesses; switches add their own buffering. The stages describe what must logically happen, not how silicon does it.

The two lifecycles meet in one place: every NVMe stage that moves bytes (fetch, data transfer, CQE post, interrupt) is one or more PCIe transactions. An NVMe CQE is a payload of a posted PCIe Memory Write; a PCIe Completion TLP is the response to a non-posted request. The Command vs Packet vs Completion appendix draws the full map.

The debugging decision tree

This is a diagnostic heuristic, not an absolute rule. It orders where to look first by layer, cheapest and most fundamental first. It misleads when a higher layer causes a lower-layer symptom (a driver that disables a link, firmware that sets a bad power state), and when failures are intermittent — a healthy link now does not prove it was healthy at the failure.

Read it as: if the link is down, inspect the LTSSM and the link; if the link is healthy, inspect PCIe transactions; if transactions are healthy, inspect NVMe queues; if queues are healthy, inspect command status; if status is successful, inspect interrupt and completion handling; if the completion exists, inspect software processing.

Where the heuristic misleads — three examples

  • Intermittent link problems. The link is in L0 now; it was in Recovery during the failure. Check error counters and the timeline, not the current state.
  • Top-down causes of bottom-layer symptoms. A driver's runtime power management puts the device in D3cold; the "link down" box is where you start, but the cause is in the last box.
  • Healthy everything, wrong result. Every box answers "yes" for the overlapping-writes case in multi-layer failure. The tree does not cover contract misuse; ask "was the interface used as specified?" separately.

Safe hardware practice

Every hardware lab — in this course and in your career — states three things before it starts:

The three classification axes for any hardware procedure
AxisValuesQuestion to ask
Destructive?Destructive / non-destructiveCan this lose data or damage hardware if it goes as planned? If it goes wrong?
AccessRead-only / write-capableDoes any command write to a device, register, namespace, firmware or configuration space?
EnvironmentProduction-safe / lab-onlyWould I run this on a system serving users? If not, it is lab-only.
  • Never blindly write controller registers ("let's set CC to see what happens").
  • Never format, sanitize or delete namespaces on a production device — or on any device without verifying its identity (serial number, not /dev name, which can change across boots).
  • Never run commands that destroy data without a verified backup and a written reason.
  • Never alter firmware (download, commit, activate) outside a lab with recovery means.
  • Never modify PCIe configuration space (setpci writes) without understanding the consequences: a wrong write can hang the platform or disable a device until reboot.

Data safety: sharing evidence responsibly

Real traces and logs contain more than protocol. Before sharing outside your team — with a vendor, in a public bug report, in a course — redact:

  • Serial numbers (drive, system, board) — they identify customers and assets.
  • PCIe identifiers if sensitive — subsystem IDs can reveal unreleased products.
  • Hostnames, IP addresses, NVMe Qualified Names (NQNs) — they reveal infrastructure.
  • Paths — usernames, project and customer names hide in them.
  • Proprietary payloads — data TLP contents and buffer dumps can contain customer data, keys or firmware.
  • Customer information of any kind.

What can usually be shared

TLP headers without payloads, NVMe command fields (opcode, CID, SLBA, NLB, NSID), status codes, register values, timing, link parameters, firmware revision strings (unless unreleased), and anonymised topology. Replace each redacted value with a consistent token, so that "the same serial number appears in two logs" survives redaction.

"""redact.py - redact a log or decoded trace before sharing (Executable, standard library).
Review the output by eye: no pattern list catches everything."""
import re, sys, hashlib

def token(kind, value):
    # Same input -> same token, so correlations survive redaction.
    return f"<{kind}-{hashlib.sha256(value.encode()).hexdigest()[:6]}>"

RULES = [
    ("SERIAL",   re.compile(r"(?i)\b(sn|serial(?: number)?)\s*[:=]\s*(\S+)"), 2),
    ("HOST",     re.compile(r"\b((?:[a-z0-9-]+\.)+(?:corp|lan|internal)\.[a-z]+)\b"), 1),
    ("IP",       re.compile(r"\b(\d{1,3}(?:\.\d{1,3}){3})\b"), 1),
    ("NQN",      re.compile(r"\b(nqn\.\d{4}-\d{2}\.[^\s,;]+)"), 1),
    ("PATH",     re.compile(r"(/home/[^\s:]+|/srv/[^\s:]+)"), 1),
    ("PAYLOAD",  re.compile(r"(?i)\bdata=((?:[0-9a-f]{2}){16,})"), 1),
]

def redact(line):
    for kind, rx, grp in RULES:
        line = rx.sub(lambda m: m.group(0).replace(m.group(grp), token(kind, m.group(grp))), line)
    return line

SAMPLE = """\
db7.prod.corp.com kernel: nvme nvme0: pci function 0000:01:00.0
nvme id-ctrl: sn : S5XYNA0R123456  mn : Lab SSD 1TB  fr : 2.7
connect to 10.20.30.40 subnqn nqn.2014-08.com.example:target01 failed
fio --filename=/home/alice/projects/customerX/test.img
trace row 41: MWr64 Addr=0x2_0000_3000 data=0123456789abcdef0123456789abcdef0123
"""

if __name__ == "__main__":
    text = "" if sys.stdin.isatty() else sys.stdin.read()
    text = text if text.strip() else SAMPLE      # no input: redact the built-in sample
    for line in text.splitlines():
        print(redact(line))

Run with no input to redact the built-in sample, or pipe a log in: python3 redact.py < dmesg.txt. The model name ("Lab SSD 1TB") and the PCIe address are deliberately left — decide per case whether they are sensitive. No pattern list is complete; always read the output.

Performance causality

Correlation is not causation. If latency rises after a PCIe event, do not conclude the PCIe event caused it without evidence.

Worked case

A dashboard shows p99 read latency rising from 90 µs to 2 ms at 14:02:10. The AER log shows a burst of correctable errors (Replay Timer Timeout) at 14:02:09. Tempting conclusion: "link errors caused the latency".

Testing the causal claim
TestIf PCIe errors cause the latency…Observed (synthetic)Verdict
MagnitudeReplays cost microseconds each; thousands would be needed for 2 ms p9937 replays totalWeakens
Time courseLatency tracks error rateErrors stop at 14:02:11; latency stays high until 14:09Weakens
Common cause—Drive temperature crossed its throttling threshold at 14:02:05 (SMART temperature log); hot drives can also degrade signal marginsSupports a common cause (heat) for both
InterventionFixing the link removes the latencyIncreased airflow: both errors and latency disappearSupports heat as cause

Conclusion (HIGH): thermal throttling caused the latency; the correctable errors are a co-symptom of the same heat, not the cause of the latency. Four tests — magnitude, time course, common cause, intervention — are a reusable checklist for any "X happened, then Y" claim.

Storage media reality

The NVMe interface describes logical blocks. Flash stores data in pages and erase blocks with very different rules: pages are programmed once until their block is erased; erase blocks are large; cells wear out. A Flash Translation Layer (FTL) inside the controller maps one world to the other.

Host-visible protocol versus internal flash behaviour
Host-visible (protocol)Internal (usually not exposed)
Logical block address (LBA)NAND placement: which die, plane, block, page
Write command completesWhether data is in cache, SLC buffer, or final location
Dataset Management DeallocateWhen blocks are actually erased
Latency of a commandGarbage collection algorithm and timing
SMART Percentage Used, Data Units WrittenWear-levelling implementation, actual erase counts per block
Namespace capacityPhysical capacity and over-provisioning ratio

Some features expose a little more by design — for example endurance group information, and placement-related features that let a host influence where data goes (Flexible Data Placement in recent revisions Version-dependent — confirm the revision that incorporated it; Zoned Namespaces). Even these expose a contract, not the internal algorithm.

The specifications do not define NAND placement, FTL mapping, garbage-collection algorithms or wear-levelling implementations. These are implementation details, and they differ between products and firmware versions. Claims about them need vendor documentation or vendor telemetry.

Quiz

  1. Which audience version of a bug report should contain Byte Count and Lower Address values?

    • Manager
    • Firmware engineer
    • Customer
    • All of them
    Answer

    The firmware engineer needs exact reproduction details; the others need impact and action.

  2. Is the eleven-stage command lifecycle a normative controller state machine?

    Answer

    No. It is a teaching model over normative definitions; controllers may overlap or reorder stages internally.

  3. In the PCIe transaction lifecycle, where does a posted Memory Write end?

    • At requester matching
    • At completion
    • At delivery to (and acceptance by) the receiver
    • It never ends
    Answer

    No completion exists for posted requests.

  4. Name two situations where the debugging decision tree misleads.

    Answer

    Intermittent lower-layer problems that look healthy now; higher-layer causes of lower-layer symptoms (power management taking a link down); and contract misuse where every layer is healthy.

  5. Classify nvme format on the three axes.

    Answer

    Destructive; write-capable; lab-only (on a verified device).

  6. Why redact with consistent tokens rather than deleting values?

    Answer

    So correlations (same serial in two logs, same host) survive, which an analyst may need.

  7. Latency rises one second after a burst of correctable errors. Which test can directly show causation?

    • Time ordering
    • Same dashboard panel
    • Error count magnitude
    • An intervention that removes one and observes the other
    Answer

    Only an intervention (with other factors held fixed) tests causation; ordering and magnitude can only weaken or support.

  8. A benchmark reads 4 KiB at 14 GB/s from a Gen4 x4 SSD. What do you suspect first?

    Answer

    Cache effects (page cache — check direct I/O), since Gen4 x4 cannot carry about 14 GB/s (≈ 7.9 GB/s raw after encoding, less after protocol overhead).

  9. Can you infer the FTL mapping granularity from Write latency?

    Answer

    Not without evidence beyond standard behaviour; many internal factors produce similar latency patterns.

Interview questions

  1. (Intermediate) Explain interrupt coalescing and when you would use it.

    Model answer

    The controller delays interrupts to aggregate several completions per interrupt (by count or time threshold), reducing CPU cost at high IOPS at the expense of latency. Use for throughput-oriented workloads; avoid for latency-critical queues.

  2. (Advanced) Walk me through a design review of "one queue per core, QD 1,024".

    Model answer

    As in the worked review: question depth against measured in-flight depth, isolation needs, interrupt versus polling, kernel versus user space, transport; end with a recommendation tied to the latency/throughput requirement.

  3. (Senior) How do you avoid concluding causation from a dashboard?

    Model answer

    Check magnitude, time course, common causes, and run an intervention; state confidence; prefer controlled experiments.

  4. (Senior) What do you put in a trace you send to a vendor?

    Model answer

    Unfiltered window around the event, capture settings, analyzer software version, topology, firmware/driver versions, the first abnormal event marked — with serials, hostnames, paths and payloads redacted by consistent tokens.

  5. (Principal) When would you choose NVMe/TCP over a local SSD for a latency-sensitive service?

    Model answer

    When disaggregation benefits (capacity pooling, independent scaling, failure domains, utilisation) outweigh tens of microseconds of added latency and the service's latency budget allows it; with clear evidence from a trade-off table and a pilot measurement.

Teach-back challenge

  • To a child: why a detective says "these clues point here" instead of "I know who did it".
  • To a junior engineer: the command lifecycle and which stages appear in a trace.
  • To a senior engineer: where the debugging decision tree fails and what you do instead.
Model explanation (junior)

"A command goes through eleven stages in our teaching model. Five leave a mark on the PCIe link: the doorbell write, the SQE fetch and its completion, the data transfer, the CQE write, and the interrupt — plus the CQ head doorbell at the end. Executing, media and 'completed' are invisible: you see them only as gaps in time. When something hangs, find the last visible stage; the problem is in the next one or the agent that owns it."
