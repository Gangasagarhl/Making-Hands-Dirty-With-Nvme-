The Transaction Layer builds packets; the Data Link Layer makes sure every one of them crosses the link exactly once, in order, and undamaged. This chapter teaches the small 8-byte packets that make that possible — Data Link Layer Packets (DLLPs) — and the Ack/Nak replay machine that quietly repairs bit errors before any driver ever notices.

Why this chapter exists

When you look at a real PCI Express (PCIe) capture for the first time, the thing that surprises most people is not the Transaction Layer Packets (TLPs) — those you expected. It is the flood of tiny packets in between them: Acks, UpdateFCs, the occasional Nak, power-management handshakes. On a busy NVM Express (NVMe) link there can be as many DLLPs as TLPs. If you cannot read them, you cannot tell a healthy link from one that is silently retransmitting a few percent of its traffic, and you will misdiagnose a link-quality problem as an NVMe firmware problem.

The Data Link Layer is also where PCIe gets its reliability promise. The Physical Layer delivers bits with a small but non-zero bit error rate. The Transaction Layer assumes that every TLP it hands down arrives intact and in order at the neighbouring component. The Data Link Layer is the contract between those two worlds: it attaches a sequence number and a 32-bit Link Cyclic Redundancy Check (LCRC) to every TLP, keeps a copy in a replay buffer, and only throws the copy away when the receiver says "Ack".

Everything in this chapter is hop-by-hop: it happens between the two ends of one physical link (for our lab machine, between Root Port 0000:00:01.1 and the Lab SSD at 0000:01:00.0). It is not end-to-end through switches; that distinction will matter again in Chapter 16 on flow control and in Chapter 45 on PCIe errors.

The story

Imagine two friends, Ana and Ben, passing notes across a classroom through a line of other children. Ana writes a number on every note — note 1, note 2, note 3 — and she keeps a photocopy of each one in her pocket. When Ben gets a note, he checks two things: is the paper clean (no smudges hiding words), and is it the number he expected next? If yes, he holds up a green card with the number on it: "got up to 3!" Ana then throws away her photocopies of notes 1, 2 and 3. If a note arrives smudged, Ben holds up a red card: "I only have up to 2 — send again after that!" Ana pulls out her photocopies starting from note 3 and sends them again, in order. If Ana waits a long time and sees no card at all, she gets worried and sends her photocopies again anyway. If she has to resend the same notes again and again, she decides something is wrong with the line of children and asks everyone to stand up and line up again.

A courier depot ships parcels to a single partner depot across town on a dedicated van route. Every parcel gets a running number and a tamper seal (a checksum). The sending depot keeps a duplicate of every parcel on a shelf until the receiving depot confirms receipt. Confirmations are cumulative — a single note that says "received everything through number 1,207" clears every parcel up to 1,207 from the shelf. If a parcel arrives with a broken seal, the receiver sends back "resend after 1,206"; the sender reloads everything from 1,207 onwards from its shelf, in the original order. Separately, the two depots exchange small slips on the same van saying how much empty warehouse space they have left, so neither overwhelms the other. Those slips, the confirmations and the complaint notes are all short standard forms — never parcels themselves.

Where the analogy breaks. (1) The courier keeps physical duplicates for days; a PCIe transmitter keeps copies for well under a microsecond in normal operation and its shelf (the replay buffer) is small and fixed-size. (2) A courier may reorder deliveries; PCIe never delivers TLPs out of sequence order to the Transaction Layer — a receiver discards everything after a missing number rather than holding it. (3) The confirmation slips themselves can be damaged; a bad DLLP is simply dropped and the protocol relies on the next one (or a timer) — there is no "confirm the confirmation". (4) The analogy suggests a recipient at the far end of town; in PCIe the Ack comes from the adjacent component on the same link — a switch acknowledges the TLP even though the final destination is several hops away. (5) Real couriers lose parcels; the PCIe Data Link Layer never "gives up" on a TLP — it either delivers it or takes the link down.

Beginner explanation

PCIe is a stack of three layers on each side of a link. The Transaction Layer speaks in requests and completions: "read 512 bytes at this address", "here are the 256 bytes you asked for". The Physical Layer turns bytes into electrical symbols on lanes. In the middle is the Data Link Layer, whose single job is to make one link reliable.

It does that with two tools:

  1. Wrapping every TLP. When the Transaction Layer hands a TLP down, the Data Link Layer puts a 2-byte prefix in front (holding a 12-bit sequence number) and a 4-byte LCRC behind. It also keeps a copy.
  2. Sending its own little packets. The Data Link Layer on each side talks to its peer using DLLPs. They carry Acks ("I got everything up to sequence N"), Naks ("something after N went wrong, resend"), flow-control credit information and power-management handshakes.

A DLLP is tiny and fixed-size: one type byte, three bytes of payload, and a 16-bit CRC — six bytes — plus two bytes of framing on the wire. DLLPs never leave the link they were born on: a switch consumes DLLPs from one link and generates its own on the next. They are never forwarded, never routed, and they never reach software. They have no address, no requester ID and no tag.

When a TLP is damaged, the receiver does not try to repair it. It drops it, says Nak, and the transmitter resends from its copy. Software never sees this. The only traces it leaves are counters — the correctable error bits in AER — and slightly higher latency.

TLPs carry work between functions. DLLPs carry housekeeping between two neighbouring ports. If a packet has an address, a tag or a requester ID, it is a TLP. If it is 8 bytes on the wire and talks about sequence numbers or credits, it is a DLLP.

Engineering explanation

Where the Data Link Layer sits

On transmit, the Data Link Layer receives a TLP from the Transaction Layer, prepends a 16-bit field whose low 12 bits are the sequence number (the top four bits are reserved), appends a 32-bit LCRC computed over the sequence number field and the whole TLP, stores the result in the replay buffer and hands it to the Physical Layer. On receive, it checks the LCRC and the sequence number, strips both, and passes good TLPs up.

The LCRC is different from the optional End-to-End CRC (ECRC) you met in Chapter 11. The LCRC protects one hop and is regenerated by every switch port; the ECRC is computed by the original requester or completer and survives switches (if both ends support and enable it). A switch with a buggy internal datapath can corrupt a TLP and then attach a perfectly valid new LCRC — only ECRC (or data-integrity protection above PCIe, such as NVMe end-to-end protection) would catch that.

Sequence numbers

Every TLP gets a 12-bit sequence number, so the numbers run 0 to 4095 and then wrap to 0. The transmitter keeps a counter, NEXT_TRANSMIT_SEQ, and increments it (modulo 4096) after each new TLP. The receiver keeps NEXT_RCV_SEQ, the number it expects next. Because the space wraps, all comparisons are modular: a number is "earlier" than another if it is within the previous half of the circle (2048 values).

The transmitter also tracks ACKD_SEQ, the last sequence number acknowledged. The number of unacknowledged TLPs in flight is (NEXT_TRANSMIT_SEQ − ACKD_SEQ − 1) mod 4096, and the transmitter must stop accepting new TLPs if that would exceed 2048 outstanding — otherwise the modular comparison becomes ambiguous. In practice the replay buffer fills long before that limit.

LCRC

The LCRC is a 32-bit CRC using the same polynomial family as Ethernet's CRC-32, computed over the 2-byte sequence field and all TLP bytes. It detects all single-bit, double-bit and odd-count errors and all burst errors up to 32 bits within its coverage. A TLP whose LCRC fails is a Bad TLP: the receiver discards it, does not process anything in it (not even the sequence number, which might itself be corrupted), and schedules a Nak.

A transmitter can also deliberately nullify a TLP it has started sending — for example a switch doing cut-through forwarding that discovers mid-packet that the incoming copy was bad. It ends the TLP with an inverted LCRC and an end-bad framing marker (EDB in 8b/10b; an equivalent token mechanism in 128b/130b). The receiver silently discards a nullified TLP: no Nak, no error, and the sequence number is not consumed. A nullified TLP in a trace is not a fault by itself.

The replay buffer

The replay buffer (the specification also calls it the retry buffer) holds every transmitted TLP, complete with sequence number and LCRC, in transmission order, until an Ack or Nak covers it. Its size is implementation-dependent. It must be large enough to cover the Ack round-trip time at full link bandwidth, otherwise the transmitter stalls waiting for Acks even though the link and the receiver are perfectly healthy.

Both Ack and Nak carry a 12-bit field, AckNak_Seq_Num. An Ack with value N means "I have every TLP up to and including N". A Nak with value N means "I have every TLP up to and including N, and something after N was bad — replay from N+1". So a Nak is also an acknowledgement for everything up to N; the transmitter purges those entries first and then replays the rest.

The receiver keeps a flag, NAK_SCHEDULED. Once a Nak has been sent, it does not send another Nak for every subsequent bad TLP until it receives the expected TLP successfully — otherwise one burst of noise would generate a storm of Naks. The flag clears when a good TLP with the expected sequence number arrives.

Ack coalescing and the AckNak latency timer

The receiver does not have to Ack every TLP individually. Because Acks are cumulative, one Ack can retire many TLPs. The receiver runs an AckNak latency timer: once it has received a TLP it has not yet acknowledged, it must send an Ack before that timer expires. The specification tabulates recommended timer values as a function of maximum payload size, link width and link speed. Implementation-dependent How aggressively a component coalesces is a design choice. Coalescing too much makes the transmitter's replay buffer fill; Acking every TLP wastes link bandwidth (each Ack costs 8 bytes of link time).

The replay timer and REPLAY_NUM

What if an Ack or a Nak itself is lost (a Bad DLLP is just dropped), or a TLP is lost so completely that the receiver never even saw it start? The transmitter protects itself with a replay timer. The timer runs whenever there are unacknowledged TLPs; it restarts when an Ack makes forward progress. If it expires, the transmitter replays the whole buffer, exactly as if it had received a Nak. The specification gives its timeout value as a formula and table driven by link speed, width and maximum payload; again, check the specification for the exact numbers rather than memorising one.

Every replay, whether caused by a Nak or by the timer, increments the 2-bit counter REPLAY_NUM. REPLAY_NUM resets to zero whenever an Ack or Nak arrives that acknowledges at least one new TLP (forward progress). If the transmitter would replay a fourth time without forward progress — REPLAY_NUM rolls over from 11b to 00b — the Data Link Layer asks the Physical Layer to retrain the link (the LTSSM enters Recovery, which you met in Chapter 8), and the event is reported as the correctable error REPLAY_NUM Rollover. After Recovery, the replay continues: the TLPs are still in the buffer.

DLLP integrity: what happens to a bad DLLP

The receiver checks the 16-bit CRC of every DLLP. A DLLP with a bad CRC is discarded and reported as Bad DLLP (correctable). Nobody retransmits it. This is safe because every DLLP is either cumulative or repeated: the next Ack carries an equal or larger sequence number; UpdateFC DLLPs carry the absolute running credit count, not an increment, so the next one supersedes the lost one; InitFC DLLPs are sent repeatedly during initialisation. The replay timer is the backstop if Acks keep getting lost.

Putting it together: the ladder

Every note gets a number and a seal. You keep a photocopy until your friend holds up a green card with that number. A red card means "resend from after this number". No card for too long? Resend anyway. Resent four times with no progress? Everybody lines up again.

The Data Link Layer attaches a 12-bit sequence number and a 32-bit LCRC to every TLP and keeps a copy in a replay buffer. The receiver returns cumulative Ack DLLPs for good, in-order TLPs and a Nak DLLP when it detects a bad or missing one. The transmitter replays unacknowledged TLPs on a Nak or when its replay timer expires.

Inside each port: a replay RAM (a few KB), counters NEXT_TRANSMIT_SEQ, ACKD_SEQ, NEXT_RCV_SEQ, a 2-bit REPLAY_NUM, a replay timer and an AckNak latency timer, a CRC-32 engine on the TLP path and a CRC-16 engine on the DLLP path. All of it is hardware; firmware and drivers do not touch it in normal operation.

The PCI Express Base Specification's Data Link Layer chapter defines the sequence number, LCRC, the receiver and transmitter rules, the replay timer formula, the AckNak latency table, the DLLP formats and the Data Link Control and Management State Machine. Error reporting of Bad TLP, Bad DLLP, Replay Timer Timeout, REPLAY_NUM Rollover, Data Link Protocol Error and Surprise Down is normative; the replay buffer size and Ack coalescing policy are left to the implementation.

On the wire: TLP (STP token, seq 0x2A5, …, LCRC) then, a few hundred nanoseconds later in the other direction, an 8-byte Ack DLLP carrying 0x2A5 or later. A Nak carries the last good sequence number; the next TLPs from the transmitter are repeats with the same sequence numbers and identical contents.

A protocol analyzer shows each TLP with its sequence number, and Ack/Nak DLLPs with AckNak_Seq_Num. Healthy: Acks march forward with no Naks. Unhealthy: a Nak, then TLPs with already-seen sequence numbers (the analyzer may flag them "replayed" or "duplicate"), possibly a recovery sequence of ordered sets if REPLAY_NUM rolled over.

A senior engineer seeing intermittent latency spikes on an otherwise working NVMe drive checks the AER correctable error status for Bad TLP / Bad DLLP / Replay Timer Timeout / REPLAY_NUM Rollover, correlates the counts with link speed and slot, and suspects signal integrity (equalization, connector, riser, retimer) before suspecting NVMe firmware — because no NVMe-level mechanism can produce a Nak.

Hardware view

In silicon, the Data Link Layer is a pipeline stage in every port's PCIe controller (often called the "PCIe core" or "PCIe IP"). Its main hardware blocks are:

  • Sequence-number and LCRC generator on the transmit path — a CRC-32 engine wide enough to process a full datapath width (for example 32 or 64 bytes) per clock at the core frequency.
  • Replay buffer — a dual-ported SRAM, typically a few kilobytes, sized by the designer for the highest speed and width the port supports and the worst expected Ack latency. In a Root Complex or switch with many ports, each port has its own.
  • Receive checker — LCRC check, sequence check, NAK_SCHEDULED flag, duplicate detection.
  • DLLP generator and parser — CRC-16 engine, arbitration between Ack/Nak, UpdateFC and PM DLLPs, and the TLP stream. The specification sets priorities among these (for example, a pending Ack or Nak must not be starved by a long stream of TLPs).
  • Timers — replay timer, AckNak latency timer, UpdateFC timer. Their limits scale with link speed and width, so the hardware must know the current negotiated rate.
  • Error reporting hooks — wired into the AER status registers in configuration space.

On an NVMe SSD the PCIe core usually comes from an IP vendor and sits between the PHY (SerDes) and the NVMe front-end logic. The NVMe firmware never sees sequence numbers or Acks. It sees, at most, an interrupt or status bit saying "a correctable error was logged" — and many drives do not even expose that to firmware. If you are debugging replay behaviour, you need either an analyzer on the link or the AER registers on both ports.

Two practical consequences:

  1. Replay is fast but not free. Each replay re-sends every unacknowledged TLP, including good ones. At Gen4 x4, replaying a 4 KB burst costs about half a microsecond plus the Nak round trip. A link with a replay every few microseconds will lose measurable throughput and add tail latency while reporting only correctable errors.
  2. Retimers and switches each have their own Data Link Layer… or not. A switch port is a full PCIe port with its own DLL: it Acks TLPs on its upstream link and generates new sequence numbers on its downstream links. A retimer is Physical-Layer only: it does not Ack anything, it just re-drives symbols; the Ack/Nak conversation passes through it. A redriver is analog only. When a capture is taken between a retimer and an endpoint, the Acks you see come from the Root Port on the far side of the retimer.

Specification view

The Data Link Layer is defined entirely in the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 — Data Link Layer Specification chapter, with error reporting in the PCI Express Base Specification — Error Signaling and Logging section and register definitions for AER in the PCI Express Base Specification — Advanced Error Reporting Extended Capability. The PCI Express Base Specification is a PCI-SIG members' document. The NVMe specifications say nothing about DLLPs: NVMe over PCIe Transport Specification 1.4 assumes a working PCIe link and does not redefine its reliability mechanisms.

Normative The receiver and transmitter rules (sequence numbering, LCRC, Ack/Nak meaning, replay on Nak and timer expiry, REPLAY_NUM rollover → retrain, error reporting categories) are normative requirements. Informative The specification's explanatory figures and the implementation notes about replay-buffer sizing are informative.

The sequence number is 12 bits; the LCRC is 32 bits; the DLLP CRC is 16 bits; a DLLP is 6 bytes before Physical Layer framing. Ack is type 00h and Nak is type 10h. REPLAY_NUM is a 2-bit counter whose rollover causes the Data Link Layer to direct the Physical Layer to retrain the link. Bad TLP, Bad DLLP, Replay Timer Timeout and REPLAY_NUM Rollover are correctable errors; Data Link Protocol Error and Surprise Down are uncorrectable errors (default severity: fatal). The DL states are DL_Inactive, DL_Init and DL_Active.

The replay-buffer sizing rule (bandwidth × Ack round trip), the "Ack every few TLPs is efficient" intuition and the claim that frequent replays cost tail latency are reasoned from the mechanism, not quoted from the specification.

Flit Mode is mandatory at 64.0 GT/s and above. Everything in this chapter's traces is non-flit ("classic") mode.

Check in the specification, for your revision: the exact replay timer formula and its tolerance; the AckNak latency timer table; the precise point at which DL_Up is reported during DL_Init; the complete DLLP type table (NOP, Data Link Feature, and any shared-flow-control types); the priority rules between DLLP kinds; the Flit Mode retry rules. The specification does not say how big a replay buffer must be, how many Naks per second are "acceptable", or what a "healthy" correctable error rate is — those are product and platform policy.

Never assume: that a correctable error count of zero means no replays happened (the Ack-path errors may be masked, or the counter may be on the other port); that the Ack you see comes from the final destination (it comes from the next port); that a Nak means data was corrupted at the destination (data is never delivered from a bad TLP); that Flit Mode behaves like classic mode with bigger numbers; or that a vendor-specific DLLP means anything without the vendor's documentation.

  1. The first abnormal event is #2 — not the replay in #4. Replays are a consequence; the corrupted DLLP is the cause visible on the wire.
  2. Only the Host→Dev direction is corrupted (TLPs Dev→Host all decode cleanly). Lanes are differential pairs per direction, so a one-directional problem points to the Root Port's transmitter, the SSD's receiver, or the channel between them in that direction — equalization presets, a damaged trace, a connector pin.

Illustrative output (synthetic, formatted the way recent kernels print it; exact wording varies by kernel version):

pcieport 0000:00:01.1: AER: Corrected error message received from 0000:01:00.0
nvme 0000:01:00.0: PCIe Bus Error: severity=Corrected, type=Data Link Layer, (Transmitter ID)
nvme 0000:01:00.0:   device [abcd:0420] error status/mask=00001100/00002000
nvme 0000:01:00.0:    [ 8] Rollover
nvme 0000:01:00.0:    [12] Timeout

How to read it: "type=Data Link Layer" is the kernel's classification of the error bits. "(Transmitter ID)" means the reported device is the transmitter of the TLPs whose replay failed — consistent with Rollover and Timeout, which are transmitter-side errors. Receiver-side errors (Bad TLP, Bad DLLP) are reported as "(Receiver ID)". Bit 8 (REPLAY_NUM Rollover) and bit 12 (Replay Timer Timeout) of the Correctable Error Status register match the status value 0x00001100. The mask 0x00002000 shows bit 13 (Advisory Non-Fatal) masked, the common default.

What this can prove: that the hardware detected and reported specific Data Link Layer events, on which port, and roughly when (kernel timestamps are when the message was processed, not when the error happened). What it cannot prove: how many replays actually occurred (status bits are sticky flags, not counters; the kernel counter counts reported messages); whether masked errors happened; anything about the TLP contents; whether the cause was electrical or logical. For that you need an analyzer, or at least a correlation with link speed, slot and temperature.

Clearing AER status bits (with setpci) is a write to configuration space. It is low risk on a lab machine but it destroys evidence; record the values first, and never do it on a production system without a change process.

Code / pseudocode

The simulation below models the transmitter and receiver rules of this chapter: 12-bit modular sequence numbers, cumulative Acks, Naks with NAK_SCHEDULED, duplicate discard, the replay timer and the 2-bit REPLAY_NUM. It runs with the Python standard library only. Time is in abstract ticks, one TLP per tick, and Acks take ack_delay ticks to come back. It is a teaching model, not an implementation of the specification's exact timer formulas.

"""Toy simulation of the PCIe Data Link Layer Ack/Nak replay protocol (non-flit mode).
Simulation only: timing is in abstract 'ticks', not nanoseconds."""
import random

SEQ_MOD = 4096          # 12-bit sequence numbers

def later(a, b):
    """True if sequence number a is 'later' than b in modular 12-bit space."""
    return 0 < (a - b) % SEQ_MOD < SEQ_MOD // 2

class Transmitter:
    def __init__(self, replay_timeout):
        self.next_seq = 0
        self.acked = SEQ_MOD - 1        # ACKD_SEQ starts at 4095
        self.replay = []                # list of (seq, payload)
        self.replay_num = 0
        self.timer = None
        self.timeout = replay_timeout
        self.rollovers = 0
        self.replays = 0

    def send_new(self, payload):
        seq = self.next_seq
        self.next_seq = (self.next_seq + 1) % SEQ_MOD
        self.replay.append((seq, payload))
        if self.timer is None:
            self.timer = self.timeout
        return (seq, payload)

    def on_acknak(self, kind, seq):
        progressed = False
        while self.replay and not later(self.replay[0][0], seq):
            self.replay.pop(0)
            progressed = True
        if progressed:
            self.acked = seq
            self.replay_num = 0
            self.timer = self.timeout if self.replay else None
        if kind == "NAK":
            return self.start_replay("Nak")
        return []

    def start_replay(self, why):
        self.replays += 1
        self.replay_num += 1
        if self.replay_num == 4:        # 2-bit counter rolls 11b -> 00b
            self.replay_num = 0
            self.rollovers += 1
            print(f"    !! REPLAY_NUM rollover -> link retrain (Recovery), cause={why}")
        self.timer = self.timeout
        return list(self.replay)

    def tick(self):
        if self.timer is not None:
            self.timer -= 1
            if self.timer == 0:
                print("    !! replay timer expired")
                return self.start_replay("timer")
        return []

class Receiver:
    def __init__(self):
        self.next_rcv = 0
        self.nak_scheduled = False
        self.delivered = []

    def on_tlp(self, seq, payload, lcrc_ok):
        if not lcrc_ok:
            if not self.nak_scheduled:
                self.nak_scheduled = True
                return ("NAK", (self.next_rcv - 1) % SEQ_MOD)
            return None
        if seq == self.next_rcv:
            self.delivered.append(payload)
            self.next_rcv = (self.next_rcv + 1) % SEQ_MOD
            self.nak_scheduled = False
            return ("ACK", seq)
        if later(seq, self.next_rcv):          # gap: something was lost
            if not self.nak_scheduled:
                self.nak_scheduled = True
                return ("NAK", (self.next_rcv - 1) % SEQ_MOD)
            return None
        return ("ACK", (self.next_rcv - 1) % SEQ_MOD)   # duplicate

def run(n_tlps=40, tlp_error_rate=0.05, dllp_loss_rate=0.05, ack_delay=3,
        replay_buffer=8, seed=7):
    random.seed(seed)
    tx, rx = Transmitter(replay_timeout=12), Receiver()
    to_send = [f"TLP{i}" for i in range(n_tlps)]
    replay_queue = []                 # TLPs the transmitter must resend, in order
    dllps = []                        # (arrival_tick, kind, seq) travelling back
    t = 0
    while len(rx.delivered) < n_tlps and t < 5000:
        t += 1
        # 1. transmit one TLP per tick: replays first, then new TLPs if buffer has room
        tlp = None
        if replay_queue:
            tlp = replay_queue.pop(0)
        elif to_send and len(tx.replay) < replay_buffer:
            tlp = tx.send_new(to_send.pop(0))
        if tlp:
            seq, payload = tlp
            ok = random.random() > tlp_error_rate
            reply = rx.on_tlp(seq, payload, ok)
            print(f"t={t:4d} TLP seq={seq:4d} {'ok ' if ok else 'BAD'} -> {reply}")
            if reply:
                if random.random() > dllp_loss_rate:
                    dllps.append((t + ack_delay, *reply))
                else:
                    print("        (DLLP corrupted on the way back: Bad DLLP, dropped)")
        # 2. deliver DLLPs whose flight time is over
        for d in [d for d in dllps if d[0] <= t]:
            dllps.remove(d)
            again = tx.on_acknak(d[1], d[2])
            if again:
                print(f"        {d[1]} {d[2]} -> replaying {[s for s, _ in again]}")
                replay_queue = list(again)
        # 3. replay timer
        again = tx.tick()
        if again:
            print(f"        replaying {[s for s, _ in again]}")
            replay_queue = list(again)
    assert rx.delivered == [f"TLP{i}" for i in range(n_tlps)], "order violated!"
    print(f"delivered {len(rx.delivered)} TLPs in order; replays={tx.replays} "
          f"rollovers={tx.rollovers} ticks={t}")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    run()

Things to try: set replay_buffer=2 with ack_delay=6 and no errors, and watch throughput fall (the transmitter stalls waiting for Acks — the sizing formula in action); set dllp_loss_rate=0.5 and watch timer-driven replays; run run(n_tlps=200, tlp_error_rate=0.3, dllp_loss_rate=0.3, seed=3) to provoke a REPLAY_NUM rollover. The final assert checks the one property that must never break: in-order, exactly-once delivery.

Pseudocode: receiver side, as a hardware designer writes it

on TLP received:
    if framing_error or LCRC_bad:
        if TLP was nullified (inverted LCRC + end-bad marker): discard silently; return
        report Bad TLP (correctable)
        if not NAK_SCHEDULED: NAK_SCHEDULED = 1; send Nak(NEXT_RCV_SEQ - 1)
        return
    seq = TLP.seq
    if seq == NEXT_RCV_SEQ:
        deliver TLP to Transaction Layer
        NEXT_RCV_SEQ = (NEXT_RCV_SEQ + 1) mod 4096
        NAK_SCHEDULED = 0
        start AckNak latency timer if not running      # Ack will be sent before it expires
    else if seq is earlier than NEXT_RCV_SEQ (mod 4096):   # duplicate after a replay
        discard; schedule Ack(NEXT_RCV_SEQ - 1)
    else:                                              # gap
        report Bad TLP (correctable)
        discard
        if not NAK_SCHEDULED: NAK_SCHEDULED = 1; send Nak(NEXT_RCV_SEQ - 1)

on Ack/Nak DLLP received (transmitter side):
    if DLLP CRC bad: report Bad DLLP; discard; return
    if AckNak_Seq_Num acknowledges a TLP never sent: report Data Link Protocol Error; return
    purge replay buffer up to and including AckNak_Seq_Num
    if anything purged: REPLAY_NUM = 0; restart replay timer (or stop it if buffer empty)
    if Nak: initiate replay

on replay timer expiry:
    report Replay Timer Timeout (correctable); initiate replay

initiate replay:
    REPLAY_NUM = REPLAY_NUM + 1 (2-bit)
    if REPLAY_NUM wrapped to 0: report REPLAY_NUM Rollover; direct Physical Layer to Recovery
    retransmit every TLP in the replay buffer, oldest first, unchanged
    block new TLPs until the replay has been sent

Worked example

Problem. On the lab system, a 128 KiB NVMe Read returns its data as 512 MWr TLPs of 256 bytes each from the Lab SSD to host memory. During this transfer, the Root Port's receiver detects 3 LCRC errors, spread out (never two within the same replay window). Ack round trip is 300 ns. Estimate (a) how many extra TLPs cross the link, (b) the extra time, (c) what the AER registers show afterwards.

Step 1 — TLP time. Each 256-byte MWr with a 4-DW (16-byte) header, 2-byte sequence field, 4-byte LCRC and 2-byte framing token is 280 bytes. At ≈ 7.88 GB/s that is ≈ 35.5 ns.

Step 2 — what one Nak costs. When TLP k is bad, the device keeps sending k+1, k+2, … until the Nak arrives. With a 300 ns round trip it sends about 300 / 35.5 ≈ 8 more TLPs, all of which the receiver discards. After the Nak, it replays k through k+8: about 9 TLPs re-sent. Derived

Step 3 — totals. 3 errors × ≈ 9 TLPs ≈ 27 extra TLPs ≈ 27 × 35.5 ns ≈ 0.96 µs extra on a transfer that takes 512 × 35.5 ns ≈ 18.2 µs. About 5 % for that one command — invisible at the IOPS level, visible in a latency histogram.

Step 4 — AER. Root Port: Correctable Error Status bit 6 (Bad TLP) set — once; it is a sticky flag, not a counter. The kernel likely prints one or three "Corrected error" messages (depending on whether the error messages were sent and processed separately). Lab SSD: nothing (receiving a Nak is not an error). REPLAY_NUM never exceeded 1, so no Rollover.

Lesson. Correctable errors are cheap individually but are evidence of margin loss; a link that has three per 128 KiB will usually have many more at higher temperature or after equalization drift.

Common mistakes

  • Treating an Ack as an NVMe-level or Transaction-Layer acknowledgement. It acknowledges one hop's receipt of a TLP, nothing more.
  • Looking for DLLPs end to end. DLLPs never cross a switch. A capture on the switch's downstream link shows the switch's Acks, not the Root Port's.
  • Reading AER on one side only. Receiver errors are logged by the receiver; transmitter-side timeouts by the transmitter.
  • Assuming a sequence number identifies a request. Sequence numbers are per link and reused every 4096 TLPs; the Transaction Layer tag + requester ID identify requests (Chapter 13).
  • Calling replayed TLPs "duplicates sent by the device". They are the Data Link Layer doing its job; the Transaction Layer on the receiver sees each TLP exactly once.
  • Thinking a replay can alter a TLP. The replay is of the stored bytes; if contents differ between original and replay, that is a device bug worth reporting.
  • Thinking a nullified TLP is an error. It is a deliberate cancellation.
  • Applying classic-mode rules to a Flit Mode capture. In Flit Mode, retry is per flit; there is no per-TLP LCRC.
  • Believing "no AER messages" means "no errors". Errors can be masked, AER can be firmware-first (handled by platform firmware, not reported by the kernel), or the kernel's AER driver can be disabled.

Debugging connection

Data Link Layer evidence is the first place to look when an NVMe device shows latency spikes without command errors, intermittent link-down/recovery events, or throughput below expectation on one slot but not another. A disciplined first pass:

  1. Collect AER status on both ends (device and its upstream port), and the kernel's AER counters. Record before clearing.
  2. Classify: receiver errors (Receiver Error, Bad TLP, Bad DLLP) say the receive path saw corrupted data; transmitter errors (Replay Timer Timeout, REPLAY_NUM Rollover) say acknowledgements are not coming back. Data Link Protocol Error says one side's logic did something impossible.
  3. Find the direction: Bad TLP at the Root Port → device-to-host direction marginal. Bad DLLP or Timeout at the device → host-to-device direction marginal.
  4. Correlate with the Physical Layer: did the link speed or width change? Are there Receiver Errors (Physical Layer)? Does forcing a lower speed (in a lab) make the errors disappear? That strongly suggests signal integrity.
  5. Correlate with power management: errors clustered at L1 or L0s exit point to exit-latency or clock-request problems (Chapter 50).
  6. Only then consider device logic (wrong Ack numbers, malformed DLLPs) — analyzer required.

A recurring real-world pattern: a platform logs a burst of corrected "Data Link Layer" errors at boot and never again. That is often equalization settling or a device that is not fully ready when traffic starts. Distinguish it from errors that accumulate under load, which indicate a margin problem.

Senior engineer insight

Correctable errors are not "harmless", they are leading indicators. The Data Link Layer is so good at hiding errors that a link can degrade for weeks before the first uncorrectable event. Fleet teams trend correctable error counts per slot and per device model; a step change after a firmware update, a BIOS change or a hardware revision is a design-review question. Equally, a "zero correctable errors forever" report from a device that masks them is not evidence of health.

Because the Data Link Layer guarantees reliable delivery, any data corruption that reaches software with a good LCRC history happened above the link: in a switch's internal datapath, in the device's own memory, in the host memory subsystem, or in the IOMMU translation. That is precisely why ECRC and NVMe end-to-end protection information exist. When someone says "PCIe corrupted my data", the first question is "on which hop, and how did it get past the LCRC?"

Two more habits. First, in captures, measure Ack latency and UpdateFC latency as well as transaction latency; a slow-to-Ack Root Port throttles a fast device without any error being logged. Second, when evaluating a new Gen5 or Gen6 platform, ask whether the link runs in Flit Mode — it changes what an analyzer shows and which counters mean what.

  3. Why does throughput stop improving at a particular buffer size in Step 4?

Challenge

Modify the simulator so that Acks are coalesced: the receiver sends an Ack only every 4 good TLPs or when a latency timer of 3 ticks expires. Show that in-order delivery still holds and measure the DLLP count saved.

Hint

0xC0 = bits 6 and 7. 0x40 = bit 6. Remember who receives what: the Root Port receives TLPs and DLLPs from the device; the device receives TLPs and DLLPs from the Root Port.

Solution

Device 0x000000C0: Bad TLP (bit 6) and Bad DLLP (bit 7) — the device received corrupted TLPs and corrupted DLLPs, so the host-to-device direction is marginal. Root Port 0x00000040: Bad TLP — the Root Port also received a corrupted TLP, so the device-to-host direction had at least one error too. Both directions affected suggests a shared cause (connector, reference clock, power noise, a retimer) rather than a single lane's transmitter equalization. Answers: (1) Bad DLLP is logged by whoever receives the DLLP; the device received a corrupted Ack/UpdateFC from the Root Port. (2) Replay Timer Timeout and REPLAY_NUM Rollover at the transmitter whose Acks are lost, then Recovery events, possibly a speed downgrade and eventually Surprise Down. (3) Once the buffer holds more TLPs than fit in one Ack round trip (≈ ack_delay + 1 ticks here), the transmitter never waits for Acks; more buffer adds nothing. With ack_delay=6 the knee is around 7–8 entries.

What a senior engineer notices

That the status bits are flags — one read cannot give a rate. A senior engineer reads, clears (on a lab machine), runs a known workload for a fixed time, and reads again, repeating at each link speed. They also notice whether AER is reported natively to the OS or firmware-first, because that changes what the kernel can see.

Failure variants

  • Only Receiver Error (bit 0) set: Physical Layer symbol errors — framing or 128b/130b block problems — not yet Data Link Layer corruption.
  • REPLAY_NUM Rollover without Bad DLLP on the same side: Acks are not lost but are slow; the peer may be stalled (check power management, or a peer whose Ack latency exceeds the replay timer — a compliance bug).
  • No AER capability at all: older or minimal devices; you have only the kernel log and link status.

Exercises

  1. A receiver with NEXT_RCV_SEQ = 0x005 receives a good TLP with sequence number 0xFFE. Is it a duplicate or a gap? Show the modular arithmetic.
  2. Draw the sequence diagram for: TLPs 10, 11, 12 sent; Ack for 10 lost; Ack 12 received. What happens to the replay buffer? Does a replay occur?
  3. Explain why UpdateFC DLLPs carry absolute counts rather than increments, in terms of what happens when one is lost.
  4. For a Gen5 x4 link (32 GT/s, 128b/130b) with an Ack round trip of 400 ns, estimate the minimum replay buffer size.
  5. A switch sits between the Root Port and the Lab SSD. The Root Port logs Bad TLP. Which link is suspect? What would you check next?
  6. Write the AER correctable status value (hex) for: Receiver Error + Replay Timer Timeout.
Model answers

(1) (0xFFE − 0x005) mod 4096 = 4089, which is more than 2048, so 0xFFE is earlier than 0x005: a duplicate — discard and Ack 0x004. (2) The Ack for 10 is lost, but Ack 12 is cumulative and retires 10, 11 and 12; no replay unless the replay timer expired before Ack 12 arrived. (3) If one UpdateFC is lost, the next carries the current total, so the transmitter's view is corrected without retransmission; increments would drift permanently. (4) 32 × 4 × 128/130 / 8 ≈ 15.75 GB/s; × 400 ns ≈ 6.3 KB. (5) The Root Port only ever receives TLPs from the switch's upstream port, so the Root Port ↔ switch link is suspect, not the switch ↔ SSD link; check the switch upstream port's AER and that link's speed/width history. (6) Bit 0 + bit 12 = 0x00001001.

Quiz

  1. How many bits is the TLP sequence number in non-flit mode?

    • 8
    • 12
    • 16
    • 32
    Answer and reasoning

    12 bits, values 0–4095, carried in a 16-bit field whose top four bits are reserved.

  2. A Nak DLLP with AckNak_Seq_Num = 0x123 means:

    • TLP 0x123 was bad
    • Retransmit only TLP 0x124
    • Everything through 0x123 was received; replay everything after it
    • The link must retrain
    Answer and reasoning

    A Nak is cumulative like an Ack for everything up to the given number, and requests replay of all later unacknowledged TLPs.

  3. Which of these is not a DLLP?

    • Ack
    • UpdateFC-NP
    • PM_Request_Ack
    • Completion with Data
    Answer and reasoning

    CplD is a TLP from the Transaction Layer.

  4. What happens when REPLAY_NUM rolls over?

    • The Data Link Layer directs the Physical Layer to retrain the link and reports REPLAY_NUM Rollover
    • The TLPs in the replay buffer are discarded
    • The device is reset
    • A fatal error is always reported
    Answer and reasoning

    Rollover triggers Recovery and is a correctable error. The replay buffer is preserved and replay continues after retraining.

  5. A DLLP arrives with a bad CRC. What does the receiver do?

    • Sends a Nak
    • Discards it and logs Bad DLLP
    • Requests DLLP retransmission
    • Retrains the link
    Answer and reasoning

    DLLPs are not retransmitted; their content is cumulative or repeated, and timers cover lost Acks.

  6. Which Data Link Layer state allows TLP transmission?

    • DL_Inactive
    • DL_Init (FC_INIT1)
    • DL_Active
    • DL_Recovery
    Answer and reasoning

    Only DL_Active. (There is no "DL_Recovery" state; Recovery is an LTSSM state.)

  7. Through a switch, the Lab SSD sends a TLP towards the Root Port. Who sends the Ack the SSD receives?

    • The Root Port
    • The switch's downstream port
    • The NVMe driver
    • Nobody — posted TLPs are not acknowledged
    Answer and reasoning

    Acks are hop-by-hop. Every TLP, posted or not, is acknowledged at link level by the adjacent port.

  8. Open: Why can a TLP with a good LCRC still carry corrupted data, and what mechanisms catch that?

    Model answer

    The LCRC is regenerated at every hop and only covers the wire. Corruption inside a switch, a device's buffer or host memory happens outside LCRC coverage. ECRC (end-to-end TLP digest), poisoned-TLP signalling, and higher-level protection such as NVMe end-to-end data protection catch it.

  9. Open: Explain why the transmitter must not have more than 2048 unacknowledged TLPs.

    Model answer

    Sequence comparisons are modulo 4096 with "earlier" defined as within the previous half. With more than half the space outstanding, an acknowledgement number could be interpreted as either old or new, so purge and duplicate detection would be ambiguous.

  10. Open: In Flit Mode, what replaces the per-TLP LCRC and sequence number?

    Model answer

    Fixed-size flits protected by a flit-level CRC and forward error correction, with retry performed per flit using flit sequence numbers carried in the flit's Data Link Layer bytes. Confirm the field details in the Flit Mode sections of the PCI Express Base Specification 6.x/7.0.

Interview questions

  1. Beginner: What is the difference between a TLP and a DLLP?

    Model answer

    A TLP is created by the Transaction Layer, carries requests and completions between functions, has addresses/IDs/tags, and can be routed through switches. A DLLP is created by the Data Link Layer, is 6 bytes plus framing, carries Ack/Nak, flow control and power-management information between two adjacent ports, and is never forwarded.

  2. Beginner: What does the LCRC protect, and what happens if it fails?

    Model answer

    It protects the sequence number and the TLP across one link. On failure the receiver discards the TLP, logs Bad TLP and sends a Nak; the transmitter replays from its replay buffer.

  3. Intermediate: Walk me through what happens when an Ack DLLP is corrupted.

    Model answer

    The receiving port drops it and logs Bad DLLP. If a later Ack arrives in time it retires the TLPs cumulatively and nothing else happens. If not, the transmitter's replay timer expires, it logs Replay Timer Timeout, replays, and REPLAY_NUM increments; the receiver discards the duplicates and re-Acks. Repeated failure leads to REPLAY_NUM Rollover and link retraining.

  4. Intermediate: How do you size a replay buffer?

    Model answer

    At least the bytes the port can transmit during the worst-case Ack round trip (including the peer's Ack coalescing delay) at maximum speed and width, plus a margin for the largest TLP. Otherwise the port stalls waiting for Acks and loses throughput without logging errors.

  5. Advanced: An NVMe SSD shows p99.99 latency spikes only in one server model. No NVMe errors. What do you check?

    Model answer

    AER correctable status on both ends of the link and the kernel's AER counters; link speed/width stability; whether errors correlate with ASPM L1 exits; equalization results; whether the AER reporting is firmware-first. With an analyzer, Nak/replay rate and Ack latency. Then compare against a known-good server — same drive, same firmware — to isolate the platform channel.

  6. Senior: A vendor claims "our link has zero correctable errors in 30 days of testing". What do you ask?

    Model answer

    Which registers on which ports were read, whether errors were masked, whether AER was enabled and reported natively or firmware-first, at which link speed and width, under what load and temperature, with what equalization settings, whether Flit Mode was in use (and which counters apply), and whether an analyzer was used to count Naks independently. Absence of evidence collected from the wrong place is not evidence of absence.

  7. Senior: Why is a Data Link Protocol Error treated as fatal by default while Bad TLP is correctable?

    Model answer

    Bad TLP is an expected physical event that the protocol corrects. A Data Link Protocol Error means a peer violated the protocol itself (for example acknowledging an unsent sequence number), so the link's state is no longer trustworthy; continuing could deliver wrong or duplicate TLPs. Severity is configurable in the AER Uncorrectable Error Severity register.

Chapter summary

The Data Link Layer makes each PCIe link reliable. It wraps every TLP with a 12-bit sequence number and a 32-bit LCRC, keeps a copy in a replay buffer, and frees the copy when a cumulative Ack DLLP arrives. Bad or out-of-sequence TLPs are discarded and trigger a Nak; lost acknowledgements trigger the replay timer; four replays without progress (REPLAY_NUM rollover) send the link to Recovery. DLLPs are 6-byte link-local packets — Ack, Nak, InitFC1/InitFC2 and UpdateFC per credit type, power-management handshakes and vendor-specific — that never cross a switch and never reach software. The Data Link Layer moves through DL_Inactive, DL_Init (FC_INIT1, FC_INIT2) and DL_Active, and only DL_Active carries TLPs. Its failures appear as AER correctable errors (Bad TLP, Bad DLLP, Replay Timer Timeout, REPLAY_NUM Rollover) or uncorrectable ones (Data Link Protocol Error, Surprise Down). Flit Mode in PCIe 6.0+ replaces per-TLP LCRC/sequence numbers with per-flit protection and retry.
