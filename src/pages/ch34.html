Flush is the shortest Non-Volatile Memory Express (NVMe) command you will ever decode — an opcode, a namespace identifier and nothing else — and one of the most consequential. It is the host's way of saying "everything you have told me is done, make it permanent now". This chapter teaches exactly what that promise covers, what it does not, and what it costs.

Why this chapter exists

In Chapter 33 you learned that a successful Write completion does not, by itself, mean the data would survive a power cut when the controller has an enabled volatile write cache. Something must close that gap. Non-Volatile Memory Express (NVMe) gives the host two tools: the per-command Force Unit Access (FUA) bit, and the Flush command. Flush is the general one: it reaches back over everything already completed.

Every serious storage stack depends on Flush. Journaling filesystems use it to order their journal against their commit records; databases use it on every transaction commit (often through fsync() or fdatasync()); virtual machine hypervisors must forward it from the guest or silently break the guest's durability. When Flush is wrong — not issued, not forwarded, not honored, or misunderstood — the result is the worst kind of bug: everything works perfectly until the power goes out.

Flush is also a performance topic. A Flush can take microseconds or many milliseconds depending on how much dirty data the cache holds and how the device protects it. Applications that flush too often crawl; applications that flush too rarely lose data. Engineers who understand the cost model can design commit protocols that are both safe and fast.

Learning objectives

  • State precisely what a completed Flush guarantees, including its scope in time (which writes it covers) and in space (which namespaces).
  • Explain what Flush does not guarantee: ordering of in-flight commands, a barrier for later commands, protection against torn writes, or anything when the device lies about its cache.
  • Find and interpret the Volatile Write Cache (VWC) field of Identify Controller, including its report of Flush support for the broadcast namespace identifier FFFFFFFFh, and the Volatile Write Cache feature.
  • Relate Flush to orderly shutdown (the Shutdown Notification field CC.SHN of the Controller Configuration register and the Shutdown Status field CSTS.SHST of the Controller Status register), power-loss protection, and the Self-Monitoring, Analysis and Reporting Technology (SMART) "unsafe shutdowns" counter.
  • Predict and measure Flush latency; reason about its effect on concurrent I/O.
  • Read normal, slow and failed Flush traces and debug durability incidents with evidence.

The story

Back at the bakery, the helper carries recipes in an apron and takes them to the vault in batches. Most of the time that is fine. But tonight there is a thunderstorm and the lights might go out. So you call out: "Before you do anything else for me, take everything you've already told me you have into the vault, and tell me when it's done." That call is FLUSH. The helper empties the apron into the vault and says "all safe".

Notice two things. First, "everything you've already told me you have" — if you hand the helper a new recipe while they are walking to the vault, that new one is not part of the deal. Second, if the helper never had an apron (some helpers walk every recipe straight to the vault), FLUSH is easy: "all safe" right away.

A restaurant kitchen's order spike. Cooks clip finished orders on a rail (the volatile cache) and runners carry them to tables (the non-volatile media) in batches. The manager can shout "clear the rail!" (Flush): every plate already on the rail goes out now, before the manager reports to the owner that those tables are served. Plates the cooks finish during the clearing might or might not go with that batch — the manager's report only covers plates that were on the rail when the shout went out.

What it hides. A rail is visible; a controller's cache is not. You cannot look at it to see if it is empty, and the controller may move data to media at any time without being asked — the Flush just forces it to finish. Some "rails" are actually protected by capacitors, so a power cut does not drop the plates at all.

Where it misleads. Shouting "clear the rail" in a kitchen stops cooking. Flush does not stop the controller: it may continue executing other commands, including writes submitted after the Flush, and those writes may even become durable before the Flush completes — but the host must not rely on that. The analogy also suggests Flush is about speed; it is about the promise, and a controller may satisfy it instantly if it can already keep the promise.

Beginner explanation

Many solid-state drives (SSDs) have a small, fast memory (usually dynamic random-access memory (DRAM)) where they collect written data before storing it in flash. This memory loses its contents when power disappears — that is what volatile means. It is called the volatile write cache.

When the SSD says a write is "complete", it means it has the data, and if you read it back you will get the new data. It does not necessarily mean the data is in flash yet. If power is lost at the wrong moment, completed writes that were still only in the cache can be lost.

The Flush command fixes that. You send Flush for a namespace. The SSD makes sure that every write it had already completed for that namespace before you sent the Flush is stored in non-volatile media, and only then tells you the Flush is complete. After that, those writes will survive a power cut.

Three simple rules capture most of what you need:

  1. Flush covers the past, not the future. Only writes that completed before the Flush was submitted are covered.
  2. No cache, nothing to do. If the SSD has no volatile write cache, a Flush completes successfully and does nothing.
  3. Flush costs time. The more data is waiting in the cache, the longer the Flush takes, and frequent flushes reduce the SSD's ability to batch writes efficiently.

Engineering explanation

What Flush means

Flush (opcode 00h) asks the controller to commit data and metadata associated with the specified namespace to non-volatile media. It carries no data pointer and no logical block address (LBA) range: it applies to the whole namespace. Its scope is defined by completion: it covers all write-type commands for that namespace that the controller completed before the Flush command was submitted. A controller is allowed to flush more than that — other namespaces, writes that arrived later — but the host may rely only on the defined scope. Normative (confirm wording in the Flush command section of the Base specification for your revision).

The 64-byte command is described as sixteen 32-bit Command Dwords, CDW0 to CDW15.

Why it matters

Durability protocols are built from two primitives: ordering ("B must not become durable before A") and durability ("A is durable now"). NVMe does not order commands, so the host builds ordering from waiting plus durability: write A, wait for A's completion, Flush, wait for the Flush, then write B. If power fails at any point, B can only be on media if A is. This is how a journal protects its commit record; it is how a database protects the link between its log and its data pages.

Without Flush, the host's only alternatives are FUA on every write (expensive: each write waits for media) or disabling the cache (very expensive for small writes, and it may not even be possible). Flush lets the device batch writes most of the time and pay the media cost only at commit points.

What Flush guarantees

  • When the Flush completes successfully, data and metadata of commands for that namespace completed before the Flush was submitted are on non-volatile media. Normative
  • If no volatile write cache is present, Flush has no effect and completes successfully. Normative (the specification's handling of a cache that is present but disabled should be confirmed in your revision's text; in practice a disabled cache holds no dirty data and Flush completes quickly).
  • With NSID FFFFFFFFh, on a controller that reports support for it, the flush applies to all namespaces attached to the controller processing the command.

What Flush does not necessarily guarantee

  • No coverage of in-flight commands. A write that has been submitted but not completed when the Flush is submitted is outside the guarantee, even if it completes before the Flush does.
  • Not a barrier. Commands submitted after the Flush may be executed, completed and even made durable before the Flush completes. Flush does not hold them back.
  • No atomicity. Flush says nothing about writes torn by a power failure that happens before the Flush completes. Atomicity comes from Atomic Write Unit Power Fail (AWUPF)/Namespace Atomic Write Unit Power Fail (NAWUPF) and the host's write sizes.
  • No media integrity. Flush makes data non-volatile; it does not promise the media will never fail later. Retention and error correction are separate.
  • No cross-controller promise by default. In a subsystem with several controllers sharing a namespace, the guarantee is framed around commands completed by the controller processing the Flush. Whether writes completed through another controller are also flushed is something to confirm in the specification and the product; do not assume. Uncertain
  • No protection against a dishonest device or layer. If firmware, a redundant array of independent disks (RAID) controller, a hypervisor or a Universal Serial Bus (USB) bridge acknowledges Flush without doing it, the host cannot tell from the completion.

Submitting the Flush while the writes it is meant to protect are still outstanding. The Flush may complete successfully and protect nothing you cared about. Always wait for the writes' completions, then submit the Flush.

Discovering the cache: VWC and the Volatile Write Cache feature

Identify Controller contains a field named VWC (Volatile Write Cache). Its bit 0 reports whether a volatile write cache is present. Since NVMe 1.4, bits 2:1 report how the controller handles Flush with NSID FFFFFFFFh: 00b support not indicated, 01b reserved, 10b Flush with FFFFFFFFh not supported, 11b supported. Version-dependent — a controller implementing an older revision reports 00b, and the host cannot infer support.

If a cache is present, the host can enable or disable it with Set Features using the Volatile Write Cache feature (Feature Identifier 06h); bit 0 of the feature value is the Volatile Write Cache Enable (WCE) bit. Get Features reads the current state. Whether the setting is saveable across power cycles is reported by the feature's capabilities (Get Features with the "supported capabilities" select value).

The byte offset of VWC within Identify Controller, the precise status code returned when NSID FFFFFFFFh is used on a controller that does not support it (commonly described as Invalid Namespace or Format — verify), and the exact text about Flush when the cache is present but disabled.

Power-loss implications

Three mechanisms decide what survives a power failure:

  1. Host-driven commit points — Flush and FUA — cover exactly what the specification says, no more.
  2. Orderly shutdown. Before removing power, a well-behaved host writes Shutdown Notification (CC.SHN, bits 15:14 of the Controller Configuration register: 01b normal, 10b abrupt) and waits for CSTS.SHST (bits 3:2 of Controller Status) to report shutdown processing complete (10b). A controller completing normal shutdown processing commits its cache. Linux does this on reboot and power-off. If power is removed without it, the controller counts an unsafe shutdown in the SMART / Health Information log page.
  3. Power-loss protection (PLP). Enterprise SSDs typically have capacitors (PLP) holding enough energy to program the cache's contents after external power vanishes. With working PLP, completed writes survive even without Flush. Many such drives report no volatile write cache at all (VWC bit 0 = 0), which tells the host that Flush is unnecessary. Client SSDs generally lack full PLP.

"Old data returns" is a subtle failure. The LBA reads back without error but contains the previous version, so checksums at the application level are the only way to detect it. A filesystem that wrote a commit record pointing at blocks that reverted to old content will happily serve inconsistent data.

Performance consequences

Flush latency has three main components: draining the dirty data in the cache to NAND (Not-AND gate based) flash, persisting the flash translation layer (FTL)'s own metadata (mapping table updates) that the controller requires for the data to be retrievable after power loss, and firmware overhead. The first is roughly proportional to the amount of dirty data and inversely to the number of NAND dies available; the second is implementation-specific and can dominate on drives that keep large mapping journals in DRAM.

Tflush ≈ Tfw + Ddirty ÷ Rprog + Tmeta
Tflush
time from Flush submission to its completion
Tfw
fixed firmware overhead of handling the command (microseconds)
Ddirty
bytes in the volatile cache not yet on non-volatile media for the affected namespaces
Rprog
aggregate NAND program bandwidth the controller can apply, in bytes per second
Tmeta
time to persist the FTL metadata needed to make that data recoverable

This is a Derived model, not a specification formula. Plugging in illustrative numbers — 8 MiB dirty, 2 GB/s program bandwidth, 300 µs metadata, 20 µs firmware — gives about 4.5 ms. A PLP drive that reports no volatile cache completes the same Flush in tens of microseconds because there is nothing to wait for.

Wider consequences:

  • Commit rate ceiling. A single-threaded application that does write+flush per transaction is bounded at roughly 1/(write latency + flush latency) transactions per second. Group commit (batching many transactions per flush) is the standard escape.
  • Interference. While draining, the controller's NAND dies are busy; concurrent reads may see higher latency. Some controllers prioritize flush draining over new writes; others continue accepting writes into the cache. Implementation-dependent
  • Smaller program units. Frequent flushes force the controller to program partially filled pages or use padding, increasing write amplification and wearing the drive faster.
  • Queue effects. Linux does not hold all other I/O while a flush is outstanding; it sequences flush and the writes that depend on it with a dedicated flush state machine. Flushes from many threads may be merged.

The central concept on the ladder: Flush as a durability fence over the past

"Take everything you already told me you have into the vault, then tell me." New recipes handed over meanwhile are not part of the deal.

Flush makes every write the controller completed for that namespace before the Flush was submitted non-volatile, then completes. With no volatile cache it is a no-op that succeeds.

The controller drains dirty cache lines from DRAM to NAND across dies, persists FTL mapping updates, then posts the Completion Queue Entry (CQE). Capacitor-backed drives may treat it as nearly free.

Doorbell Memory Write (MWr), one Submission Queue Entry (SQE) Memory Read (MRd)/Completion with Data (CplD), no data transfer, then — after a variable delay — a CQE MWr and Message Signaled Interrupts – Extended (MSI-X). Nothing else crosses the link for the Flush itself.

Durability lost? Prove Flush was issued after the writes completed, that the controller reported success, and whether the device has an honest VWC report — then power-cut test. Flush timeouts? Look for device-internal stalls (GC, thermal) and the size of the dirty set.

The sixteen questions for Flush

1. What is it?
An I/O command (opcode 00h) that commits previously completed writes for a namespace to non-volatile media.
2. Why was it created?
Volatile caches make writes fast but not durable; hosts need a way to obtain durability at chosen points.
3. What problem does it solve?
It lets the host build ordered, durable commit protocols without paying media latency on every write.
4. What existed before it?
AT Attachment (ATA) FLUSH CACHE / FLUSH CACHE EXT and Small Computer System Interface (SCSI) SYNCHRONIZE CACHE, plus FUA bits in ATA and SCSI write commands. NVMe kept the concept with a simpler command.
5. Why is the current design better?
It is a normal queued command (no special serialization), its scope is defined by completion, and Identify reports whether it is needed at all.
6. Who creates it?
The host driver, usually on behalf of filesystem preflush requests, fsync(), or block-device flush requests.
7. Who consumes it?
The controller's command processor and cache manager.
8. Where does it live?
In an I/O Submission Queue slot like any I/O command.
9. What memory/registers are involved?
Submission Queue (SQ)/Completion Queue (CQ) rings, doorbells; Identify Controller VWC; the Volatile Write Cache feature; CC.SHN/CSTS.SHST for the related shutdown path.
10. What happens before it?
The writes it is meant to protect complete; the host waits for them.
11. What happens after it?
The host writes data that depends on the flushed data (a commit record), often with FUA.
12. What happens if it is wrong?
Missing or mis-ordered Flush → data loss or inconsistency after power failure; Flush with unsupported NSID → error; excessive Flush → poor performance and extra wear.
13. What does the analyzer show?
An SQE with opcode 00h, no data MRds, and a CQE after a delay that reflects the dirty data.
14. What does Linux show?
nvme_cmd_flush in tracepoints, write-cache mode in sysfs, VWC in nvme id-ctrl, feature 06h state, unsafe shutdown count in the SMART log.
15. What would a validation engineer test?
Power-cut durability of flushed vs unflushed data; Flush latency vs dirty size; NSID FFFFFFFFh handling vs VWC bits 2:1; Flush with cache disabled; Flush during heavy I/O; shutdown notification behavior.
16. What would a senior engineer suspect when it fails?
Host ordering bugs (flush before writes completed), layers that swallow flushes, an incorrect VWC report, firmware that acknowledges without committing, and FTL metadata not persisted with the data.

Writing user data to a fresh NAND page is useless after a power failure if the mapping "LBA 0x100000 now lives at die 3, block 812, page 47" was only in DRAM. Controllers either persist mapping deltas in a journal, write reverse-mapping information alongside the data in the page's spare area so the map can be rebuilt on power-up, or both. Either way, a correct Flush must make the data findable, not just written. Bugs here produce the strangest power-loss failures: data is physically on the NAND but the LBA returns old content.

On the host side, nothing special happens: Flush carries no data, so the input–output memory management unit (IOMMU) and memory controller are idle for it apart from the SQE fetch and CQE post.

Specification view

Flush is defined in the NVM Express Base Specification, Revision 2.4 — I/O commands: Flush command. In the NVMe 2.x document family it lives in the Base specification because it applies across I/O command sets; in NVMe 1.x it was part of the single combined specification's NVM command set chapter. Version-dependent The Identify Controller VWC field and the Volatile Write Cache feature are in the Base specification too. FUA, the per-command alternative, is defined with the Write command in the NVM Express NVM Command Set Specification, Revision 1.3. Shutdown notification is in the Base specification's controller register and controller shutdown descriptions.

Flush opcode 00h, no data transfer. Volatile Write Cache feature identifier 06h with enable bit 0. VWC bit 0 indicates presence of a volatile write cache. CC.SHN encodes 01b normal and 10b abrupt shutdown notification; CSTS.SHST reports 10b when shutdown processing is complete. The SMART / Health Information log reports a count of unsafe shutdowns.

The latency model, the claim that frequent flushes increase write amplification, and the interpretation that Flush is "a fence over the past, not a barrier for the future" are reasoned from the normative scope rule and SSD architecture.

How the cache is organized and drained, whether Flush blocks or slows other commands, how fast Flush completes, whether a PLP drive reports a volatile cache, whether a Flush for one namespace also drains others, and the controller's behavior if power fails during a Flush are implementation-dependent.

VWC bits 2:1 (Flush with NSID FFFFFFFFh support reporting) appeared in NVMe 1.4; earlier controllers report 00b. The move of Flush into the Base specification came with the 2.0 document restructuring. Always match behavior to the revision the controller reports.

  • The specification does not bound Flush latency.
  • It does not require Flush to act as a barrier for later commands.
  • It does not say how the device makes data non-volatile.
  • Check in the specification: the exact status for NSID FFFFFFFFh when unsupported; the wording for a present-but-disabled cache; whether and how the scope rule refers to commands completed by other controllers in the subsystem.

Never assume that a successful Flush completion proves the data is on media if any intermediate layer (hypervisor, RAID adapter, USB/Thunderbolt bridge, network storage gateway) sits between your software and the controller. Each layer must forward Flush faithfully; any of them can lie.

Protocol view

Flush is the purest example of a command whose PCIe footprint tells you nothing about its work: four packets on the link for possibly milliseconds of internal activity.

Request, response and ordering

  • Request. The SQE; no data, no Physical Region Page (PRP) or Scatter Gather List (SGL).
  • Response. One CQE with status. Success means the guarantee holds. An error status (for example an internal error, or Invalid Namespace or Format for an NSID problem) means the host must treat durability as not achieved for that namespace and escalate.
  • Ordering. PCIe ordering does not enter into it: there is no data to order. The NVMe ordering that matters is the host's own: wait for writes, then submit Flush, then wait for Flush, then submit dependent writes.
  • Concurrency. Other commands on the same or other queues keep flowing during the Flush. A trace often shows reads and writes from other queues completing while the Flush is outstanding.

Writing write through into /sys/block/nvme0n1/queue/write_cache does not disable the drive's cache. It tells the kernel to stop sending flushes, because it now believes there is no cache. On a drive with an enabled volatile cache and no PLP, that is how you manufacture data loss. Changing the device's actual cache state is done with Set Features (feature 06h), and is a Caution lab operation on any machine you care about.

On the kernel side, the relevant pieces are the block layer's flush machinery (preflush/postflush sequencing for requests marked REQ_PREFLUSH and REQ_FUA) and the NVMe driver's translation of a flush request into an NVMe Flush command with the namespace's own NSID. The kernel document Documentation/block/writeback_cache_control.rst describes the contract between filesystems and drivers. If a command times out (by default the NVMe I/O timeout is 30 seconds, controlled by the nvme_core.io_timeout module parameter), the driver logs a timeout for the command's tag and queue, attempts an Abort (Admin opcode 08h), and may reset the controller.

Code / pseudocode

First, the host-side commit protocol in pseudocode. The waiting is the whole point.

/* commit(): make a transaction durable, then publish it */
for each data block d in txn:
    submit_write(d);                       /* FUA clear: let the device batch */
wait_for_all_completions(txn.writes);      /* (1) every write must be COMPLETED ... */

if (ctrl.vwc_present && ctrl.vwc_enabled) {
    status = submit_and_wait(FLUSH, nsid);  /* (2) ... before the Flush is SUBMITTED */
    if (status != SUCCESS)
        return FAIL_NOT_DURABLE;            /* never ignore a Flush error */
}

status = submit_and_wait(WRITE_FUA, commit_record);  /* (3) the record itself */
return status == SUCCESS ? COMMITTED : FAIL_NOT_DURABLE;

/* Broadcast flush only if the controller says it is supported */
bool can_broadcast_flush(uint8_t vwc) {
    return (vwc & 0x1) && ((vwc >> 1) & 0x3) == 0x3;   /* bits 2:1 == 11b */
}

Second, a runnable toy model of a volatile cache, Flush, FUA and power loss. It shows which completed writes survive under four configurations. The randomized background destaging stands in for the controller's freedom to move data whenever it likes; the seed makes the run repeatable.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""flush_sim.py - a toy model of a volatile write cache, Flush, FUA and power loss.

Simulation only (standard library). It models the *guarantees* a host may rely on,
not how any real controller schedules its cache. Times are abstract ticks.
"""
import random


class ToyController:
    def __init__(self, vwc_enabled=True, seed=1):
        self.vwc = vwc_enabled
        self.cache = {}          # lba -> data, volatile
        self.media = {}          # lba -> data, non-volatile
        self.rng = random.Random(seed)

    def write(self, lba, data, fua=False):
        """Returns when the command 'completes'."""
        if fua or not self.vwc:
            self.media[lba] = data           # durable before completion
            self.cache.pop(lba, None)
        else:
            self.cache[lba] = data           # completion only means 'accepted'

    def background_destage(self):
        """The controller may destage cached data whenever it likes (implementation choice)."""
        for lba in list(self.cache):
            if self.rng.random() < 0.3:
                self.media[lba] = self.cache.pop(lba)

    def flush(self):
        """Everything completed before this Flush was submitted becomes non-volatile."""
        self.media.update(self.cache)
        self.cache.clear()

    def read(self, lba):
        return self.cache.get(lba, self.media.get(lba))

    def power_loss(self):
        lost = sorted(self.cache)
        self.cache.clear()                   # no power-loss protection in this model
        return lost


def scenario(name, vwc, use_flush, use_fua):
    c = ToyController(vwc_enabled=vwc)
    for lba in range(8):
        c.write(lba, f"v1-{lba}", fua=use_fua)
        if lba % 3 == 0:
            c.background_destage()
    if use_flush:
        c.flush()
    c.write(100, "journal-commit", fua=use_fua)   # written after the Flush completed
    before = {lba: c.read(lba) for lba in list(range(8)) + [100]}
    lost = c.power_loss()
    after = {lba: c.read(lba) for lba in before}
    survived = sum(1 for k in before if before[k] == after[k])
    print(f"{name:<34} completed=9 survived={survived} lost LBAs={lost}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    scenario("VWC on, no Flush, no FUA", True, False, False)
    scenario("VWC on, Flush after LBAs 0-7", True, True, False)
    scenario("VWC on, every write FUA", True, False, True)
    scenario("VWC off (or not present)", False, False, False)

Expected output:

VWC on, no Flush, no FUA           completed=9 survived=3 lost LBAs=[1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 100]
VWC on, Flush after LBAs 0-7       completed=9 survived=8 lost LBAs=[100]
VWC on, every write FUA            completed=9 survived=9 lost LBAs=[]
VWC off (or not present)           completed=9 survived=9 lost LBAs=[]

Notice the second line: the Flush protected LBAs 0–7, but the "journal-commit" write to LBA 100 came after the Flush and was lost. That is correct behavior — and a reminder that the commit record itself needs FUA or a second Flush.

Worked example

Scenario (synthetic). A key-value store on the lab system commits transactions of 8 KiB of data plus a 4 KiB commit record. Measured on the lab SSD (illustrative values): cached 4 KiB write 12 µs, cached 8 KiB write 15 µs, FUA 4 KiB write 180 µs, Flush with ≤ 16 KiB dirty 220 µs. The device reports VWC bit 0 = 1 and no PLP.

Design 1 — Flush per transaction

Write data (15 µs) → wait → Flush (220 µs) → wait → FUA commit (180 µs) → wait. Per-transaction latency ≈ 415 µs plus host overhead; a single thread achieves at most about 1 / 415 µs ≈ 2,400 commits per second.

Design 2 — Group commit of 32 transactions

Write 32 × 8 KiB of data concurrently (at queue depth 32 the batch might finish in ≈ 60 µs on this device), Flush (with 256 KiB dirty, perhaps ≈ 400 µs), FUA write of one commit record covering all 32 (180 µs). ≈ 640 µs for 32 commits → about 50,000 commits per second per thread, at the cost of each transaction waiting up to one batch period.

Design 3 — Rely on a PLP drive

On a drive that reports no volatile write cache, the kernel suppresses flushes and FUA. Per-transaction cost is just the writes: ≈ 30 µs. The durability now rests entirely on the drive's protection circuitry and firmware.

What we learned

  • Flush cost is amortized by batching; the specification's scope rule (completed-before-submission) is exactly what makes batching correct.
  • The correct order — data completions, then Flush, then commit — is non-negotiable in Designs 1 and 2.
  • Numbers like 220 µs and 180 µs are illustrative; measure your own device at your own dirty-set sizes. Derived

Debugging connection

Flush bugs announce themselves in three ways: data loss after power failure, Flush latency or timeouts, and Flush errors. Each has a disciplined path.

Data loss or inconsistency after power failure

  1. Establish the claim. Which data was lost? Was it old content (reverted) or garbage (torn or corrupted)? Reverted content points to unflushed cache; garbage points to atomicity or media.
  2. Prove the host asked. Using tracepoints (or an analyzer), show a Flush (or FUA writes) after the lost data's write completed and before the power cut. Many investigations end here: no Flush was ever sent, because an application, filesystem option or hypervisor dropped it.
  3. Prove the device acknowledged. The Flush CQE with success must exist.
  4. Check the report. VWC bit 0, feature 06h state, SMART unsafe shutdowns increment.
  5. Reproduce. Power-cut test with a signature-writing tool that records which completions it observed. If flushed data is lost on a device that acknowledged Flush, the device (or a layer below the host's tracepoint) is at fault.

Flush latency or timeout

Measure dirty data at Flush time (bytes written since the last Flush). If latency scales with it, the device is behaving normally and the host should flush more often or smaller. If latency is long even with little dirty data, suspect device internals: garbage collection, thermal throttling (check temperature and throttling counters in the SMART log), or firmware issues. A Flush that never completes leads to the Linux timeout and abort path; capture the kernel log and, if possible, an analyzer trace showing that the SQE was fetched (the device knows about the command) and no CQE followed.

Flush errors

Decode Status Code Type and Status Code. An NSID-related error points at host use of FFFFFFFFh or a detached namespace. An internal error on Flush is serious: durability of previously completed writes is now uncertain. Pull the Error Information log page and the SMART log; consider the device suspect.

See Chapter 44 — NVMe Errors for status decoding and Chapter 48 — NVMe Reset for what happens after a timeout.

Senior engineer insight

The NVMe specification defines one link in a long chain: application → language runtime → filesystem → block layer → (device mapper, RAID, hypervisor, virtual disk format) → driver → controller → firmware → media. The guarantee the application wants exists only if every layer forwards flushes and honors their ordering. When I review a storage design, I ask for each layer: "Show me where flush requests enter, where they leave, and what this layer acknowledges before the layer below has."

The second thing I look at is the honesty of VWC. A drive that reports no volatile cache is promising that every completed write survives power loss. That promise should be verified by power-cut testing on every firmware release, because a regression there is invisible to every functional test.

The third is the scope rule's interaction with multi-queue drivers. Linux spreads writes across per-CPU queues. "Completed before the Flush was submitted" is about completions, not queues, so a Flush on queue 3 covers completed writes from queue 7 — but only if the host waited for those completions. Custom user-space drivers (for example built on SPDK-style polling) sometimes track completions per queue and get this wrong.

A final senior observation: when you see Flush latencies that are suspiciously constant and tiny on a drive that reports a volatile cache with no PLP, treat it as a red flag worthy of a power-cut test, not as good news.

Exercises

  1. Decode VWC values 00h, 01h, 05h, 07h and say whether the host should send Flush at all and whether it may use NSID FFFFFFFFh.
  2. A trace shows Write CID 0x10 submitted at t = 0 and completed at t = 50 µs, Flush CID 0x11 submitted at t = 40 µs and completed at t = 300 µs. Is the Write covered?
  3. Explain how a hypervisor that caches guest writes in host random-access memory (RAM) must handle guest Flush commands to preserve the guest's durability.
  4. Why might frequent Flush commands shorten an SSD's life?
  5. Write the CC and CSTS register sequence for a normal shutdown, naming the fields.
Answers

1. 00h: no volatile cache; Flush unnecessary (harmless, completes successfully); broadcast support not indicated. 01h: cache present; flush per namespace; broadcast support not indicated (pre-1.4 behavior) so do not rely on it. 05h: cache present; broadcast not supported. 07h: cache present; broadcast supported.

2. No. The write completed (50 µs) after the Flush was submitted (40 µs), so it was in flight at submission and is outside the guarantee.

3. It must, on a guest Flush, write all guest data it has acknowledged into the host's storage and then issue a real Flush (e.g. fsync on the image file) to the physical device, and only then complete the guest's Flush. Alternatively it must report no volatile cache to the guest and write through with durability on every write.

4. Each Flush may force the controller to program partially filled pages (with padding) or close blocks early, increasing NAND writes per host write (write amplification).

5. Host writes CC.SHN = 01b (normal shutdown notification); host polls CSTS.SHST until it reads 10b (shutdown processing complete); then power may be removed. During processing CSTS.SHST reads 01b.

Quiz

  1. Which writes does a successful Flush guarantee are on non-volatile media?

    • All writes submitted before the Flush completes
    • Writes to the namespace that completed before the Flush was submitted
    • All writes in the same Submission Queue
    • Only writes with FUA set
    Answer and reasoning

    The scope is defined by completion before submission. In-flight and later writes may happen to be flushed but are not guaranteed.

  2. What is the opcode of Flush, and how many data bytes does it transfer?

    • 00h, none
    • 01h, none
    • 00h, 4 KiB
    • 09h, 16 bytes per range
    Answer and reasoning

    Flush is 00h; its low bits 00b indicate no data. 01h is Write; 09h is Dataset Management.

  3. Identify Controller VWC = 01h. What should a host conclude?

    • No cache is present
    • A cache is present and Flush with NSID FFFFFFFFh is supported
    • A cache is present; broadcast flush support is not indicated
    • The cache is power-loss protected
    Answer and reasoning

    Bit 0 = 1 means present; bits 2:1 = 00b means support for FFFFFFFFh is not indicated. VWC never reports PLP directly.

  4. Which Feature Identifier controls the volatile write cache?

    • 01h
    • 02h
    • 07h
    • 06h
    Answer and reasoning

    Volatile Write Cache is Feature 06h; bit 0 of its value enables the cache.

  5. Writing "write through" to /sys/block/nvme0n1/queue/write_cache does what?

    • Disables the SSD's volatile cache with Set Features
    • Makes the kernel stop issuing flushes and FUA for that device
    • Enables FUA on every write
    • Nothing
    Answer and reasoning

    It changes only the kernel's belief about the device. If the device really has a volatile cache, durability is lost.

  6. True or false: commands submitted after a Flush cannot complete before the Flush completes.

    • True
    • False
    Answer and reasoning

    False. Flush is not a barrier. NVMe does not order separate commands.

  7. Why can an SSD with power-loss protection reasonably report no volatile write cache?

    Model answer

    Because the specification's meaning of "volatile" is about what survives power loss from the host's point of view. If capacitors guarantee buffered data reaches non-volatile media after power is lost, completed writes are effectively durable, so there is nothing for Flush to do; reporting no volatile cache lets hosts skip flushes and FUA.

  8. After an outage, a database finds a committed transaction's data pages contain old content, but the commit record is present. List the likely causes in order of suspicion.

    Model answer

    (1) The host did not Flush between the data writes completing and writing the commit record (or submitted the Flush before data completions). (2) A layer between the database and the drive dropped or acknowledged flushes falsely (hypervisor, virtual disk cache mode, RAID controller cache without battery). (3) The device acknowledged Flush without committing or without persisting FTL metadata. Evidence: tracepoints/analyzer for Flush ordering; configuration of intermediate layers; power-cut reproduction.

  9. Give a formula-level explanation for why Flush latency grows with the time since the previous Flush.

    Model answer

    Tflush ≈ Tfw + Ddirty/Rprog + Tmeta. Dirty data Ddirty accumulates with write rate × time since last Flush (minus whatever background destaging removed), so longer intervals mean more to drain.

  10. Which register fields does a host use to perform an orderly shutdown that commits the cache?

    Model answer

    CC.SHN (write 01b for normal shutdown) and CSTS.SHST (poll until 10b, shutdown processing complete).

Interview questions

  1. Beginner. What does the NVMe Flush command do?

    Model answer

    It commits data and metadata of previously completed writes for the specified namespace from any volatile cache to non-volatile media, completing only when that is done. With no volatile cache it does nothing and succeeds.

  2. Beginner. How does a host know whether it needs to send Flush?

    Model answer

    Identify Controller's VWC field bit 0 reports a volatile write cache; Get Features 06h reports whether it is enabled. If no volatile cache is present (or it is disabled), Flush is unnecessary.

  3. Intermediate. Difference between Flush and FUA?

    Model answer

    FUA (Write CDW12 bit 30) makes that write durable before its completion; it does not affect other cached data. Flush makes all writes completed before its submission durable for the namespace. Filesystems combine them: Flush to make earlier writes durable, then FUA for the commit record.

  4. Intermediate. What do VWC bits 2:1 mean and why were they added?

    Model answer

    They report whether Flush with NSID FFFFFFFFh (all namespaces) is supported: 10b not supported, 11b supported, 00b not indicated. Added in NVMe 1.4 because controllers differed in handling the broadcast form, and hosts need a reliable way to know.

  5. Advanced. A Flush takes 40 ms on a client SSD. Is that a bug?

    Model answer

    Not necessarily. Compute the dirty data at Flush time; at ~2 GB/s program bandwidth, 80 MiB takes ~40 ms. If the dirty set was small, investigate GC, thermal throttling and firmware. Also check whether concurrent I/O was slowed; that tells you the device prioritized draining.

  6. Advanced. How does Linux ensure the journal is durable before the commit record?

    Model answer

    The filesystem waits for the journal writes to complete, then issues the commit write marked REQ_PREFLUSH | REQ_FUA. The block layer's flush machinery issues a Flush first (if the device has a write-back cache), waits for it, then sends the commit write with FUA (or a post-flush if FUA is not supported).

  7. Senior. How would you prove that a device honors Flush?

    Model answer

    A power-cut test harness: write unique, checksummed signatures; record exactly which writes and Flushes completed before the cut, using a host whose own logging is durable (e.g. on another device or a network log); cut power at random times, including during Flush and immediately after Flush completion; on restore verify that every write completed before a completed Flush is present and intact. Run thousands of cycles across temperatures, fill levels and firmware versions. Also check SMART unsafe shutdown counts increment as expected.

  8. Senior. A cloud provider wants to report no volatile cache to guests while using host RAM caching for speed. What do you tell them?

    Model answer

    That reporting no volatile cache promises guests that every completed write survives power loss. Host RAM caching breaks that promise unless the host RAM is itself protected (battery/uninterruptible power supply (UPS) with orderly flush on outage, replicated cache) with failure modes analyzed. Otherwise report a volatile cache and honor guest Flush by flushing to stable storage.

Chapter summary

Flush (opcode 00h, no data) commits to non-volatile media the data and metadata of writes to the namespace that completed before the Flush was submitted. It is not a barrier, does not cover in-flight commands, and says nothing about atomicity. Identify Controller's VWC field reports whether a volatile cache exists (bit 0) and, since 1.4, whether Flush with NSID FFFFFFFFh is supported (bits 2:1); feature 06h enables or disables the cache. Orderly shutdown (CC.SHN/CSTS.SHST) and power-loss protection are the other paths to durability. Flush latency grows with dirty data and can disturb other I/O; batching and group commit amortize it. The most dangerous Flush failures are invisible on the link: layers or firmware that acknowledge without committing.

Do not move on until you can:

  • state the Flush scope rule and draw the timeline that shows which writes it covers
  • decode VWC bit 0 and bits 2:1 and say what the host should do for each value
  • explain the difference between Flush, FUA and orderly shutdown
  • write a correct commit protocol and justify every wait
  • recognize a Flush in a trace and interpret its latency
  • investigate a post-power-loss inconsistency with evidence from each layer
