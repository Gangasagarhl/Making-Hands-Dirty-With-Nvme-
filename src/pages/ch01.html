A computer is a collection of workers moving information. This chapter introduces the workers one by one, shows how they hand information to each other, and ends by following a single request all the way from a program to a solid-state drive and back. Everything later in the course is a closer look at one of these hand-offs.

Why this chapter exists

NVM Express (NVMe), the storage protocol, and PCI Express (PCIe), the interconnect, are not magic. They are answers to very old questions: how does a thinker that can only hold a few numbers at a time get work done on enormous amounts of information that lives somewhere else? How does it ask for that information, how does it know when it has arrived, and how does it avoid doing all the carrying itself?

If you understand those questions, every design decision in NVMe and PCIe will feel inevitable instead of arbitrary. If you skip them, you will spend the rest of the course memorizing acronyms. This chapter builds the questions.

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

  • explain what information is and how bits and bytes represent it, and read hexadecimal;
  • describe the jobs of the central processing unit (CPU), its registers, memory, devices, controllers, buses and drivers;
  • distinguish virtual addresses, physical addresses and device-visible addresses, and say why there are three;
  • explain the difference between the CPU moving data itself and a device moving it by direct memory access (DMA);
  • explain why interrupts exist and what they replace;
  • trace one storage read through the chain CPU → memory → input/output (I/O) → bus → PCIe → device → NVMe → solid-state drive (SSD), naming the worker at each step;
  • inspect the workers in your own Linux machine with read-only commands.

The story

Imagine a small, busy town whose whole job is answering questions.

In the middle of town sits the Thinker. The Thinker is brilliant and incredibly fast, but has a strange limitation: she can only hold a handful of numbers in her hands at once. She keeps them on a tiny notepad on her desk. Everything she knows right now fits on that notepad.

Next to her office is the Great Wall of Boxes. It is a wall with millions of little boxes, each with a number painted on it: box 0, box 1, box 2, all the way up. Each box holds one small number. The Thinker can reach any box quickly, but not as quickly as her notepad, and when the town's lights go out at night, every box on the Wall is emptied.

Far away, across the river, is the Warehouse. It holds far more than the Wall ever could, and it never forgets, even in the dark. But it is far away, and it has its own staff who speak their own careful language.

Between the town and the Warehouse runs a road. Not a lazy country lane: a superhighway with several lanes in each direction, where trucks carry sealed packages with addresses written on them.

When the Thinker needs a page from a book in the Warehouse, she does not drive there herself. That would be a waste of the smartest person in town. Instead she writes a request slip: "Please bring the page at shelf 2,048 and put it in Wall boxes 8,000 to 12,095." She puts the slip in a tray on the Wall, then rings a bell at the Warehouse's office in town. Then she goes back to thinking about other things.

The Warehouse's chief clerk hears the bell, sends a truck to fetch the slip, finds the page, and sends trucks full of words straight to the Wall boxes the Thinker asked for. The clerk does not bother the Thinker while doing it. When everything has arrived, the clerk writes a little note, "Done, slip number 42, all good", puts it in an answer tray on the Wall, and taps the Thinker on the shoulder.

The Thinker reads the note, sees her page is ready in the Wall boxes, and carries on.

Nobody in this town is very complicated. The Thinker thinks. The Wall remembers for a short while. The Warehouse remembers forever. The road carries packages. The clerk fetches and delivers. The slips and notes say what to do and what was done. A computer is just this: a collection of workers moving information.

Think of a modern logistics company. Head office (the CPU) makes decisions but never touches a parcel. A local depot (memory) holds stock that is needed today; it is fast to reach but small and cleared out every night. A national distribution center (the SSD) holds everything, permanently, but is a long drive away. Trucks on a motorway (the PCIe link) carry consignments between them, each with a waybill (a packet header) saying where it goes and how big it is. Head office places orders by writing them into a shared order system (a queue in memory) and pinging the distribution center (a doorbell); the distribution center's own fleet (DMA) delivers straight into the depot, then sends a delivery confirmation (a completion) and a text message (an interrupt).

What the analogies hide. Real "workers" are circuits; none of them decides anything on its own. The clerk in the story seems to choose what to do, but a device controller only follows its firmware and the rules of its protocol. The story's road carries one truck at a time per lane; a real PCIe link splits each packet across all of its lanes byte by byte. The Wall in the story is a single wall; real memory is reached through several layers of caches and an address-translation system, so "box 8,000" for the CPU may be a different number for the device. And in the story the tap on the shoulder is free; a real interrupt costs the CPU thousands of instructions' worth of time, which is why later chapters care so much about reducing them.

Where they mislead. The logistics analogy suggests the distribution center is slow because it is far away. An SSD is slow compared with memory mostly because of the physics of flash, not distance; the PCIe trip itself takes on the order of a microsecond, while reading flash takes tens of microseconds.

Beginner explanation

A computer does three things with information: it stores it, it moves it, and it transforms it. Transforming (adding, comparing, deciding) is what the CPU is famous for, but most of the effort in a real system goes into moving. The CPU cannot transform data until it has been moved close to it, and the result is useless until it has been moved somewhere it will be kept or shown.

Here is the chain of workers involved when a program reads part of a file from an NVMe solid-state drive. Read it top to bottom once now; by the end of the chapter you will be able to explain each box.

Three ideas will keep coming back:

  1. Everything has an address. Memory boxes have addresses. Device registers have addresses. Even interrupts, in modern systems, are writes to special addresses. Moving information is mostly about getting addresses right.
  2. The CPU should not carry bulk data. It is the most expensive worker. Devices that can move data into memory on their own (DMA) free it to do useful work.
  3. Somebody has to say "done". When work happens in parallel, the requester needs a reliable way to learn it has finished: either by checking (polling) or by being told (an interrupt).

Store, move, transform. Addresses everywhere. The CPU delegates the carrying. Someone must say "done". If you remember only four things from Chapter 1, remember these.

Engineering explanation

This section takes the fifteen building blocks one at a time. Each gets the same treatment: a one-line kid analogy, a simple explanation, the engineering explanation, a diagram, a practical example, a common misconception, and a mini exercise with its answer hidden.

What is information?

Information is anything that tells you something you did not know: which door to open, what time it is, which page of the book to read.

Simple explanation

Information is a choice made out of a set of possibilities. A light switch can say one of two things: on or off. A traffic light can say one of three. A letter of the alphabet is one of 26. The more possibilities there are, the more information one choice carries.

Engineering explanation

Computers represent all information as patterns of two-valued signals, because two-valued circuits (high or low voltage, charged or not charged, magnetized one way or the other) are cheap, fast and very tolerant of noise. A text file, a photograph, a storage command and a PCIe packet are all, physically, long sequences of these two-valued choices. What makes them different is only the agreement about what the pattern means: a file format, a character encoding, a command layout in a specification. A large part of this course is learning those agreements.

Practical example

The four bytes 06 00 00 00 could be the number 6 written to a doorbell, part of a picture, or the start of a command. A protocol analyzer shows you bytes; only knowledge of the agreement (the specification) tells you which.

Common misconception

"Data and commands are different kinds of things." They are not, physically. An NVMe command is just 64 bytes in memory, sitting next to ordinary data. The controller treats them as a command because the driver told it, through the queue's address, that those bytes are commands.

A traffic light has three states. At least how many bits do you need to represent which state it is in?

Answer

Two bits. One bit gives two possibilities, two bits give four (00, 01, 10, 11), which is enough for three states with one pattern unused. In specifications, unused patterns like that are usually called reserved.

Bits and bytes

A bit is a single light switch. A byte is a row of eight switches. With eight switches you can make 256 different patterns, enough for every letter, digit and punctuation mark.

Simple explanation

A bit (binary digit) is 0 or 1. A byte is 8 bits and can hold a number from 0 to 255. Bigger numbers use several bytes: 2 bytes (16 bits), 4 bytes (32 bits, called a doubleword or DW in PCIe and NVMe), 8 bytes (64 bits, a quadword).

Engineering explanation

Engineers write binary numbers in hexadecimal (base 16) because each hex digit is exactly four bits. Digits run 0–9 then A–F (10–15). A byte is two hex digits; a 32-bit register is eight. Code writes hex with a 0x prefix (0x1008); specifications often use an h suffix (1008h).

Two more conventions matter immediately:

  • Bit numbering. Bit 0 is the least significant (worth 1), bit 31 the most significant in a 32-bit value. Field tables in specifications say things like "bits 15:8", meaning bits 15 down to 8 inclusive.
  • Byte order. When a multi-byte number is stored in memory, the least significant byte can be stored at the lowest address (little-endian) or the most significant first (big-endian). x86 and ARM systems normally run little-endian, and NVMe data structures are little-endian. PCIe packet headers on the wire are transmitted most significant byte first. Analyzers hide most of this, but when you decode raw bytes by hand it will bite you.

Memory

Memory is the Great Wall of Boxes: millions of numbered boxes, each holding one byte, all forgotten when the power goes off.

Simple explanation

Main memory, usually called RAM (random-access memory), is where the computer keeps everything it is working on right now: programs, their data, the operating system, and buffers waiting to go to or from devices. "Random access" means any box can be reached in about the same time as any other, unlike a tape.

Engineering explanation

Physically, main memory is DRAM (dynamic RAM) on modules plugged into the motherboard, connected to a memory controller built into the CPU package. Each byte has a physical address. DRAM is volatile: it loses its contents without power, and even with power it must be refreshed thousands of times per second. Access takes on the order of 100 nanoseconds from the CPU's point of view; to hide that, CPUs keep small, fast copies of recently used memory in caches. Devices also read and write main memory directly (DMA, below), which is why the same buffer can be touched by both the CPU and an SSD. The queues NVMe uses live here, in ordinary host memory.

Practical example

The reference machine's I/O submission queue 1 occupies 64 KiB of host memory starting at 0x0000_0001_2340_0000: 1024 slots of 64 bytes. To the memory chips these are just bytes; the meaning "queue" exists only in the agreement between the NVMe driver and the controller.

Common misconception

"An SSD is a kind of memory, so it is like RAM." Both store bits, but RAM is byte-addressable, volatile and about a thousand times faster to access; an SSD is block-addressed, non-volatile, and reached through a device protocol. Chapter 2 explains why that difference shapes everything.

How many bytes are in the reference machine's I/O SQ1, and what is the address of slot 5?

Answer

1024 × 64 = 65,536 bytes (64 KiB). Slot 5 starts at base + 5 × 64 = 0x1_2340_0000 + 0x140 = 0x1_2340_0140.

CPU

The CPU is the Thinker: very fast, very clever, but able to hold only a handful of numbers at once.

Simple explanation

The central processing unit (CPU) executes instructions, one simple step at a time: load a number from memory, add two numbers, compare, jump somewhere else, store a number to memory. Billions of these steps per second, on each of several cores, add up to everything a computer does.

Engineering explanation

A modern CPU package contains several cores (each an independent instruction executor), multiple levels of cache, a memory controller, and, importantly for us, the root complex: the logic that connects the CPU and memory to PCIe. Each core fetches instructions from memory, decodes them and executes them, using registers as its working space. Instructions that touch memory go through the memory-management unit (address translation, below) and the caches. Instructions that touch device registers go out through the root complex as I/O transactions. The CPU is the only worker that runs software; devices run firmware on their own small processors, but the CPU runs the operating system and drivers that coordinate everything.

Practical example

When the NVMe driver "rings a doorbell", the CPU core is executing a single store instruction whose target address happens to belong to the SSD's registers. The core does not know it is talking to a drive; the address decoding in the root complex routes the store out over PCIe.

Common misconception

"The CPU reads the file from the disk." In a modern system the CPU never touches the bulk data on its way in. It writes a 64-byte request, rings a doorbell, and later reads a 16-byte completion. The device moves the data into memory by itself.

Name two jobs in the read path that only the CPU does, and one that the CPU deliberately does not do.

Answer

Only the CPU runs the application and the driver that builds the NVMe command and writes the doorbell; it also handles the interrupt and checks the completion status. It does not copy the data from the device into memory: the device's DMA engine does that.

Registers

Registers are the Thinker's notepad: a few slots right under her hands. Devices have notepads too, and the Thinker can write on them from across town.

Simple explanation

A register is a small, named storage location built directly into a piece of hardware. CPU registers hold the numbers the CPU is currently working on. Device registers hold a device's settings and status, and act as its control panel.

Engineering explanation

There are two very different kinds of register, and confusing them causes real misunderstanding:

  • CPU registers (general-purpose, instruction pointer, flags…) are inside the core. They are not in any address space; instructions name them directly. Access takes well under a nanosecond.
  • Device registers are inside a device. The system makes them reachable by giving them addresses, usually in the memory address space (memory-mapped I/O, or MMIO). Reading or writing one is a bus transaction that may take hundreds of nanoseconds to a microsecond or more. Some device registers have side effects: writing a doorbell does not just store a number, it causes the device to start work; reading some status registers can clear them.

Each bit or group of bits in a device register is a field with a name, a width, an access type (read-only, read/write, write-1-to-clear…) and a reset value. Specifications define these field by field. NVMe's controller registers include CAP (Controller Capabilities) at offset 00h, VS (Version) at 08h, CC (Controller Configuration) at 14h, CSTS (Controller Status) at 1Ch, AQA (Admin Queue Attributes) at 24h, ASQ (Admin Submission Queue base) at 28h, ACQ (Admin Completion Queue base) at 30h, and doorbells from 1000h. Verified NVM Express Base Specification 2.4 — Controller Registers

Practical example

To check whether an NVMe controller is ready, the driver reads CSTS and looks at its Ready bit (CSTS.RDY). That single read crosses the PCIe link and waits for the answer; it is far slower than reading a CPU register, which is why NVMe was designed so that the normal I/O path needs no register reads at all.

Common misconception

"Writing a register is like writing memory." Memory remembers what you wrote and gives it back. A device register may ignore what you wrote, act on it and forget it, or return something completely different when read. Always read the field's access type.

Why is it a design goal for the NVMe fast path to avoid reading device registers, while writing them (doorbells) is acceptable?

Answer

A read is non-posted: the CPU must wait for the value to come back across PCIe before it can continue, stalling that core for around a microsecond. A write is posted: the CPU hands it to the root complex and continues immediately. Doorbell writes are cheap; register reads in the I/O path would be expensive. This is one of the main lessons of Chapter 3.

Address spaces

An address space is a numbering system for boxes. The town has more than one: the Thinker's private numbering, the true street numbering, and the numbering the Warehouse's trucks use.

Simple explanation

An address space is the set of all addresses a worker can use, and what each one refers to. Not all addresses refer to memory: some refer to device registers, some to nothing at all.

A device emits a DMA write to address 0x2_0000_0000. On the reference machine, which address space is that number in, and why can it not be assumed equal to the physical address?

Answer

It is an I/O virtual address (IOVA), because the reference machine has the IOMMU enabled. The IOMMU translates it to a physical address using tables the operating system set up for that device; the physical page could be anywhere.

Virtual memory

Each program gets its own pretend numbering for the Wall, so that two programs can both use "box 100" without bumping into each other. A helper quietly translates pretend numbers into real ones.

Simple explanation

Virtual memory gives every program the illusion of having the whole memory to itself. Programs use virtual addresses; the hardware translates them into physical addresses, page by page.

Engineering explanation

Memory is divided into pages, typically 4 KiB. The operating system keeps page tables that map each virtual page of a process to a physical page (or mark it as absent). The CPU's memory-management unit (MMU) walks these tables on every access, with a translation cache (the TLB, translation lookaside buffer) to keep it fast. Consequences that matter for storage:

  • A buffer that is contiguous in virtual memory may be scattered across many non-contiguous physical pages. That is why NVMe needs a way to describe lists of pages (Physical Region Page (PRP) lists and Scatter Gather Lists (SGLs), Chapter 36).
  • Pages can be moved or swapped out by the operating system. A page that a device is about to write by DMA must be pinned so it stays put.
  • Devices do not use the CPU's page tables. They either use physical addresses directly or go through the IOMMU's own tables.

Practical example

A program reads 12 KiB into a buffer. The driver must give the SSD three separate page addresses, not one start address and a length, because the physical pages are not adjacent.

Common misconception

"Virtual memory means swap space on disk." Swapping is one feature built on virtual memory. The core idea is address translation and isolation, and it is active on every memory access even on systems with no swap at all.

A 4 KiB-aligned buffer of 8 KiB is contiguous in virtual memory. What is the minimum and the maximum number of physical pages it could occupy?

Answer

Exactly two 4 KiB pages, always: alignment fixes the count. What varies is whether those two physical pages are adjacent. (An unaligned 8 KiB buffer could touch three pages.)

Physical memory

Physical memory is the real Wall with the real painted numbers, the one the trucks actually drive up to.

Simple explanation

Physical memory is the actual DRAM, with addresses that the memory controller decodes into a module, a chip, a row and a column.

Engineering explanation

Physical addresses are what appear on the CPU's internal fabric and, without an IOMMU, on the PCIe link in DMA requests. The physical address space contains DRAM ranges with holes for MMIO. The operating system allocates physical pages to processes, to the kernel, and to DMA buffers. For DMA, three properties matter: the page must stay resident (pinned); the address handed to the device must be the one the device should use (physical, or an IOVA mapped by the IOMMU); and caches must be coherent, meaning the device sees what the CPU wrote and vice versa. On x86 servers, PCIe DMA is normally cache-coherent by hardware; on some other platforms, drivers must explicitly flush or invalidate caches. Implementation-dependent

Practical example

free -h shows how much physical memory exists and how it is used. /proc/iomem (as root) shows where it sits in the physical address space.

Common misconception

"If the device wrote the data into memory, the CPU will see it." Only if caches are coherent and the CPU reads after the device's write is visible. That ordering is guaranteed by protocol rules (PCIe ordering, the completion-then-interrupt sequence), not by luck; Chapter 14 explains how.

Why must a DMA buffer be pinned before its address is given to an SSD?

Answer

If the operating system moved or reused the page while the command was outstanding, the device would write into memory that now belongs to something else, silently corrupting it. Pinning keeps the physical page in place until the command completes.

I/O

I/O is everything that crosses the town wall: messages going out, packages coming in.

Simple explanation

Input/output (I/O) is any transfer of information between the CPU-memory core of the computer and the outside world: keyboards, screens, networks and storage.

Engineering explanation

There are three classic ways for a CPU to perform I/O, and the history of storage interfaces is a march from the first to the third:

  1. Programmed I/O (PIO): the CPU moves every word itself, reading from a device data register and writing to memory, over and over. Simple, but the CPU is busy for the entire transfer.
  2. Polling: the CPU starts an operation and then repeatedly reads a status register until it says "done". Fast response, but wasteful if the wait is long.
  3. DMA plus interrupts: the CPU describes the work, the device moves the data into memory itself, then signals completion. The CPU is free in the meantime.

NVMe uses the third method, with a twist: completion signaling is itself done by DMA (the device writes a completion entry into memory) and the interrupt is optional; a driver may poll the completion queue in memory, which is cheap because it is a memory read, not a device-register read.

Practical example

Linux /proc/interrupts counts interrupts per device per CPU. Run a large read from an NVMe drive and watch the nvme lines climb: each count is a "done!" message.

Common misconception

"Polling is always worse than interrupts." For very fast devices, polling a completion queue in memory can give lower latency than waiting for an interrupt, at the cost of a busy CPU. Linux supports polled NVMe queues for exactly this reason. It is a trade-off, not a rule.

Which of the three I/O methods wastes the most CPU time when reading 1 MiB from a device that takes 100 µs to respond?

Answer

Programmed I/O: the CPU both waits and carries every word. Polling wastes the wait but not the carrying (if combined with DMA). DMA with interrupts wastes neither, at the cost of the interrupt overhead.

Devices

A device is a workshop outside the town centre: the Warehouse, the post office, the town crier. Each has its own staff and its own way of working.

Simple explanation

A device is a piece of hardware attached to the computer to provide some service: storing data, sending network packets, drawing pictures. An NVMe SSD is a device.

Engineering explanation

In PCIe terms, a device is identified by its location in the hierarchy (bus, device and function numbers, written like 01:00.0), and presents one or more functions, each with its own configuration space, its own Base Address Registers (BARs) mapping its registers into the physical address space, and its own interrupt resources. The device contains a controller that implements a protocol (here NVMe), firmware running on embedded processors, DMA engines, buffers, and the actual medium (NAND flash dies). Software never touches the medium directly; it only talks to the controller.

Practical example

lspci -nn lists PCIe functions with their class code. An NVMe controller shows the class "Non-Volatile memory controller" with programming interface NVM Express, class code 0108 with prog-if 02. Verified

Common misconception

"One drive = one device = one controller." Many drives are one PCIe function with one controller, but a drive can expose several functions (for example with SR-IOV, Chapter 56), and one NVM subsystem can contain several controllers sharing the same flash. Keep "device", "function", "controller" and "subsystem" separate in your head.

In the address 0000:01:00.0, which numbers are the domain, bus, device and function?

Answer

Domain (segment) 0000, bus 01, device 00, function 0. The short form 01:00.0 omits the domain.

Controllers

A controller is the chief clerk at a workshop. You never talk to the workers on the shelves; you hand slips to the clerk, and the clerk organizes everything.

Simple explanation

A controller is the part of a device that understands requests from the host, manages the device's internal resources, and reports results.

Engineering explanation

The word "controller" means slightly different things in different places. A host controller sits on the host side and drives a bus (for example an Advanced Host Controller Interface (AHCI) host controller drives Serial ATA (SATA) disks). A device controller sits inside the device. In NVMe, the "NVMe controller" is the device-side interface that the host driver talks to: it exposes registers, processes commands from queues, and posts completions. The NVMe Base Specification defines its behavior from the host's point of view; what the controller does inside (how it schedules flash operations, caches data, handles wear) is implementation-specific. Implementation-dependent

Practical example

nvme list and nvme id-ctrl /dev/nvme0 (from nvme-cli) ask the controller to describe itself through the Identify command: model, serial number, firmware revision, capabilities.

Common misconception

"The controller is the flash." The controller is a chip (often with several CPU cores of its own) that manages the flash. Two drives with identical flash can perform very differently because of their controllers and firmware.

Is the NVMe controller a host controller or a device controller? What about the AHCI controller in an older SATA system?

Answer

The NVMe controller is on the device side: it lives in the SSD. The AHCI controller is a host-side controller (usually in the chipset) that talks SATA to the drive, which has its own device controller behind it. This difference is a big part of Chapter 3.

Buses

A bus is a road. An old bus is a single street that everyone shares, so only one cart can use it at a time. A modern link is a private highway between two places.

Simple explanation

A bus is a communication path that carries data, addresses and control signals between components.

Engineering explanation

Classic buses (like the original PCI bus) were parallel and shared: many wires side by side, many devices attached, one transfer at a time, arbitration to decide who goes next, and a clock that had to reach all devices at once, which capped the speed. PCI Express replaced this with serial point-to-point links: each link connects exactly two components, carries packets over differential pairs, with an embedded clock, independently in each direction. Switches create fan-out. The word "bus" survives in PCIe (bus numbers, "bus master") even though no shared bus exists, because PCIe preserved the PCI software model so that old software kept working. Chapter 4 builds the PCIe mental model from here.

Practical example

lspci -tv draws the PCIe hierarchy as a tree. Every branch is a link; there is no shared wire.

Common misconception

"PCIe lanes are like separate buses, one per transfer." A multi-lane link is one logical link; each packet is striped across all its lanes. Four lanes do not carry four independent packets; they carry one packet four times as fast.

Give one reason a shared parallel bus cannot simply be clocked faster and faster.

Answer

At high frequencies, signals on many parallel wires arrive at slightly different times (skew), reflections from multiple attached devices distort the signal, and every device must keep up with the shared clock. Serial links with embedded clocks and point-to-point connections avoid these problems.

Drivers

A driver is an interpreter who speaks both the Thinker's language and the Warehouse's language, so the Thinker can just say "get me this page".

Simple explanation

A driver is software, usually part of the operating system kernel, that knows how to operate one kind of device and presents it to the rest of the system in a standard way.

Engineering explanation

The Linux NVMe driver (nvme and nvme-core modules) binds to any PCIe function with the NVMe class code. On start-up it maps BAR0, reads CAP, resets and configures the controller (writing AQA, ASQ, ACQ and CC, then waiting for CSTS.RDY), creates I/O queues with admin commands, sets up MSI-X interrupts, and registers block devices such as /dev/nvme0n1. During operation it translates block-layer requests into NVMe commands, places them in queues, rings doorbells, and handles completions. Because NVMe standardizes the register and command interface, one driver works with every compliant NVMe device. Before NVMe, early PCIe SSDs often needed vendor-specific drivers.

Practical example

lspci -k -s 01:00.0 shows "Kernel driver in use: nvme" for an NVMe device. ls /sys/class/nvme lists the controllers the driver created.

Common misconception

"The driver talks to the flash." The driver talks only to the controller, through registers and queues defined by the NVMe specifications. It has no knowledge of the flash layout.

Why does a single standard driver for all NVMe devices make NVMe adoption easier?

Answer

Operating systems can ship the driver in-box, so any compliant drive works without vendor software. Drive makers compete on hardware and firmware, not on driver quality, and boot support becomes possible everywhere.

Interrupts

An interrupt is a tap on the Thinker's shoulder: "Excuse me, something you were waiting for has happened."

Simple explanation

An interrupt is a signal from hardware that makes the CPU pause what it is doing, run a short piece of code (an interrupt handler), and then resume.

Engineering explanation

Interrupts let the CPU do other work while waiting, instead of polling. Historically interrupts were dedicated wires (PCI's INTA#–INTD#), shared between devices. PCIe emulates those with messages (INTx emulation), but modern devices use Message Signaled Interrupts: MSI and its extended form MSI-X. An MSI-X interrupt is literally a memory write: the device writes a small data value to a special address that the platform's interrupt controller watches. Each vector has its own address and data, so a device can target different CPU cores for different queues. On the reference machine the NVMe SSD has 33 MSI-X vectors: vector 0 for the admin queue and one per I/O queue. Interrupts are costly (context switch into the handler, cache disturbance), so NVMe allows interrupt coalescing and polled queues.

Practical example

grep nvme /proc/interrupts shows lines such as nvme0q0 (admin) and nvme0q1, nvme0q2… (I/O queues), each with a count per CPU. On a typical system, queue n is mostly serviced by the CPUs it is affined to.

Common misconception

"An interrupt carries the result." It carries only "look now". The result is in the completion queue entry that the device wrote to memory before sending the interrupt. PCIe ordering rules ensure the entry is visible when the handler looks.

Why is it important that the controller writes the completion entry before it writes the MSI-X message?

Answer

Because the handler reads the completion queue as soon as it runs. If the interrupt could overtake the completion entry, the handler would find nothing new. Both are posted memory writes from the same device, and PCIe's ordering rules keep them in order. See Chapter 14.

DMA

Direct memory access is the clerk's own trucks driving straight to the Wall, putting pages into the right boxes without asking the Thinker to carry anything.

Simple explanation

Direct memory access (DMA) lets a device read from and write to main memory on its own, without the CPU copying each byte.

Engineering explanation

A device capable of DMA is a bus master: it can originate memory transactions. On PCIe, DMA writes are Memory Write Transaction Layer Packets (TLPs) from the device to the root complex; DMA reads are Memory Read TLPs answered by Completions with data. The operating system must enable bus mastering for the function (the Bus Master Enable bit in the PCI Command register), give the device addresses that are valid in its address space (physical addresses, or IOVAs if an IOMMU is active), and keep the memory pinned. The IOMMU protects the system: a device can only reach pages that were mapped for it, so a buggy or malicious device cannot scribble over the kernel. NVMe uses DMA for everything heavy: fetching commands from submission queues, moving data to and from buffers, and writing completion entries.

Practical example

Reading a 1 GiB file from an NVMe SSD with DMA costs the CPU a few thousand commands' worth of bookkeeping. Copying the same gigabyte by programmed I/O would occupy a core for the whole transfer.

Common misconception

"DMA is a separate chip." In early PCs, a shared DMA controller chip moved data for devices. Modern PCIe devices contain their own DMA engines; there is no central DMA controller in the NVMe path.

With a Max Payload Size of 256 bytes, how many Memory Write TLPs does the SSD need to deliver 4 KiB of read data?

Answer

4096 / 256 = 16 Memory Write TLPs (assuming the buffer is aligned so that no TLP must be split further). You will see these sixteen in the trace view below.

The central concept, climbed: moving one block into memory

The single most important idea in this chapter is that storage I/O is a cooperative hand-off: the CPU describes, the device moves, the device reports. Here it is on all seven rungs.

The Thinker writes a slip, rings a bell and goes back to thinking. The Warehouse clerk fetches the slip, finds the page, has trucks carry it straight into the right Wall boxes, leaves a "done" note in the answer tray and taps the Thinker on the shoulder.

The NVMe driver builds a Read command in a submission queue in memory and writes the queue's tail doorbell. The NVMe controller fetches the command, reads the data from flash, writes it into the memory buffer named in the command by DMA, writes a completion entry into a completion queue in memory, and sends an interrupt.

Involved hardware: the CPU core (store instructions), the root complex and IOMMU (routing and translation), DRAM (queues and buffers), the PCIe link (Gen4 x4 on the reference machine), the controller's registers in BAR0, its DMA engines and its flash back end, and the platform interrupt controller that receives the MSI-X write.

The NVMe Base Specification defines queues, command and completion formats and the register map; the NVM Command Set Specification defines the Read command; the NVMe over PCIe Transport Specification defines how queues, doorbells and interrupts map onto PCIe; the PCI Express Base Specification defines the memory transactions and ordering that carry it all. The internal flash work is not specified by any of them.

On the link: one posted Memory Write (doorbell), one non-posted Memory Read (command fetch) and its Completion with Data, sixteen posted Memory Writes of 256 bytes (data), one posted Memory Write of 16 bytes (completion entry), one posted Memory Write of 4 bytes (MSI-X), and later another posted Memory Write (completion queue head doorbell).

In an analyzer this appears as a burst at the doorbell, a quiet gap of tens of microseconds while the flash works, then a dense run of upstream writes ending with the small completion and interrupt writes. The trace view below shows it row by row.

If the application hangs, find the last step that provably happened. Doorbell but no fetch: controller or bus-mastering problem. Fetch but error completion: address-translation problem. Data but no completion: controller or firmware problem. Completion and interrupt but the application still waits: host software problem. Each answer points to a different team.

Code / pseudocode

The read path in pseudocode

This is what the NVMe driver does for one read, stripped to the essentials. It is not real driver code; it shows the hand-offs.

void submit_read(queue *q, u64 lba, u32 nblocks, dma_addr_t buf)
{
    sqe *cmd = &q->sq[q->sq_tail];          /* a 64-byte slot in host memory */
    memset(cmd, 0, sizeof *cmd);
    cmd->opcode = 0x02;                       /* Read (NVM Command Set) */
    cmd->cid    = alloc_command_id(q);
    cmd->nsid   = 1;
    cmd->prp1   = buf;                        /* device-visible address of the buffer */
    cmd->slba   = lba;
    cmd->nlb    = nblocks - 1;                /* zero-based count */

    q->sq_tail = (q->sq_tail + 1) % q->depth;
    write_barrier();                          /* command must be visible before the doorbell */
    mmio_write32(q->sq_doorbell, q->sq_tail);  /* ring the bell: one posted PCIe write */
}

void on_interrupt(queue *q)
{
    while (cqe_is_new(&q->cq[q->cq_head], q->phase)) {  /* read host memory, not the device */
        cqe *c = &q->cq[q->cq_head];
        complete_request(c->cid, c->status);
        advance_head(q);
    }
    mmio_write32(q->cq_doorbell, q->cq_head);  /* tell the device which entries are consumed */
}

Fields such as the phase tag and the exact command layout are covered in Chapter 25 and Chapter 29.

A runnable model: who is busy?

This small program compares programmed I/O, where the CPU carries every word, with DMA, where the device does. The costs are illustrative teaching numbers, chosen to be plausible, not measured.

"""workers.py - compare how busy the CPU is when moving data by
programmed I/O (the CPU carries every word) versus DMA (the device carries).
All costs are illustrative teaching numbers, not measurements."""

WORD_BYTES = 8            # the CPU moves 8 bytes per load/store pair under PIO
PIO_NS_PER_WORD = 250     # one uncached device-register read + one memory store
DEVICE_LATENCY_US = 80    # time for the device to fetch data from flash
SETUP_NS = 600            # DMA: CPU builds a 64-byte command and rings a doorbell
COMPLETE_NS = 2000        # DMA: interrupt handling + completion processing
LINK_GBPS = 7.88          # Gen4 x4 payload bandwidth, GB/s, per direction


def pio(nbytes):
    words = nbytes // WORD_BYTES
    wait_ns = DEVICE_LATENCY_US * 1000          # CPU polls while device fetches
    copy_ns = words * PIO_NS_PER_WORD          # then carries every word itself
    cpu_busy = wait_ns + copy_ns
    elapsed = wait_ns + copy_ns
    return cpu_busy, elapsed


def dma(nbytes):
    transfer_ns = nbytes / LINK_GBPS            # bytes / (GB/s) = ns
    cpu_busy = SETUP_NS + COMPLETE_NS
    elapsed = SETUP_NS + DEVICE_LATENCY_US * 1000 + transfer_ns + COMPLETE_NS
    return cpu_busy, elapsed


print(f"{'size':>8} | {'PIO cpu busy':>13} {'PIO elapsed':>12} | {'DMA cpu busy':>13} {'DMA elapsed':>12}")
for size in (4096, 65536, 1048576):
    pb, pe = pio(size)
    db, de = dma(size)
    print(f"{size:>8} | {pb/1000:>10.1f} us {pe/1000:>9.1f} us | {db/1000:>10.1f} us {de/1000:>9.1f} us")

Two lessons. First, under DMA the CPU's cost per command does not grow with the transfer size: the device does the carrying. Second, under DMA the elapsed time for small transfers is dominated by the device's flash latency, not by the link: making the link faster would barely help a 4 KiB read. Worked example

A program on the reference machine reads 4 KiB from a file. Follow every worker, with every number.

  1. Application. Calls read(fd, buf, 4096). buf is a virtual address, say 0x7f3a_1c00_0000, page-aligned.
  2. File system. Finds that this part of the file is stored at byte offset 1 MiB on the namespace. With 512-byte logical blocks, that is logical block address (LBA) 1,048,576 / 512 = 2048, and 4096 bytes is 8 blocks.
  3. Block layer. Builds a request: read 8 blocks at LBA 2048 into this page.
  4. DMA mapping. The driver asks the kernel for a device-visible address for the buffer's physical page. With the IOMMU, the kernel maps it at IOVA 0x2_0000_0000 for device 01:00.0.
  5. NVMe driver. Writes a 64-byte Read command into SQ1 slot 5 at 0x1_2340_0140: opcode 02h, command identifier 0x0042, namespace 1, data pointer 0x2_0000_0000, starting LBA 2048, number of blocks 7 (zero-based, meaning 8).
  6. Doorbell. Writes 6 to the SQ1 tail doorbell at BAR0 + 0x1008 = 0xFE60_1008. One posted PCIe write.
  7. Controller. Fetches 64 bytes from 0x1_2340_0140 (one read request, one completion), decodes it, asks its flash translation layer where LBAs 2048–2055 live, and reads the flash.
  8. DMA. Writes 4096 bytes to IOVA 0x2_0000_0000 as 16 writes of 256 bytes. The IOMMU translates each to the physical page.
  9. Completion. Writes a 16-byte entry to CQ1 slot 5 at 0x1_2341_0050, with command identifier 0x0042 and success status.
  10. Interrupt. Writes the MSI-X message for vector 1. A CPU core runs the NVMe interrupt handler.
  11. Driver completion. Reads the entry, matches CID 0x0042 to the request, unmaps the IOVA, writes the CQ1 head doorbell (0xFE60_100C), and completes the request.
  12. Application. read() returns 4096. The bytes are in buf.

Juniors describe systems as boxes: "the CPU, the SSD, the link." Seniors describe them as agents that own state and hand work to each other: "the driver owns the submission queue tail; the controller owns the head; the doorbell is how ownership of new slots is transferred." Once you think this way, every bug becomes a question about which agent believed what, and when. Two agents disagreeing about who owns a piece of memory is behind a surprising fraction of the hardest storage bugs: stale doorbells, double completions, DMA into freed buffers.

In the abnormal trace, the most important information was what did not happen: no data, no completion, no interrupt. Beginners look for error messages. Seniors also look for expected events that never arrived, and they know the expected sequence well enough to notice.

Sixty microseconds of silence in a trace is not "nothing". It is the most important work in the whole operation, happening where no analyzer can see. Knowing what is and is not observable from each vantage point (link, kernel, device logs) is half of debugging.

Lab

Lab 1 — Meet the workers in your own machine

Safe lab Every step is read-only inspection or a pure simulation. Nothing you do here can change a device, a setting or any data.

Objective

Identify, on a real Linux machine, each worker from the chapter's chain (CPU, memory, I/O, bus, PCIe, device, NVMe controller, SSD) and record one piece of evidence for each.

Skills

  • Running read-only inspection commands
  • Reading hexadecimal addresses
  • Relating command output to architectural concepts
  • Keeping a lab notebook

Hardware required

Any computer running Linux (physical or virtual). An NVMe SSD is ideal but not required; without one, do steps 1–4 and use the reference machine values for the rest.

Software required

A terminal; pciutils (for lspci); optionally nvme-cli; Python 3 for step 7.

Input

Your machine as it is now.

Expected output

A one-page notebook entry: a table with one row per worker, the command that showed it, the relevant line of output, and one sentence explaining what that line proves.

Background

Re-read Address spaces, Devices and Interrupts if needed. The Linux view lists every command.

Step 1 — The CPU

Run lscpu. Record the model name, the number of CPUs, and the L3 cache size. Question for your notebook: how many cores could, in principle, each own a separate NVMe queue?

Step 2 — Memory

Run free -h, then sudo grep -i 'system ram' /proc/iomem. Record the total memory and the first two "System RAM" ranges. Convert the start of the second range from hex to decimal and to GiB.

Step 3 — The bus and PCIe

Run lspci -tv. Find the branch that leads to your storage device. Record the root port and the device's bus:device.function address.

Step 4 — The device and its controller

Run lspci -nn -k | grep -A3 -i 'non-volatile'. Record the class code in brackets (expect [0108] for NVMe) and the "Kernel driver in use" line.

Step 5 — Registers in the physical address space

Run sudo grep -B1 -A1 -i nvme /proc/iomem. Record the address range assigned to the NVMe controller's registers (its BAR). Compute its size.

Step 6 — Interrupts and DMA

Run grep -i nvme /proc/interrupts and record how many queues you see. Then run sudo lspci -vvv -s <your device> | grep -E 'Control:|MSI-X' and record whether you see BusMaster+ and MSI-X: Enable+.

Step 7 — Model the hand-off

Run workers.py from the Code section. Change DEVICE_LATENCY_US to 10 and then to 1000, and note how the DMA "elapsed" column changes while "cpu busy" does not.

Observation

Fill your notebook table: Worker · Command · Output line · What it proves · What it does not prove.

Questions

  1. Why might the NVMe register range in /proc/iomem be small (16 KiB in the reference machine) when the drive stores a terabyte?
  2. How many I/O queues did your driver create, compared with your CPU count? Why might they match?
  3. If BusMaster showed a minus sign, what could the device not do?
  4. Which step's evidence is closest to the PCIe link, and which is furthest from it?

Challenge

Start a large sequential read (for example sudo dd if=/dev/nvme0n1 of=/dev/null bs=1M count=2000 iflag=direct, which only reads) and run grep -i nvme /proc/interrupts before and after. Which queues' counts increased, and on which CPUs? Explain why. Make sure you type if= and of=/dev/null exactly; reversing them would overwrite the drive.

Hint for question 1

The register range is a control panel, not a window onto the data. Data moves by DMA into host memory buffers.

Hint for the challenge

The process doing the read ran on some CPU; the NVMe driver typically uses the queue assigned to that CPU, and that queue's interrupt is affined to it.

Solution

Q1. The BAR holds only the controller's registers (capabilities, configuration, status, doorbells, and on many devices the MSI-X table). The stored data is never mapped there; it is moved by DMA in response to commands. So the BAR size is unrelated to capacity.

Q2. Linux's NVMe driver typically creates one I/O queue per CPU, up to what the controller supports, so that each CPU can submit without locking against others. If the controller supports fewer queues than you have CPUs, several CPUs share one, and you will see fewer queues than CPUs.

Q3. With Bus Master Enable clear, the device cannot originate memory transactions: it could not fetch commands, move data, write completions or send MSI-X messages. The driver sets it during initialization; if it is clear on a working system, you are probably looking at the wrong device or the driver is not bound.

Q4. Closest to the link: the lspci -vvv link and control state (configuration-space registers) and the BAR range. Furthest: lscpu and free, which describe the host only.

Challenge. Mostly the queue (or queues) whose interrupts are affined to the CPU(s) where dd ran. If the scheduler moved dd between CPUs, several queues increase. Reads with iflag=direct bypass the page cache, so every block becomes a real NVMe command.

What a senior engineer notices

That every piece of evidence collected here is configuration and counters, not individual transactions. Linux tells you the stage is set correctly (bus mastering on, MSI-X on, queues created) and that events occurred (interrupt counts), but not what any single command did. For that you need tracing (Chapter 71) or a link analyzer (analyzer training). Knowing which tool can prove which claim is a senior skill.

Failure variants

  • No addresses in /proc/iomem (all zeros): you ran without root. The kernel hides addresses from unprivileged users.
  • No NVMe device in lspci: the machine uses SATA or a virtual disk. Look for class 0106 (SATA AHCI) instead and keep it for Chapter 3.
  • In a virtual machine: you may see a virtio block device rather than NVMe. QEMU can emulate an NVMe controller; see Lab Environment.
  • Only one nvme interrupt line: older kernels or limited MSI-X vectors; compare with the "MSI-X: Count" value.

Extension

Run sudo nvme show-regs /dev/nvme0 -H (read-only on most versions of nvme-cli; check its help first) and find CAP, CC and CSTS. You will decode them properly in Chapter 24.

Exercises

  1. Convert to decimal: 0x40, 0x200, 0x1000, 0xFE60_100C − 0xFE60_0000.
  2. On the reference machine, what is the address of SQ1 slot 1023? Of CQ1 slot 1023?
  3. Draw the town story from memory with the real names added. Then mark every arrow as "CPU does it" or "device does it".
  4. Write down the four bytes, in memory order, of the little-endian 32-bit value 0x0000_0042.
  5. A program's 16 KiB buffer starts at virtual address 0x…1800 (not page-aligned). How many 4 KiB pages does it touch?
  6. List three things the driver must do before the controller can perform any DMA.
Answers

1. 64; 512; 4096; 0x100C = 4108.

2. SQ1: 0x1_2340_0000 + 1023 × 64 = 0x1_2340_0000 + 0xFFC0 = 0x1_2340_FFC0. CQ1: 0x1_2341_0000 + 1023 × 16 = 0x1_2341_0000 + 0x3FF0 = 0x1_2341_3FF0.

3. CPU does: write command, ring SQ doorbell, handle interrupt, read completion, ring CQ doorbell. Device does: fetch command, read flash, write data, write completion, send interrupt.

4. 42 00 00 00.

5. It spans offsets 0x1800 to 0x57FF within the page grid: pages starting 0x1000, 0x2000, 0x3000, 0x4000 and 0x5000, so five pages.

6. Enable bus mastering for the function; map the queue and buffer memory for the device (IOMMU) and use those addresses; configure and enable the controller (admin queue addresses in ASQ/ACQ, then CC.EN) and create I/O queues.

Quiz

  1. In the story, the Thinker can hold only a few numbers at once on her notepad. What is the notepad?

    • Main memory
    • The SSD
    • CPU registers
    • The PCIe link
    Answer and reasoning

    CPU registers are the tiny, fastest storage inside the core. Main memory is the Wall: large, slower, volatile.

  2. Which agent moves the 4 KiB of data from the SSD into host memory in an NVMe read?

    • The CPU, using load and store instructions
    • The SSD controller's DMA engines
    • A DMA controller chip on the motherboard
    • The IOMMU
    Answer and reasoning

    The SSD is a bus master; its DMA engines issue Memory Write TLPs. The IOMMU only translates and checks addresses; it does not move data.

  3. On the reference machine, a doorbell write to 0xFE60_1008 is which kind of PCIe transaction?

    • A posted Memory Write from the root complex to the device
    • A non-posted Memory Read from the device
    • A configuration write
    • An interrupt message
    Answer and reasoning

    The CPU stores to an address inside the device's BAR0; the root complex sends it as a Memory Write, which is posted (no completion returns).

  4. What does an MSI-X interrupt physically consist of on PCIe?

    • A dedicated interrupt wire
    • A special Data Link Layer Packet (DLLP)
    • A configuration read by the CPU
    • A memory write of a data value to an address the platform watches
    Answer and reasoning

    MSI and MSI-X are message-signaled: the device writes a programmed data value to a programmed address, and the platform's interrupt controller turns that into an interrupt on a CPU.

  5. Why does a 12 KiB buffer that is contiguous in virtual memory usually need several addresses in an NVMe command?

    • Because NVMe commands can only describe 4 KiB
    • Because its physical pages need not be contiguous
    • Because PCIe packets carry at most 4 KiB
    • Because the IOMMU requires one address per page
    Answer and reasoning

    Virtual-to-physical (or IOVA) mapping is per page; adjacent virtual pages may map to scattered physical pages. NVMe's PRP lists and SGLs exist to describe such buffers. (With an IOMMU, the OS could map them contiguously in IOVA space, but drivers cannot assume it.)

  6. In the abnormal trace, which row is the first abnormal event?

    • Row 1, the doorbell
    • Row 2, the command fetch
    • Row 3, the Unsupported Request completion
    • Row 6, the controller reset
    Answer and reasoning

    Rows 1 and 2 match the normal trace. Row 3 is the first deviation; rows 4–6 are the driver's reaction to a 30-second timeout.

  7. For a 4 KiB read on the reference machine, which part of the latency is largest?

    • Reading the NAND flash
    • Transferring 4 KiB over the Gen4 x4 link
    • The doorbell write
    • The MSI-X write
    Answer and reasoning

    Flash reads take tens of microseconds; the 4 KiB transfer takes around a microsecond. This is why link speed matters little for small random reads.

  8. Open question: explain in two or three sentences why NVMe's fast path avoids reading device registers.

    Model answer

    Register reads are non-posted PCIe transactions: the CPU must wait for a completion to come back across the link, stalling for around a microsecond each time. NVMe places completions in host memory, where the CPU can read them cheaply, and uses only posted writes (doorbells) to talk to the device in the I/O path.

  9. Open question: name the three address spaces you might meet when reading a DMA address in a trace, and which one the device uses on the reference machine.

    Model answer

    Virtual (used by software), physical (used by the memory system and the root complex), and device-visible / I/O virtual addresses (used by devices when an IOMMU is present). On the reference machine the IOMMU is enabled, so the device uses IOVAs.

  10. Open question: in the normal trace, why must row 20 (the completion entry) come after row 19 (the last data write)?

    Model answer

    The completion entry tells software the data is ready. If the completion were visible before the data, software could read stale buffer contents. The controller writes them in order, and PCIe ordering rules for posted writes from the same requester keep them in order on their way to memory.

Interview questions

Beginner

  1. What is DMA, and why do storage devices use it?

    Model answer

    Direct memory access lets a device read and write host memory itself, so the CPU does not copy data. Storage devices move large volumes of data; DMA frees the CPU and lets transfers run at link speed.

  2. What is the difference between a CPU register and a device register?

    Model answer

    A CPU register is inside the core and named directly by instructions. A device register is inside a device and reached by an address, usually memory-mapped, through a bus transaction; it can have side effects.

  3. What is an interrupt?

    Model answer

    A hardware signal that makes a CPU pause, run a handler and resume. In PCIe systems it is usually a message-signaled interrupt: a memory write that the platform converts into an interrupt.

Intermediate

  1. Walk me through, at a high level, what happens when an application reads 4 KiB from an NVMe drive.

    Model answer

    System call into the file system, which maps the file offset to logical blocks; the block layer creates a request; the NVMe driver maps the buffer for DMA, writes a 64-byte Read command into a submission queue in host memory and writes the tail doorbell. The controller fetches the command by DMA, reads flash, writes the data into the buffer by DMA, writes a 16-byte completion entry into the completion queue, and sends an MSI-X interrupt. The driver processes the completion, writes the CQ head doorbell and completes the request; the system call returns.

  2. What is an IOMMU and why would you enable it?

    Model answer

    An I/O memory-management unit translates device-visible addresses to physical addresses using per-device tables. It isolates devices (a device can only reach what was mapped for it), protects against buggy or malicious DMA, enables device assignment to virtual machines, and can make scattered physical pages look contiguous to a device.

Senior

  1. An I/O times out after 30 seconds. You have a link trace showing the doorbell and the controller's command fetch, which received an Unsupported Request completion. Where do you look next and why?

    Model answer

    The first abnormal event is the refused DMA read, so the timeout and reset are consequences. I check the kernel log for an IOMMU fault naming the device and address, and compare the faulting address with the queue base the driver programmed. If they match, the mapping was missing or torn down: suspect driver lifetime bugs or a race with a reset or hot-plug path. If they differ, the controller fetched from a corrupted address: suspect controller state or a wrong queue base. I would state confidence and propose a reproduction with IOMMU fault logging and driver tracing enabled.

  2. Why is it a poor idea to call a storage latency problem "a PCIe problem" without evidence?

    Model answer

    Latency accumulates across software, the link, the controller and the media. For small I/O, the link contributes about a microsecond out of tens; the media and software dominate. Without a breakdown (software timestamps, link timestamps, device behavior), blaming any one layer is a guess. A senior first measures where the time goes.

Teach-back challenge

Explain "how a computer reads a file from an SSD" three times, aloud or in writing:

  1. To a child, in under one minute, with no technical words.
  2. To a junior engineer, in three minutes, naming CPU, memory, driver, queue, doorbell, DMA, completion and interrupt.
  3. To a senior engineer, in two minutes, focusing on which agent owns which state and what the trace would show.
Model explanations

Child: "The computer's brain is very fast but can't carry much. When it needs something from the storage box, it writes a note, rings a bell, and goes back to thinking. The storage box reads the note, puts what was asked for straight onto the brain's table, leaves a 'done' note, and taps the brain on the shoulder."

Junior: "The driver puts a 64-byte Read command into a submission queue in RAM and writes the queue's tail doorbell, which is a register on the SSD reached over PCIe. The SSD controller fetches the command by DMA, reads flash, DMA-writes the data into the buffer the command pointed to, writes a 16-byte completion entry into a completion queue in RAM, and sends an MSI-X interrupt. The driver reads the completion, checks the status and tells the block layer the request is done."

Senior: "The driver owns the SQ tail and the CQ head; the controller owns the SQ head and the CQ tail; doorbells and completion entries transfer that ownership. On the link you see one downstream posted write, a device read and its completion, a silent gap for media access, sixteen 256-byte upstream writes on our Gen4 x4 machine with MPS 256, a 16-byte completion write and an MSI-X write, then the CQ head doorbell. Ordering of posted writes guarantees the data and completion are visible before the interrupt. Failures localize to whichever hand-off is the last one we can prove."

Chapter summary

A computer is a collection of workers moving information. The CPU thinks but holds little; memory holds a lot, briefly; storage holds everything, permanently, far away. Information is bits under an agreement. Everything has an address, but there are several address spaces, and you must always know which one you are reading. Devices are reached through controllers, over buses that are today point-to-point PCIe links. Drivers translate between software and controllers. Interrupts tell the CPU that something finished; DMA lets devices move data without the CPU. An NVMe read is the cooperation of all of these: the CPU describes the work and rings a doorbell, the device fetches, moves and reports, and an interrupt closes the loop.

  • tell the town story and give every character its real name;
  • convert between hexadecimal, binary and decimal for numbers up to 0xFFFF;
  • explain the difference between virtual, physical and device-visible addresses;
  • explain why device-register reads are expensive and doorbell writes are cheap;
  • describe PIO, polling and DMA-with-interrupts, and when each makes sense;
  • draw the sequence diagram of one NVMe read from memory;
  • find the first abnormal event in this chapter's abnormal trace and explain why the later events are consequences;
