Link training is the conversation two PCIe ports hold, in training sequences called TS1 and TS2, before they can carry a single byte of NVMe traffic. This chapter reads that conversation field by field: how receivers are detected, how lanes and links are numbered, how speed is raised, how equalisation tunes the channel at 8 GT/s and above, and how to diagnose a link that will not reach L0.

Why this chapter exists

Chapter 8 named the states. This chapter explains what is said in them. When an SSD is missing, trains narrow, or stays at a low speed, the evidence is in the training sets: which lanes sent what, which fields disagreed, which phase of equalisation failed. A protocol analyzer will show you thousands of TS1 and TS2 ordered sets; you need to know which few fields matter and in what order to read them.

It is also where many "it's the SSD's fault" escalations get resolved. Training failures are frequently caused by the platform (reset sequencing, clocking, channel, retimers, firmware speed limits), and the training sets show which side stopped cooperating first.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the structure and purpose of TS1 and TS2 ordered sets and name their main fields.
  • Explain receiver detection in electrical terms and its failure modes.
  • Walk through lane and link number negotiation, including degraded width and lane reversal.
  • Explain speed negotiation: rate advertisement, the speed-change handshake through Recovery, and fallback.
  • Explain the purpose and phases of equalisation at a conceptual level (details of presets and coefficients are in Chapter 54).
  • Read synthetic training traces: a normal Gen4 x4 bring-up, a link that settles at Gen1 because equalisation fails, and a link that settles at x2.
  • Diagnose a link that refuses to reach L0, using captures and read-only Linux evidence (lspci -vvv LnkCap/LnkSta).

The story

A new class is choosing seats for a group project. The teacher (the downstream port) and the group leader (the upstream port) pass notes back and forth. Every note has the same boxes: "Group number", "Seat number", "How fast can you read?", and some tick-boxes such as "Start over" or "Stop".

First, the teacher checks which seats have someone sitting in them. Then everyone passes "hello" notes with empty boxes, until all notes are being read clearly. The teacher writes "Group 0" on every note; the group leader writes "Group 0" back on the seats that will work together. The teacher numbers the seats 0, 1, 2, 3; the leader copies the numbers back. Then they both tick "I can read faster", move to faster reading, and spend a few minutes adjusting their glasses (equalisation) until everything is sharp again.

Two professional musicians meeting for a session do something similar. They check both are plugged in (detect), play a reference note until both instruments are in tune with each other (polling and lock), agree who plays which part (configuration), then speed up the tempo and adjust their monitor mixes until each can hear the other clearly at the faster tempo (speed change and equalisation). If it does not work at the faster tempo, they go back to the slower one rather than stopping the session.

  • In the story, notes are passed one at a time. In PCIe, each lane carries its own stream of identical-format training sets simultaneously, and decisions depend on counting consecutive identical sets received on each lane.
  • Musicians adjust their own monitors. In PCIe equalisation, each receiver mostly asks the other side's transmitter to change its settings (and adapts its own receiver internally).
  • "Going back to the slower tempo" in PCIe is a defined state transition with timeouts, not a friendly suggestion; and it can happen silently, leaving a link slower than anyone expects.

Beginner explanation

Link training happens automatically every time a link comes up. It has five jobs:

  1. Receiver detection: find out which lanes have a partner.
  2. Lock: each receiver learns to read the incoming bit stream (bit lock, then symbol or block lock) using training sequences.
  3. Lane and link numbering: decide which lanes form the link and number them 0, 1, 2, …
  4. Speed: start at 2.5 GT/s, then switch to the fastest rate both support.
  5. Equalisation: at 8 GT/s and above, tune the transmitters and receivers so the faster signals arrive cleanly.

The messages used are called TS1 and TS2 (Training Sequence 1 and 2). They look alike: both carry a link number, a lane number, the supported speeds and some control flags. Roughly speaking, TS1 means "here is my proposal" and TS2 means "I agree, let's move on".

If any step fails, the link tries again, or settles for less (fewer lanes, lower speed), or gives up and goes back to Detect. Linux shows the end result in lspci -vvv as LnkSta.

Engineering explanation

Receiver detection

In Detect.Active, each transmitter lane performs receiver detection: it changes its common-mode voltage and watches how fast the line voltage follows. Behind the AC coupling capacitor, a partner receiver presents a termination to ground (during detection a defined DC impedance); charging the coupling capacitor through that termination takes noticeably longer than charging only the small parasitic capacitance of an open line. A slow change means "receiver present"; a fast change means "nothing there".

  • A powered-off or held-in-reset partner may not present termination: not detected.
  • A missing or open AC coupling capacitor: not detected on that lane.
  • Some test equipment presents termination but never sends training sets: detected, then Polling.Compliance.

The precise voltage step, timing and termination ranges are electrical specifications. Uncertain Confirm values in the Electrical Sub-Block for your revision.

TS1 and TS2 ordered sets

TS1 and TS2 are 16-symbol ordered sets transmitted on every lane simultaneously, each lane carrying its own lane-specific values. In 8b/10b mode (2.5 and 5.0 GT/s) they start with a COM control symbol; in 128b/130b mode (8.0–32.0 GT/s) they are sent in ordered-set blocks (sync header 01b) and start with an identifier byte. Flit-Mode-capable ports use additional TS formats and fields at 64 GT/s and above. Version-dependent

Progress is decided by counting: a port moves on when it has received a required number of consecutive TS1 or TS2 with matching fields on the relevant lanes and has sent a required number in return. Inconsistent fields reset the count. This is why a single noisy lane can delay training even when the others are perfect.

Lane negotiation

Lane negotiation happens in Configuration, after all detected lanes have been through Polling.

  • Link number: a downstream port that can split into several links (bifurcation) gives each a different link number. An upstream port has one link and echoes the number it receives on the lanes it wants to use; on lanes it will not use it returns PAD.
  • Lane numbers: the downstream port assigns 0…N−1. The upstream port either returns the same numbers or, if it sees them in reverse order and supports reversal, handles the reversal; otherwise the downstream port may try the reverse assignment. If no agreement is reached in time, Configuration times out.
  • Width selection: the resulting width is the largest valid width covering contiguous lanes from logical lane 0 that both ports accept. A port may also be configured (by hardware strapping or firmware) to a maximum width smaller than its physical lanes.
  • Deskew: during Config.Complete and Config.Idle, receivers align lanes using the simultaneous arrival of ordered sets.

A link that trains at x2 when x4 was expected tells you something specific: lanes 2 and 3 (or one of them) failed to participate. It never means "the link is at half speed for an unknown reason". The trace shows exactly which lanes returned PAD in Config.Linkwidth.Accept (see the x2 trace in Chapter 5).

Speed negotiation

Every TS1 and TS2 carries the sender's supported data rates. After the first arrival in L0 at 2.5 GT/s, either port may initiate a speed change, but normally the downstream port does, either autonomously (hardware wants the highest common rate) or because software wrote a Target Link Speed in Link Control 2 and set Retrain Link.

  1. The initiating port enters Recovery.RcvrLock and sends TS1 with the speed-change flag set; the partner follows into Recovery when it sees TS1.
  2. In Recovery.RcvrCfg, both send TS2 with the speed-change flag. For a change to 8 GT/s (or higher, depending on revision), the downstream port sends equalisation TS2 sets carrying the transmitter presets the upstream port should use at the new rate (equalisation Phase 0).
  3. When enough TS2 with speed change have been exchanged, both enter Recovery.Speed: send EIOS, go electrically idle, switch rate (PLL relock, encoding change if crossing 5.0 → 8.0 GT/s).
  4. At the new rate, back to Recovery.RcvrLock; for 8 GT/s and above, Recovery.Equalization runs the remaining phases (unless already done for this rate since the last fundamental reset, or bypass modes apply).
  5. Recovery.RcvrCfg and Recovery.Idle, then L0 at the new rate.
  6. If lock cannot be achieved at the new rate within the timeout, the ports return to Recovery.Speed and revert to the previous rate. The link survives at the lower rate. Whether and when a port retries the higher rate is implementation-dependent.

The new rate is the highest rate advertised by both ports, limited by Target Link Speed. Ports may step through intermediate rates or, in later revisions, use mechanisms that reach the highest rate with fewer steps. Version-dependent

Registers involved: Link Capabilities 2 (Supported Link Speeds Vector), Link Control 2 (Target Link Speed, Hardware Autonomous Speed Disable, Enter Compliance), Link Control (Retrain Link), Link Status (Current Link Speed, Link Training), Link Status 2 (equalisation status at 8 GT/s), and the Physical Layer 16.0 GT/s and 32.0 GT/s extended capabilities (equalisation status and settings at those rates). Equalisation (conceptual)

At 8 GT/s and above, a fixed transmitter setting cannot suit every channel. Equalisation lets the two ports tune each other's transmitters (and adapt their own receivers) for the actual channel, in four phases. Phase numbering is from the downstream port's point of view:

The four equalisation phases at 8 GT/s and above (conceptual summary)
PhaseWhen / at what rateWhat happensGoal
Phase 0Before the rate change, at the lower rateDownstream port tells the upstream port which transmitter preset (and receiver hint) to start with at the new rateA reasonable starting point
Phase 1At the new rateBoth ports exchange TS1 using the initial presets; they exchange information about their transmitter capabilities (the FS and LF values bounding coefficients)A link good enough to exchange training sets reliably (a modest bit error rate)
Phase 2At the new rateThe upstream port's receiver evaluates and requests changes to the downstream port's transmitter settings (presets or coefficients) until satisfiedTune the downstream→upstream direction
Phase 3At the new rateThe downstream port's receiver requests changes to the upstream port's transmitter settingsTune the upstream→downstream direction to the target bit error rate
  • Settings are expressed as presets (a defined set of de-emphasis/pre-shoot combinations, numbered P0 to P10 for NRZ rates) or as explicit coefficients (pre-cursor, cursor, post-cursor) of the transmitter's finite impulse response filter.
  • Equalisation is done separately for each rate (8, 16, 32 GT/s and the PAM4 rates), normally once after a fundamental reset, with results stored for later Recovery entries. Software can request re-equalisation (a Perform Equalization control) and a port can request it autonomously.
  • Phases 2 and 3 may be shortened or skipped when the receiver is already satisfied, or by revision-specific bypass mechanisms. Version-dependent
  • Receivers also adapt internally (CTLE, DFE) during these phases; that adaptation is implementation-dependent and invisible on the wire.

Preset tables, coefficient rules, FS/LF constraints, timeouts per phase, the 16 GT/s and 32 GT/s procedures and PAM4 equalisation are taught in Chapter 54 — Equalization and Module 09 — Link Training. Here, the concept is enough: equalisation is a negotiated, per-rate, per-direction tuning loop that happens inside Recovery.

Link recovery

"Recovery" is used in two senses:

  • The LTSSM Recovery state, which repairs lock and alignment in microseconds without software (Chapter 8).
  • Recovering a link that went down: when Recovery fails and the link returns to Detect, the hardware retrains from scratch. Whether software sees a brief "Link Down / Link Up" (hot-plug capable port), a Surprise Down error, a Downstream Port Containment event, or nothing at all depends on the port's capabilities and configuration. The NVMe driver then needs to reset and re-initialise the controller, because outstanding commands were lost.

Software can also deliberately retrain a link (Retrain Link in the downstream port's Link Control), for example after changing Target Link Speed. That is a Recovery entry on request; it is safe in principle but disruptive to traffic and must be used with care on live systems. Caution lab

The central concept on the ladder: training sets

Notes with the same boxes passed back and forth until both sides agree on group, seats and reading speed.

TS1 proposes, TS2 confirms. Fields: link number, lane number, N_FTS, supported rates, training control flags, equalisation information.

The LTSSM logic generates TS on every lane, counts consecutive matching TS received per lane, and programs the SerDes (rate, presets) based on their contents.

The Base Specification defines the TS formats per encoding, the counting rules, and the meaning of every field per LTSSM substate.

On the wire: 16-symbol ordered sets, identical in timing across lanes, lane-specific in the Lane Number field; COM-delimited at 8b/10b, ordered-set blocks at 128b/130b.

An analyzer shows per-lane TS with decoded fields; the first lane or field that disagrees, or the first missing response, marks the failure.

Compare what the downstream port proposed with what the upstream port echoed, lane by lane, and what rates each advertised: that isolates width, reversal and speed problems in minutes.

The sixteen questions for "link training"

1. What is it?
The process by which two PCIe ports detect each other, lock, number lanes, choose speed and equalise, using TS1/TS2 under LTSSM control.
2. Why was it created?
Serial links with variable width, speed and channels need an automatic, interoperable bring-up procedure.
3. What problem does it solve?
It turns unknown wiring and capabilities into a working, tuned link without software.
4. What existed before it?
Fixed-width parallel buses with no negotiation.
5. Why is the current design better?
It adapts to every combination of generation, width, lane order, polarity and channel loss, and degrades gracefully.
6. Who creates it?
Both ports' LTSSMs; the downstream port leads numbering and usually speed changes.
7. Who consumes it?
The partner LTSSM; ultimately the Data Link Layer (LinkUp) and software (Link Status).
8. Where does it live?
On every lane of the link, in ordered sets, during Polling, Configuration and Recovery.
9. What memory/registers are involved?
LnkCap/LnkCap2, LnkCtl/LnkCtl2, LnkSta/LnkSta2, Physical Layer 16/32 GT/s capabilities, Lane Margining capability; vendor equaliser registers.
10. What happens before it?
Power, REFCLK, PERST# de-assertion, receiver detection.
11. What happens after it?
L0; Data Link initialisation; enumeration and driver probe.
12. What happens if it is wrong?
No link, narrow link, slow link, or marginal link that flaps.
13. What does the protocol analyzer show?
TS1/TS2 per lane with fields, LTSSM state bands, equalisation requests and responses, timing.
14. What does Linux show?
LnkCap vs LnkSta, LnkSta2 equalisation flags, Physical Layer 16 GT/s status (if decoded), kernel "limited by" bandwidth messages, hot-plug link events.
15. What would a validation engineer test?
Thousands of bring-ups across resets, temperatures and voltages; each lane disabled in turn; reversed lanes; each rate; equalisation with each preset; interop with many partners; margining.
16. What would a senior engineer suspect when it fails?
Reset and clock sequencing, channel quality and retimers, firmware rate limits, equalisation incompatibility, then silicon errata.

Lane Margining at the Receiver (an extended capability introduced with Revision 4.0) lets software ask a receiver to step its sampling point in time and voltage and report errors, giving an in-system view of how much margin each lane has after equalisation. With a retimer in the path, training happens on two segments: the retimer forwards and adjusts training sets, and equalisation is performed separately on each side of the retimer. A failure can therefore be on the Root Port–retimer segment, the retimer–SSD segment, or in the retimer's handling of training.

Specification view

Link training is specified in the PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 — Physical Layer Logical Block (ordered sets, TS1/TS2 formats, LTSSM rules, equalisation procedure), with electrical aspects (detection, presets, coefficient ranges) in the — Electrical Sub-Block and register interfaces in the — PCI Express Capability Structure and the Physical Layer 16.0 GT/s, 32.0 GT/s and 64.0 GT/s and Lane Margining extended capability descriptions.

  • TS1 and TS2 are 16-symbol ordered sets carrying Link Number, Lane Number, N_FTS, Data Rate Identifier and Training Control, plus equalisation information at 8 GT/s and above.
  • At 8b/10b, TS1 identifier symbols are D10.2 (4Ah) and TS2 identifier symbols are D5.2 (45h); at 128b/130b the first symbol identifies TS1 (1Eh) or TS2 (2Dh).
  • The downstream port proposes link and lane numbers; speed changes and equalisation happen in Recovery; equalisation has four phases (0–3) with presets and coefficients.
  • Link Status 2 reports equalisation completion and phase success at 8 GT/s.

A port that reaches 16 GT/s must have completed (or been permitted to bypass) equalisation at 8 GT/s and 16 GT/s since the last fundamental reset; therefore "EqualizationComplete-" in LnkSta2 on a link reporting 16 GT/s deserves a second look (some implementations use bypass modes legitimately).

Initial preset choices, receiver adaptation algorithms, how long Phase 2/3 iterate, retry policies after failed speed changes, use of bypass modes, whether the Root Port retries a failed rate later, what vendor debug registers report.

8b/10b versus 128b/130b TS formats; equalisation at 8.0 (Revision 3.0), 16.0 (4.0) and 32.0 GT/s (5.0) with associated capabilities; equalisation bypass options (5.0); PAM4 equalisation and Flit Mode negotiation (6.0); 128 GT/s (7.0). Field additions in TS1/TS2 accompany most revisions.

  • The specification does not say which preset is best for a given board; it defines the mechanism.
  • It does not specify how a port reports why training failed; there is no standard "training failure reason" register.
  • Confirm in the specification: TS field bit positions; counts of consecutive TS required in each substate; equalisation phase timeouts; rules for Phase 2/3 skipping and bypass.

Never assume the faster rate was attempted just because both sides support it (check Target Link Speed); never assume equalisation found a good setting just because the link reached the rate (check errors and margin); never assume the device caused a training failure without capturing both directions.

Reasoning for the Gen1 link

  1. Everything at 2.5 GT/s worked on all four lanes: wiring and lane order are fine.
  2. The failure is all four lanes at once, at the first higher rate, in Phase 1 (before any tuning). A single bad lane would not look like this. Suspect something common: a channel component rated only for low speeds (riser, cable, adapter), an incorrectly configured retimer, an inappropriate initial preset in the Root Port's platform settings, or a reference clock problem that only bites at higher rates.
  3. Confirm direction: Phase 1 failed at the USP's receiver (it never locked), so the Root Port's transmit path into the SSD is the first suspect.
  4. Note why 5 GT/s was not tried: in this implementation the port jumps from 2.5 GT/s directly to the highest advertised rate's first step (8 GT/s, since 8 GT/s equalisation is a prerequisite for 16 GT/s) and reverts to the last good rate on failure.

LnkCtl2: Target Link Speed: 16GT/s, EnterCompliance- SpeedDis-
LnkSta2: Current De-emphasis Level: -6dB, EqualizationComplete+ EqualizationPhase1+

This excerpt is synthetic, written to show which lines matter; never treat it as a reference output. How to read it:

  • LnkCap vs LnkSta: capability 16GT/s x4, negotiated 16GT/s x4. In the Gen1 failure case you would see LnkSta: Speed 2.5GT/s (downgraded), Width x4 with newer pciutils.
  • LnkCtl2 Target Link Speed: if this were 8GT/s, the link would never try 16 GT/s: a configuration limit, not a failure. SpeedDis+ (hardware autonomous speed disable) also limits autonomous speed changes.
  • LnkSta2: the 8 GT/s equalisation flags. EqualizationPhase1- after a speed-change attempt matches the Gen1 trace. Equalisation at 16 GT/s and 32 GT/s is reported in their own capabilities.
  • DLActive- on an Endpoint is normal if the port does not implement Data Link Layer Link Active reporting (LLActRep- in LnkCap); on a Root Port with LLActRep+, DLActive- means the link is down.

What Linux cannot prove: why training ended where it did, which lane failed, which equalisation phase took long, or how many attempts were made. It can prove the end state, the configured limits (Target Link Speed), and the 8 GT/s equalisation outcome flags. When the link never reaches L0, the device has no configuration space to read at all; only the Root Port's registers remain, which is why the parent port is always the first place to look.

Code / pseudocode

This script reads lspci -vvv text (from a file, standard input, or a built-in synthetic sample) and produces a training verdict per device: capability versus negotiated state, target speed, and equalisation flags. It parses text only; it never touches hardware.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Summarise PCIe link training outcome from `lspci -vvv` text (read-only parsing)."""
import re, sys

SAMPLE = """\
00:01.1 PCI bridge: Example Root Port
\tLnkCap:\tPort #0, Speed 16GT/s, Width x4, ASPM L1, Exit Latency L1 <64us
\tLnkSta:\tSpeed 2.5GT/s (downgraded), Width x4
\tLnkCtl2: Target Link Speed: 16GT/s, EnterCompliance- SpeedDis-
\tLnkSta2: Current De-emphasis Level: -6dB, EqualizationComplete- EqualizationPhase1-
01:00.0 Non-Volatile memory controller: Example Lab SSD
\tLnkCap:\tPort #0, Speed 16GT/s, Width x4, ASPM L1, Exit Latency L1 <64us
\tLnkSta:\tSpeed 2.5GT/s (downgraded), Width x4
\tLnkCtl2: Target Link Speed: 16GT/s, EnterCompliance- SpeedDis-
\tLnkSta2: Current De-emphasis Level: -6dB, EqualizationComplete- EqualizationPhase1-
"""

def gts(s):
    m = re.search(r"Speed ([\d.]+)GT/s", s)
    return float(m.group(1)) if m else None

def width(s):
    m = re.search(r"Width x(\d+)", s)
    return int(m.group(1)) if m else None

def parse(text):
    devs, cur = {}, None
    for line in text.splitlines():
        m = re.match(r"^([0-9a-f]{2,4}:)?[0-9a-f]{2}:[0-9a-f]{2}\.[0-7]\s", line)
        if m:
            cur = line.split()[0]
            devs[cur] = {"name": line[len(cur):].strip()}
            continue
        if cur is None:
            continue
        for key in ("LnkCap:", "LnkSta:", "LnkCtl2:", "LnkSta2:"):
            if line.strip().startswith(key):
                devs[cur][key[:-1]] = line.strip()[len(key):].strip()
    return devs

def verdict(d):
    cap, sta = d.get("LnkCap", ""), d.get("LnkSta", "")
    out = []
    cs, cw, ns, nw = gts(cap), width(cap), gts(sta), width(sta)
    if None in (cs, cw, ns, nw):
        return ["link fields not found (no PCIe capability, or not run as root)"]
    out.append(f"capable {cs} GT/s x{cw}, negotiated {ns} GT/s x{nw}")
    tgt = re.search(r"Target Link Speed: ([\d.]+)GT/s", d.get("LnkCtl2", ""))
    if tgt and float(tgt.group(1)) < cs:
        out.append(f"Target Link Speed limits the link to {tgt.group(1)} GT/s (configuration, not failure)")
    if ns < cs:
        out.append("speed below this port's capability: check the partner's LnkCap, then Target Link Speed, then equalisation")
    if nw < cw:
        out.append("width below this port's capability: check the partner's LnkCap, then lanes above x%d" % nw)
    eq = d.get("LnkSta2", "")
    if "EqualizationPhase1-" in eq and cs >= 8:
        out.append("8 GT/s equalisation Phase 1 not successful: a speed change above 5 GT/s failed or was never attempted")
    return out

if __name__ == "__main__":
    text = SAMPLE
    if len(sys.argv) > 1:
        text = sys.stdin.read() if sys.argv[1] == "-" else open(sys.argv[1]).read()
    for bdf, d in parse(text).items():
        print(f"{bdf} {d['name']}")
        for v in verdict(d):
            print("   ", v)

Use it on your own system with sudo lspci -vvv | python3 link_verdict.py -. The built-in sample encodes the Gen1 failure trace from this chapter.

Worked example

Scenario. After a platform firmware update, all Gen4 SSDs in a server model train at Gen3. Nothing else changed.

  1. Observe: LnkCap on both ends says 16GT/s; LnkSta says 8GT/s (downgraded); LnkCtl2 on the Root Port says Target Link Speed: 8GT/s.
  2. Interpret: the firmware update set the Root Ports' Target Link Speed to 8 GT/s (perhaps a new default for a platform validation issue). No training failure occurred: the link correctly trained to the highest permitted rate.
  3. Distinguish from failure: had Target Link Speed been 16GT/s with LnkSta at 8GT/s, the next check would be the 16 GT/s equalisation status in the Physical Layer 16.0 GT/s capability, and an analyzer capture of the attempt.
  4. Action: raise with the platform vendor; ask why the default changed. Do not change Target Link Speed on production machines to "fix" it: the vendor may have limited the speed because of a known signal-integrity issue on some boards.

Second scenario. One server in a fleet has an SSD at 16GT/s x4 whose kernel log shows correctable Receiver Errors every few minutes, while identical servers show none. LnkSta2 is healthy (all phases successful). What next? Training "succeeded", but with less margin. Compare lane margining results with a healthy server, re-seat or swap the drive and riser, and check whether the errors follow the drive or the slot. Equalisation is per link, so a drive moved to another slot re-equalises with a different channel; a fault that follows the slot is a channel problem.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a configuration limit (Target Link Speed, width strap) a training failure, or the reverse.
  • Reading only the device's registers when the device is missing: if training never reached L0, the device has no readable configuration space; read the Root Port.
  • Assuming TS1 and TS2 are different message types with different fields. They share a layout; their role differs.
  • Thinking equalisation happens once for all rates. It is per rate (8, 16, 32 GT/s, and PAM4 rates).
  • Thinking equalisation tunes only one's own transmitter. Phases 2 and 3 tune the partner's transmitter at the request of one's own receiver.
  • Changing Target Link Speed and setting Retrain Link on a production system to "test" a theory.
  • Analysing a training capture from one direction only.

Debugging connection

A disciplined order of questions for any training complaint:

  1. Did the link reach L0 at all? Root Port Link Status (DLActive if supported; LnkSta values; a device appearing in lspci).
  2. If not: which state is it stuck in? Detect loop (no receivers), Polling loop (no lock), Configuration loop (no agreement), Polling.Compliance (partner not training). Analyzer or vendor LTSSM register.
  3. If yes, at what width? Compare with both LnkCaps. Narrower: find which lanes failed and in which substate.
  4. At what speed? Compare with both LnkCaps and Target Link Speed. Slower: check equalisation flags per rate; capture the speed-change attempt.
  5. Is it stable? Correctable error rates, Recovery frequency, BWMgmt.

Always capture both directions and, if a retimer is present, both segments. A training failure is a conversation that broke; you need both sides of it to see who stopped answering first.

Senior engineer insight

Seniors separate three questions that juniors blur: Is the link allowed to go faster? (configuration), Did it try? (training), Did it succeed with margin? (equalisation and errors). Each has different evidence and different owners.

They also know that "the SSD is not detected" is, statistically, more often a platform issue (reset sequencing, clocking, firmware) than a drive defect, and they prove it quickly by moving the drive to a known-good slot and putting a known-good drive in the suspect slot. Training traces then explain the mechanism.

Finally, they are wary of equalisation results that are "good enough to train". A link that trained at 16 GT/s with minimal margin will pass every boot test and fail in the field when temperature or aging shifts it. Margining data, not LnkSta, is the evidence for shipping.

Expected output

For each capture: the LTSSM substate where training stops, the first abnormal event, two or three ranked hypotheses, and one discriminating experiment.

Background

Detect failures mean no termination seen; Polling failures mean no reliable lock at 2.5 GT/s; Configuration failures mean the ports cannot agree on numbering. When the device is absent, the Root Port's Link Status and Slot Status, the analyzer and the platform's reset/clock design are the evidence.

Step 3 — Hypothesise and design an experiment

Challenge

Write ltssm_check.py-style logs for captures A–C and extend the checker to classify them automatically as "Detect loop", "Polling loop", or "Configuration loop".

Hint

Presence detect is a separate mechanism (pins or in-band presence) from receiver detection (termination on the lanes). Clock tolerance problems show up as errors on all lanes at once. Lane reversal shows up as mirrored lane numbers.

Solution

Capture A — Detect loop. First abnormal event: row 2, no receivers on any lane although presence is detected. The drive is physically present (presence pins) but its receivers are not terminated: most likely the drive is not powered (a rail missing in the bay), PERST# is still asserted to the drive (reset sequencing; the drive keeps its PHY off), or REFCLK is absent so the PHY never enables termination. Experiment: measure the drive's supply rail and PERST# at the connector during boot; if both are fine, check REFCLK. Swap in a known-good drive to separate drive from slot.

Capture B — Polling loop. First abnormal event: row 2, symbol/disparity errors on all lanes from the USP. Errors on every lane at the lowest rate point away from per-lane channel damage and towards clocking: the host uses SSC on its reference while the bay uses an independent clock, so the ports are in a separate-clock configuration with independent SSC that the drive (or the platform setting) does not support. Hypotheses: clocking architecture mismatch (most likely), a faulty clock buffer in the bay, a drive PHY fault. Experiment: disable SSC on the host clock (in a lab) or feed the bay the common host clock; if training succeeds, the clocking architecture is confirmed.

Capture C — Configuration loop. First abnormal event: row 5, lane numbers returned in reverse order. The new adapter revision routes lanes reversed; the USP reports the reversal but neither side remaps (the drive does not support reversal, and this Root Port does not try the reversed assignment). Hypotheses: adapter routing change (most likely, given the revision difference), Root Port lane-reversal setting disabled by firmware. Experiment: put the drive on the previous adapter revision (trains x4, already known) and check the adapter netlist; check whether the platform exposes a lane-reversal option for that port.

LnkSta at the Root Port: in all three cases, the link is down, so LnkSta shows no meaningful negotiated width (often reported as x0 or the last value, implementation-dependent) and DLActive-. The Root Port is the only place Linux can see anything; the device has no configuration space.

What a senior engineer notices

  • Capture A's 12 ms period matches Detect.Quiet's commonly cited timeout: the Root Port is behaving correctly.
  • Capture B's "one in five" error rate with periodic wander is the fingerprint of spread-spectrum modulation, not random noise.
  • Capture C is a regression tied to a hardware revision: the bug report should go to the adapter team with the trace attached, not to the SSD vendor.

Failure variants

  • Polling.Compliance entered instead of a Polling loop: the partner terminates but does not send TS (firmware not running).
  • Link reaches L0 at 2.5 GT/s but the device still does not appear: an enumeration or configuration-response problem (device returns Configuration Request Retry Status for too long, or firmware never becomes ready); see Chapter 17.
  • Link reaches L0, then drops within seconds: the flapping pattern of Chapter 8.

Extension

Design a production test that would have caught Capture C before shipping: which adapter/drive/Root Port combinations, how many power cycles, and which pass criteria (width, speed, error counts) you would enforce.

Answers

1: Link Number (Configuration), Lane Number (Configuration, reversal detection), N_FTS (L0s exit), Data Rate Identifier (speed negotiation, all phases), Training Control (Hot Reset/Disable/Loopback/scrambling/compliance), equalisation fields (Recovery.Equalization and EQ TS2 before rate change), identifier (TS1 vs TS2). 2: Numbers are assigned only in Configuration; in Polling the ports are only establishing lock, and PAD signals "not yet assigned". 3: L0 → Recovery.RcvrLock (TS1, speed change) → Recovery.RcvrCfg (TS2/EQ TS2 with presets) → Recovery.Speed (EIOS, idle, rate switch) → Recovery.RcvrLock at 8 GT/s → Recovery.Equalization (Phases 1–3) → Recovery.RcvrCfg → Recovery.Idle → L0. 4: The downstream port's transmitter is adjusted, at the request of the upstream port's receiver. 5: The 16 GT/s equalisation status in the Physical Layer 16.0 GT/s capability (if decoded), the partner's LnkCap (does the SSD support 16?), error/Recovery history, and then an analyzer capture of the attempted change to 16 GT/s. 6: In the stepwise procedure defined for these rates, 8 GT/s equalisation establishes a working 128b/130b link over which the 16 GT/s equalisation information is exchanged; later revisions define bypass options that change this. Quiz

  1. Which port proposes the link number?

    • The downstream port
    • The upstream port
    • Whichever finishes Polling first
    • Software
    Answer and reasoning

    The downstream port proposes; the upstream port echoes on lanes it will use.

  2. What does a TS2 generally indicate compared with a TS1?

    • A higher data rate
    • An error
    • Agreement/confirmation, allowing progress to the next step
    • A request for Hot Reset
    Answer and reasoning

    TS1 carries proposals; TS2 confirms.

  3. Where is the speed-change request carried?

    • In a DLLP
    • In the Data Rate Identifier of TS1/TS2
    • In a configuration write to the device
    • In the SKP ordered set
    Answer and reasoning

    The Data Rate Identifier symbol carries supported rates and the speed-change flag.

  4. In which equalisation phase does the downstream port tell the upstream port which preset to start with at the new rate?

    • Phase 0
    • Phase 1
    • Phase 2
    • Phase 3
    Answer and reasoning

    Phase 0, using EQ TS2 at the lower rate.

  5. Receiver detection tells a transmitter:

    • The partner's maximum speed
    • The partner's lane numbers
    • The channel loss
    • Whether a terminated receiver is present on each lane
    Answer and reasoning

    It senses termination, nothing more.

  6. A link fails to change speed. What does it normally do?

    • Goes to Detect and stays there
    • Reverts to the previous rate through Recovery.Speed and returns to L0
    • Enters Loopback
    • Reports a fatal error
    Answer and reasoning

    It falls back to the last working rate; software sees only a slower link.

  7. The SSD is absent from lspci. Which device's registers do you read, and why?

    Model answer

    The Root Port (or Switch downstream port) above it: the SSD has no accessible configuration space without a working link; the port's Link Status, Slot Status and AER show whether the link trained.

  8. Why are TS-level traces needed from both directions?

    Model answer

    Training is a conversation: progress depends on what each side receives. One direction shows only what one port proposed; the failure is often that the other port did not receive, did not agree or did not answer, visible only in the other direction.

  9. Explain why a correctly trained 16 GT/s link can still be unhealthy.

    Model answer

    Equalisation may have converged to a setting with little margin; the link passes training but accumulates errors and Recovery events under temperature, noise or aging. Training success proves a working state, not a robust one.

Interview questions

  1. (Beginner) What are TS1 and TS2?

    Model answer

    16-symbol training ordered sets sent on every lane during Polling, Configuration and Recovery, carrying link and lane numbers, N_FTS, supported rates, training controls and equalisation information; TS1 proposes, TS2 confirms.

  2. (Beginner) Why does every link start at 2.5 GT/s?

    Model answer

    So that any two PCIe ports of any generation can establish communication and exchange capabilities before moving to a higher common rate.

  3. (Intermediate) Walk through lane number negotiation with lane reversal.

    Model answer

    The DSP proposes lane numbers 0..N−1 on its physical lanes. The USP receives them in reverse order on its physical lanes. If the USP supports reversal, it remaps internally and returns numbers consistent with the DSP's assignment; otherwise it returns what it sees, and a DSP that supports reversal may reassign in reverse. If neither does, Configuration times out.

  4. (Intermediate) What is equalisation and why does it exist from 8 GT/s?

    Model answer

    A negotiated tuning of transmitter FIR settings (presets/coefficients) guided by the partner's receiver, plus receiver adaptation, to compensate channel loss and reflections. At 8 GT/s and above, fixed de-emphasis cannot cover the range of real channels while meeting the target bit error rate.

  5. (Senior) A link trains at Gen4 on cold boot but only Gen3 after warm reboots. Hypotheses?

    Model answer

    Equalisation results stored from a previous training being reused inappropriately after a warm reset (a reset type that does not clear them), a retimer that is not reset on warm reboot and retains state, temperature-dependent margin (warmer after running), or platform firmware applying different speed policies on warm boot. Capture both cases, compare equalisation phases and presets, and check which components are reset by each reset type.

  6. (Senior) How do you prove to a platform vendor that a training failure is not the SSD's fault?

    Model answer

    Show the failure follows the slot, not the drive (swap with known-good drives and slots); provide analyzer captures of both directions identifying the first abnormal event and which receiver failed; show the drive trains correctly with other platforms or reference hosts; show platform-side measurements (PERST#, REFCLK, rails) if relevant; and present margining data comparing slots.

Model explanation (child)

Two people want to work together but have never met. First they check who is sitting in which seat. Then they keep passing "hello" notes until they can each read the other's writing. The teacher says "we're group 0, you're seat 0, you're seat 1…", and the leader writes the same back. Then they agree to read faster, and spend a moment adjusting their glasses until the faster writing is clear again. If it is still blurry, they go back to the slower speed, which still works.

Chapter summary

  • Training = receiver detection, lock (Polling), numbering (Configuration), speed change and equalisation (Recovery).
  • TS1/TS2 carry Link Number, Lane Number, N_FTS, Data Rate Identifier, Training Control and equalisation fields; TS1 proposes, TS2 confirms.
  • The downstream port leads numbering and usually speed changes; failed speed changes fall back silently.
  • Equalisation runs per rate in four phases: presets announced (0), basic link (1), DSP transmitter tuned by USP receiver (2), USP transmitter tuned by DSP receiver (3).
  • Linux sees the outcome (LnkSta, LnkCtl2 limits, LnkSta2 flags), not the process; when the device is missing, read the Root Port.

Do not move on until you can:

  • name the TS1/TS2 fields and the training step that uses each;
  • walk through link and lane numbering, including reversal and degraded width;
  • explain the speed-change handshake through Recovery and its fallback;
  • explain equalisation Phases 0–3 conceptually;
  • distinguish a configuration limit from a training failure using LnkCtl2 and LnkSta2;
  • diagnose Detect, Polling and Configuration loops from a capture.
