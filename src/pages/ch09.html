Link training is the conversation two PCIe ports hold, in training sequences called TS1 and TS2, before they can carry a single byte of NVMe traffic. This chapter reads that conversation field by field: how receivers are detected, how lanes and links are numbered, how speed is raised, how equalisation tunes the channel at 8 GT/s and above, and how to diagnose a link that will not reach L0.

Why this chapter exists

Chapter 8 named the states. This chapter explains what is said in them. When an SSD is missing, trains narrow, or stays at a low speed, the evidence is in the training sets: which lanes sent what, which fields disagreed, which phase of equalisation failed. A protocol analyzer will show you thousands of TS1 and TS2 ordered sets; you need to know which few fields matter and in what order to read them.

It is also where many "it's the SSD's fault" escalations get resolved. Training failures are frequently caused by the platform (reset sequencing, clocking, channel, retimers, firmware speed limits), and the training sets show which side stopped cooperating first.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the structure and purpose of TS1 and TS2 ordered sets and name their main fields.
  • Explain receiver detection in electrical terms and its failure modes.
  • Walk through lane and link number negotiation, including degraded width and lane reversal.
  • Explain speed negotiation: rate advertisement, the speed-change handshake through Recovery, and fallback.
  • Explain the purpose and phases of equalisation at a conceptual level (details of presets and coefficients are in Chapter 54).
  • Read synthetic training traces: a normal Gen4 x4 bring-up, a link that settles at Gen1 because equalisation fails, and a link that settles at x2.
  • Diagnose a link that refuses to reach L0, using captures and read-only Linux evidence (lspci -vvv LnkCap/LnkSta).

The story

A new class is choosing seats for a group project. The teacher (the downstream port) and the group leader (the upstream port) pass notes back and forth. Every note has the same boxes: "Group number", "Seat number", "How fast can you read?", and some tick-boxes such as "Start over" or "Stop".

First, the teacher checks which seats have someone sitting in them. Then everyone passes "hello" notes with empty boxes, until all notes are being read clearly. The teacher writes "Group 0" on every note; the group leader writes "Group 0" back on the seats that will work together. The teacher numbers the seats 0, 1, 2, 3; the leader copies the numbers back. Then they both tick "I can read faster", move to faster reading, and spend a few minutes adjusting their glasses (equalisation) until everything is sharp again.

Two professional musicians meeting for a session do something similar. They check both are plugged in (detect), play a reference note until both instruments are in tune with each other (polling and lock), agree who plays which part (configuration), then speed up the tempo and adjust their monitor mixes until each can hear the other clearly at the faster tempo (speed change and equalisation). If it does not work at the faster tempo, they go back to the slower one rather than stopping the session.

  • In the story, notes are passed one at a time. In PCIe, each lane carries its own stream of identical-format training sets simultaneously, and decisions depend on counting consecutive identical sets received on each lane.
  • Musicians adjust their own monitors. In PCIe equalisation, each receiver mostly asks the other side's transmitter to change its settings (and adapts its own receiver internally).
  • "Going back to the slower tempo" in PCIe is a defined state transition with timeouts, not a friendly suggestion; and it can happen silently, leaving a link slower than anyone expects.

Beginner explanation

Link training happens automatically every time a link comes up. It has five jobs:

  1. Receiver detection: find out which lanes have a partner.
  2. Lock: each receiver learns to read the incoming bit stream (bit lock, then symbol or block lock) using training sequences.
  3. Lane and link numbering: decide which lanes form the link and number them 0, 1, 2, …
  4. Speed: start at 2.5 GT/s, then switch to the fastest rate both support.
  5. Equalisation: at 8 GT/s and above, tune the transmitters and receivers so the faster signals arrive cleanly.

The messages used are called TS1 and TS2 (Training Sequence 1 and 2). They look alike: both carry a link number, a lane number, the supported speeds and some control flags. Roughly speaking, TS1 means "here is my proposal" and TS2 means "I agree, let's move on".

If any step fails, the link tries again, or settles for less (fewer lanes, lower speed), or gives up and goes back to Detect. Linux shows the end result in lspci -vvv as LnkSta.

Engineering explanation

Receiver detection

In Detect.Active, each transmitter lane performs receiver detection: it changes its common-mode voltage and watches how fast the line voltage follows. Behind the AC coupling capacitor, a partner receiver presents a termination to ground (during detection a defined DC impedance); charging the coupling capacitor through that termination takes noticeably longer than charging only the small parasitic capacitance of an open line. A slow change means "receiver present"; a fast change means "nothing there".

  • A powered-off or held-in-reset partner may not present termination: not detected.
  • A missing or open AC coupling capacitor: not detected on that lane.
  • Some test equipment presents termination but never sends training sets: detected, then Polling.Compliance.

The precise voltage step, timing and termination ranges are electrical specifications. Uncertain Confirm values in the Electrical Sub-Block for your revision.

TS1 and TS2 ordered sets

TS1 and TS2 are 16-symbol ordered sets transmitted on every lane simultaneously, each lane carrying its own lane-specific values. In 8b/10b mode (2.5 and 5.0 GT/s) they start with a COM control symbol; in 128b/130b mode (8.0–32.0 GT/s) they are sent in ordered-set blocks (sync header 01b) and start with an identifier byte. Flit-Mode-capable ports use additional TS formats and fields at 64 GT/s and above. Version-dependent

Progress is decided by counting: a port moves on when it has received a required number of consecutive TS1 or TS2 with matching fields on the relevant lanes and has sent a required number in return. Inconsistent fields reset the count. This is why a single noisy lane can delay training even when the others are perfect.

Lane negotiation

Lane negotiation happens in Configuration, after all detected lanes have been through Polling.

  • Link number: a downstream port that can split into several links (bifurcation) gives each a different link number. An upstream port has one link and echoes the number it receives on the lanes it wants to use; on lanes it will not use it returns PAD.
  • Lane numbers: the downstream port assigns 0…N−1. The upstream port either returns the same numbers or, if it sees them in reverse order and supports reversal, handles the reversal; otherwise the downstream port may try the reverse assignment. If no agreement is reached in time, Configuration times out.
  • Width selection: the resulting width is the largest valid width covering contiguous lanes from logical lane 0 that both ports accept. A port may also be configured (by hardware strapping or firmware) to a maximum width smaller than its physical lanes.
  • Deskew: during Config.Complete and Config.Idle, receivers align lanes using the simultaneous arrival of ordered sets.

A link that trains at x2 when x4 was expected tells you something specific: lanes 2 and 3 (or one of them) failed to participate. It never means "the link is at half speed for an unknown reason". The trace shows exactly which lanes returned PAD in Config.Linkwidth.Accept (see the x2 trace in Chapter 5).

Speed negotiation

Every TS1 and TS2 carries the sender's supported data rates. After the first arrival in L0 at 2.5 GT/s, either port may initiate a speed change, but normally the downstream port does, either autonomously (hardware wants the highest common rate) or because software wrote a Target Link Speed in Link Control 2 and set Retrain Link.

  1. The initiating port enters Recovery.RcvrLock and sends TS1 with the speed-change flag set; the partner follows into Recovery when it sees TS1.
  2. In Recovery.RcvrCfg, both send TS2 with the speed-change flag. For a change to 8 GT/s (or higher, depending on revision), the downstream port sends equalisation TS2 sets carrying the transmitter presets the upstream port should use at the new rate (equalisation Phase 0).
  3. When enough TS2 with speed change have been exchanged, both enter Recovery.Speed: send EIOS, go electrically idle, switch rate (PLL relock, encoding change if crossing 5.0 → 8.0 GT/s).
  4. At the new rate, back to Recovery.RcvrLock; for 8 GT/s and above, Recovery.Equalization runs the remaining phases (unless already done for this rate since the last fundamental reset, or bypass modes apply).
  5. Recovery.RcvrCfg and Recovery.Idle, then L0 at the new rate.
  6. If lock cannot be achieved at the new rate within the timeout, the ports return to Recovery.Speed and revert to the previous rate. The link survives at the lower rate. Whether and when a port retries the higher rate is implementation-dependent.

The new rate is the highest rate advertised by both ports, limited by Target Link Speed. Ports may step through intermediate rates or, in later revisions, use mechanisms that reach the highest rate with fewer steps. Version-dependent

Registers involved: Link Capabilities 2 (Supported Link Speeds Vector), Link Control 2 (Target Link Speed, Hardware Autonomous Speed Disable, Enter Compliance), Link Control (Retrain Link), Link Status (Current Link Speed, Link Training), Link Status 2 (equalisation status at 8 GT/s), and the Physical Layer 16.0 GT/s and 32.0 GT/s extended capabilities (equalisation status and settings at those rates). Equalisation (conceptual)

At 8 GT/s and above, a fixed transmitter setting cannot suit every channel. Equalisation lets the two ports tune each other's transmitters (and adapt their own receivers) for the actual channel, in four phases. Phase numbering is from the downstream port's point of view:

The four equalisation phases at 8 GT/s and above (conceptual summary)
PhaseWhen / at what rateWhat happensGoal
Phase 0Before the rate change, at the lower rateDownstream port tells the upstream port which transmitter preset (and receiver hint) to start with at the new rateA reasonable starting point
Phase 1At the new rateBoth ports exchange TS1 using the initial presets; they exchange information about their transmitter capabilities (the FS and LF values bounding coefficients)A link good enough to exchange training sets reliably (a modest bit error rate)
Phase 2At the new rateThe upstream port's receiver evaluates and requests changes to the downstream port's transmitter settings (presets or coefficients) until satisfiedTune the downstream→upstream direction
Phase 3At the new rateThe downstream port's receiver requests changes to the upstream port's transmitter settingsTune the upstream→downstream direction to the target bit error rate
  • Settings are expressed as presets (a defined set of de-emphasis/pre-shoot combinations, numbered P0 to P10 for NRZ rates) or as explicit coefficients (pre-cursor, cursor, post-cursor) of the transmitter's finite impulse response filter.
  • Equalisation is done separately for each rate (8, 16, 32 GT/s and the PAM4 rates), normally once after a fundamental reset, with results stored for later Recovery entries. Software can request re-equalisation (a Perform Equalization control) and a port can request it autonomously.
  • Phases 2 and 3 may be shortened or skipped when the receiver is already satisfied, or by revision-specific bypass mechanisms. Version-dependent
  • Receivers also adapt internally (CTLE, DFE) during these phases; that adaptation is implementation-dependent and invisible on the wire.

Preset tables, coefficient rules, FS/LF constraints, timeouts per phase, the 16 GT/s and 32 GT/s procedures and PAM4 equalisation are taught in Chapter 54 — Equalization and Module 09 — Link Training. Here, the concept is enough: equalisation is a negotiated, per-rate, per-direction tuning loop that happens inside Recovery.

Link recovery

"Recovery" is used in two senses:

  • The LTSSM Recovery state, which repairs lock and alignment in microseconds without software (Chapter 8).
  • Recovering a link that went down: when Recovery fails and the link returns to Detect, the hardware retrains from scratch. Whether software sees a brief "Link Down / Link Up" (hot-plug capable port), a Surprise Down error, a Downstream Port Containment event, or nothing at all depends on the port's capabilities and configuration. The NVMe driver then needs to reset and re-initialise the controller, because outstanding commands were lost.

Software can also deliberately retrain a link (Retrain Link in the downstream port's Link Control), for example after changing Target Link Speed. That is a Recovery entry on request; it is safe in principle but disruptive to traffic and must be used with care on live systems. Caution lab

The central concept on the ladder: training sets

Notes with the same boxes passed back and forth until both sides agree on group, seats and reading speed.

TS1 proposes, TS2 confirms. Fields: link number, lane number, N_FTS, supported rates, training control flags, equalisation information.

The LTSSM logic generates TS on every lane, counts consecutive matching TS received per lane, and programs the SerDes (rate, presets) based on their contents.

The Base Specification defines the TS formats per encoding, the counting rules, and the meaning of every field per LTSSM substate.

On the wire: 16-symbol ordered sets, identical in timing across lanes, lane-specific in the Lane Number field; COM-delimited at 8b/10b, ordered-set blocks at 128b/130b.

An analyzer shows per-lane TS with decoded fields; the first lane or field that disagrees, or the first missing response, marks the failure.

Compare what the downstream port proposed with what the upstream port echoed, lane by lane, and what rates each advertised: that isolates width, reversal and speed problems in minutes.

The sixteen questions for "link training"

1. What is it?
The process by which two PCIe ports detect each other, lock, number lanes, choose speed and equalise, using TS1/TS2 under LTSSM control.
2. Why was it created?
Serial links with variable width, speed and channels need an automatic, interoperable bring-up procedure.
3. What problem does it solve?
It turns unknown wiring and capabilities into a working, tuned link without software.
4. What existed before it?
Fixed-width parallel buses with no negotiation.
5. Why is the current design better?
It adapts to every combination of generation, width, lane order, polarity and channel loss, and degrades gracefully.
6. Who creates it?
Both ports' LTSSMs; the downstream port leads numbering and usually speed changes.
7. Who consumes it?
The partner LTSSM; ultimately the Data Link Layer (LinkUp) and software (Link Status).
8. Where does it live?
On every lane of the link, in ordered sets, during Polling, Configuration and Recovery.
9. What memory/registers are involved?
LnkCap/LnkCap2, LnkCtl/LnkCtl2, LnkSta/LnkSta2, Physical Layer 16/32 GT/s capabilities, Lane Margining capability; vendor equaliser registers.
10. What happens before it?
Power, REFCLK, PERST# de-assertion, receiver detection.
11. What happens after it?
L0; Data Link initialisation; enumeration and driver probe.
12. What happens if it is wrong?
No link, narrow link, slow link, or marginal link that flaps.
13. What does the protocol analyzer show?
TS1/TS2 per lane with fields, LTSSM state bands, equalisation requests and responses, timing.
14. What does Linux show?
LnkCap vs LnkSta, LnkSta2 equalisation flags, Physical Layer 16 GT/s status (if decoded), kernel "limited by" bandwidth messages, hot-plug link events.
15. What would a validation engineer test?
Thousands of bring-ups across resets, temperatures and voltages; each lane disabled in turn; reversed lanes; each rate; equalisation with each preset; interop with many partners; margining.
16. What would a senior engineer suspect when it fails?
Reset and clock sequencing, channel quality and retimers, firmware rate limits, equalisation incompatibility, then silicon errata.
