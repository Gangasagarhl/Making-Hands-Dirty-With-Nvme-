Two schedules for the same course. The 12-month plan takes a working engineer from zero to senior-level reasoning at about eight to ten hours a week. The 30-day intensive covers the core of the course at four to six hours a day. Every week and every day is mapped to specific pages, labs and checkpoints.

How to use these plans

A plan does not make you learn, but it does stop you from drifting: re-reading favorite chapters, skipping labs, and putting off the parts that are hard. Both plans here follow three rules:

  1. The order follows the dependency map. Nothing is scheduled before the stage it depends on (see the Learning Map).
  2. Every week ends in evidence. A checkpoint, a lab artifact or a teach-back, never "read chapters X–Y" alone.
  3. Review is scheduled, not hoped for. Part checkpoints and major assessments from Testing, Spaced Review and Teach-Back are placed a week or more after the Part is finished, so they work as spaced retrieval.

Learning a big subject is like climbing a mountain. A good guide doesn't just point at the top. They plan each day's walk, choose where to camp, and check that everyone can still breathe before going higher.

A marathon training plan builds distance week by week, puts long runs on fixed days, schedules rest, and tests pace in a tune-up race before the real one. Skipping the easy runs is how people get injured in week 12.

A marathon plan trains one skill toward one fixed race day. Here the "race" is open-ended: real debugging problems arrive whenever they arrive. Also, missing a week of running costs fitness evenly, but missing a stage here costs unevenly. Skipping PRPs hurts far more later than skipping KV command sets. When you fall behind, cut from the advanced features, never from the spine.

What you need

  • Always: a computer with Python 3 for the simulator labs, a notebook (paper or digital) for diagrams, and the official specifications (free from NVM Express; the PCI Express Base Specification is a PCI-SIG members' document — use the course chapters and public material if you do not have access).
  • For Observe steps: a Linux machine with an NVMe SSD and the pciutils and nvme-cli packages. Read-only commands only, unless a lab says otherwise.
  • Optional: a spare NVMe SSD with no valuable data (Caution labs), a QEMU virtual machine with an emulated NVMe controller, and access to a protocol analyzer (Months 7–12 benefit most). See Lab Environment.

Every lab in these plans is Safe lab (simulation or read-only inspection) unless marked Caution lab. Caution labs write to a test device and can destroy its data. Never run them on a disk that holds an operating system or data you need.

The 30-day intensive plan

Budget: four to six hours a day. Every day has six parts: reading (pages), theory (the one idea to own by evening), analogy (from the analogy library, with its boundary), lab, quiz (the page's quiz or the named checkpoint), and a trace exercise. Days 7, 14, 21 and 30 are consolidation days with no new reading.

Thirty days covers the spine through error handling and a first look at performance and advanced features. It does not cover the deep-dive modules, analyzer workflows in depth or the senior material. After the intensive, continue with Months 7–12 of the yearly plan at a slower pace.

A suggested day: 90 minutes reading, 30 minutes theory notes and analogy, 90 minutes lab, 20 minutes quiz, 40 minutes trace exercise, 10 minutes zeros list and the next day's preview.

Adapting the plans

When you fall behind

Fall-behind is normal. Handle it by priority, not by cramming:

  1. Never cut the spine chapters (stages 1–14 of the learning map), the checkpoints, or the teach-backs.
  2. Cut deep-dive modules first; they can be read later.
  3. Cut advanced feature chapters you will not use in your job next.
  4. If you are more than two weeks behind on the yearly plan, insert a catch-up week with no new reading: only the checkpoints and the zeros list.

Faster or slower

  • Faster: experienced engineers can merge Months 1–2 into one and skip the beginner sections of known topics, but should still take every checkpoint. If you score 2 on all ten questions, move on; if not, you are not as far along as you thought.
  • Slower: stretch any month into six weeks by giving each module its own week. Keep the checkpoint dates relative to the end of each Part.

Quiz

  1. You are three weeks behind in Month 6. What do you cut first?

    • The Part XIII checkpoint
    • Chapter 41 (correlation)
    • Deep-dive modules dd36 and dd37
    • The teach-back
    Answer and reasoning

    Modules can be read later; spine chapters, checkpoints and teach-backs cannot be skipped without creating gaps later.

  2. Why is the Part X checkpoint scheduled in the week after Chapter 28 rather than the same day?

    Model answer

    So that it acts as spaced retrieval: some forgetting has begun and retrieval strengthens memory more. Same-day checking measures short-term recall.

  3. Which six parts make up every day of the 30-day plan?

    Answer

    Reading, theory, analogy, lab, quiz, trace exercise.

  4. An engineer with five years of PCIe validation experience starts the 12-month plan. Where should they start, and what must they still do?

    Model answer

    Take the placement test; likely start at Month 4. They must still take checkpoints IV–VIII to confirm, and pay attention to DMA and IOMMU from the host's point of view, which PCIe validation work may not have covered.

  5. Why does the 30-day plan put evidence-debugging on Day 26 rather than Day 5?

    • It is optional
    • Evidence discipline needs traces and errors to apply to; before Phase 6 there is nothing to reason about
    • It is a senior topic only
    • It depends on fabrics
    Answer and reasoning

    The method is about reasoning from traces and logs; without knowing what normal looks like, a learner cannot find a first abnormal event.

  6. Which months of the yearly plan benefit most from analyzer access?

    Answer

    Months 7–12 (trace analysis onward).

  7. What is the maximum score of a Part checkpoint, and why track zeros rather than the score?

    Model answer

    20 (10 questions × 2). Zeros show exactly what to study; the score hides which items were missed.

  8. Your Day 13 lab is the CQ phase simulator. What exactly should you be able to explain afterwards?

    Model answer

    Why a host that does not flip its expected phase on wrap stops seeing completions, why the controller then sees the queue as full, and why that looks like a hung device to the application.

Mastery gate and next steps
