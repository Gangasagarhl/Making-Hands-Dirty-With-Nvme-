This module treats link training as one end-to-end negotiation — from the first TS1 at 2.5 GT/s to L0 at the highest common rate — and studies how its decisions are made, how software steers and re-runs it, how retimers and bifurcated ports change it, and how it fails: slowly, partially (narrower or slower than expected) or not at all. Equalization appears here as one stage of the negotiation; its electrical detail is in Chapter 54.

Orientation

Chapter 9 introduced receiver detection, TS1/TS2, lane and speed negotiation and the four equalization phases conceptually; Chapter 54 went deep into presets, transmitter coefficients, receiver equalization and Lane Margining at the Receiver. This module sits between them and above them: it explains the decision logic of training (which width, which rate, which fallback), the controls software has over it (Target Link Speed, Retrain Link, Perform Equalization, Hardware Autonomous Speed Disable, lane equalization presets), the time budget it consumes and why that matters at boot, and the failure signatures of each stage. It uses the LTSSM substates of Module 08 as its skeleton.

Read first: Chapter 9 (PCIe Link Training) for TS1/TS2 fields and the shape of lane and speed negotiation; Chapter 54 (Equalization) for presets, FS/LF, phases and margining; Module 08 (LTSSM) for substates and timeouts; Chapter 5 for widths, lane reversal and bifurcation in hardware.

Kid story

Two marching bands meet on a long bridge and want to march across together. First the leader of one band shouts "Band number 1, are you with me?" and the other band's drummers on each row answer "Band 1!" — except the rows that cannot hear, who stay quiet. Then the leader numbers the rows that answered: "you are row 0, you row 1, row 2, row 3". If the other band already counts its rows from the other end, they say so, and everyone agrees which way to count.

Then they start marching slowly. Once they are in step, the leader says "let's go faster!" Both bands stop for a moment, change their drum beat, and try again at the faster pace. At the faster pace, the drummers on each side coach each other: "hit a bit harder on the first beat… a bit softer… perfect". If at the new speed they keep stumbling, they go back to the slower pace that worked.

Nobody announces how fast they ended up marching. You find out only by looking at their feet.

Mechanism

The lab system trains its Gen4 x4 link from cold. Each step names who acts, what changes, where, and the evidence.

  1. Polling at 2.5 GT/s. Who: both LTSSMs. What changes: bit and symbol lock per lane; polarity corrected per lane. Where: each receiver. Evidence: TS1 then TS2 with link and lane = PAD; Data Rate Identifier in every set already advertises the rates each side supports (2.5 through 16 GT/s).
  2. Link number. Who: DSP proposes link 0 on all lanes; USP echoes link 0 on lanes it will use, PAD on others. What changes: candidate lane set. Evidence: TS1 link field per lane (Configuration.Linkwidth).
  3. Lane numbers. Who: DSP proposes 0–3; USP accepts or reports reversal. What changes: logical lane mapping. Evidence: TS1 lane fields (Configuration.Lanenum).
  4. Confirm and deskew. Who: both. What changes: width fixed (x4); deskew established. Evidence: TS2 with agreed numbers; Configuration.Idle; L0 at 2.5 GT/s; InitFC DLLPs.
  5. Decide to change speed. Who: DSP (autonomously because a higher common rate exists, within Target Link Speed). What changes: LTSSM enters Recovery. Evidence: TS1 with the speed-change flag; the USP follows.
  6. Phase 0 for 8 GT/s. Who: DSP. What changes: USP learns which transmitter preset (and receiver hint) to use at 8 GT/s, from the DSP's Lane Equalization Control values. Evidence: EQ TS2 at 2.5 GT/s in Recovery.RcvrCfg.
  7. Rate change. Who: both. What changes: EIOS, electrical idle, PLL to 8 GT/s, encoding to 128b/130b. Evidence: idle gap; EIEOS and TS1 at the new rate.
  8. Phases 1–3 at 8 GT/s. Who: Phase 1 both; Phase 2 USP requests DSP transmitter changes; Phase 3 DSP requests USP transmitter changes. What changes: transmitter coefficients per lane and direction. Evidence: TS1 equalization-control fields, requested and echoed presets/coefficients, Reject Coefficient flags; Link Status 2 phase success bits afterwards.
  9. Back to L0 at 8 GT/s, then repeat steps 5–8 for 16 GT/s (Phase 0 information for 16 GT/s is carried at 8 GT/s; status lands in the Physical Layer 16.0 GT/s capability). Evidence: second Recovery with equalization; LnkSta shows 16 GT/s.
  10. Software observes. Who: firmware and OS. What changes: nothing on the link. Evidence: LnkSta, LnkSta2, per-rate status; kernel messages if bandwidth is below capability.

Width decision rules

  • The resulting width is the largest valid width (x1, x2, x4, x8, x16; x12 and x32 have special status in the specification and are rarely implemented) whose lanes are all usable, starting from logical lane 0. Verified as the general rule; check the Configuration substate rules for x12/x32 details.
  • Lane 0 is special: if the physical lane that must become logical lane 0 is broken, the link cannot form at any width unless lane reversal lets the other end of the lane group become lane 0.
  • A port's maximum width can be reduced by firmware or straps (bifurcation) — a x16 slot bifurcated x4x4x4x4 gives an SSD in the first quarter a x4 link even if the SSD is x8-capable.
  • Upconfiguration (returning to a wider width after a narrower one) is optional and advertised in Link Capabilities; without it, a link that narrowed stays narrow until a reset or retraining through Detect.

Speed decision and fallback rules

  • The goal rate is the highest rate advertised by both ends in their Data Rate Identifiers, capped by Target Link Speed. Ports traditionally step through every intermediate rate, equalizing at each from 8 GT/s upward. Verified
  • From the 5.0 revision, optional mechanisms let capable ports skip intermediate equalization ("equalization bypass to highest rate") or skip equalization when both agree it is unnecessary ("no equalization needed"). Version-dependent; support and disable bits live in the 32.0 GT/s capability registers. Uncertain on exact field names in your revision.
  • If a port cannot achieve lock at the new rate within the Recovery timeouts, both return to the previous rate (Recovery.Speed again) and the link survives at the lower rate. Whether and when a port retries the higher rate is Implementation-dependent; software can retry with Retrain Link.
  • Equalization is done once per rate after a fundamental reset; later speed changes to that rate reuse stored results unless equalization is requested again (by Perform Equalization, by a Link Equalization Request from the partner, or by the port deciding to redo it).

Time budget

Ttrain ≈ Tdetect + Tpoll+cfg + Σr (Tspeed,r + TEQ,r)
Ttrain
time from release of reset (or the start of training) to L0 at the final rate
Tdetect
time until the partner's receivers are detected (includes the partner's own reset release time)
Tpoll+cfg
time spent in Polling and Configuration at 2.5 GT/s (normally well under a millisecond on a healthy link)
r
each higher rate the link steps through (8, 16, 32 GT/s …)
Tspeed,r
Recovery speed-change time for rate r (electrical idle, PLL relock, re-lock), typically microseconds
TEQ,r
equalization time at rate r: sub-millisecond when Phases 2–3 converge quickly, up to the phase timeouts (milliseconds each) when they search long or fail

A healthy Gen4 link trains in a few milliseconds. With retimers (equalization per segment), long Phase 2/3 searches, or a failed rate followed by fallback, training can take tens or hundreds of milliseconds. That matters when platform firmware enumerates devices at a fixed time after reset: a device whose link is still training may be missed at boot and appear only after a later rescan. Derived

The ladder for link training

Two bands agree which rows march and how to count them, start slow, speed up, coach each other's drummers, and fall back to a slower pace if they stumble.

Training finds which lanes work, numbers them, fixes the width, then raises the rate step by step, tuning signal quality at each rate, and settles at the best rate that works.

Each port's LTSSM drives its SerDes: lock, polarity, deskew, PLL rate changes, transmitter FIR coefficients, receiver adaptation; firmware provides presets and limits.

The PCI Express Base Specification's Physical Layer Logical Block defines TS formats, Configuration and Recovery substates, equalization procedures and the registers that control and report them.

TS1/TS2 on every lane carrying link/lane numbers, rates, speed-change requests, equalization requests and responses; EIOS/EIEOS around each rate change.

A capture shows PAD versus numbered lanes, the speed-change handshake, idle gaps, equalization iterations with requested/echoed presets or coefficients, rejections, and the final rate.

Width below expectation → which lanes returned PAD and why; rate below expectation → which equalization phase or rate failed, or which cap applied; slow training → which stage consumed the time.

Specification discipline

Applicable document: PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 — Physical Layer Logical Block (Training Sets, LTSSM Configuration and Recovery substates, Link Equalization Procedure), plus PCI Express Base Specification — PCI Express Capability Structure; Secondary PCI Express Extended Capability; Physical Layer 16.0 GT/s and 32.0 GT/s Extended Capabilities; Lane Margining at the Receiver Extended Capability. Retimer behaviour is specified in the Base Specification's retimer sections. Form-factor and CEM specifications constrain which lanes exist and channel budgets.

Training starts at 2.5 GT/s. Width is negotiated in Configuration using link and lane numbers in TS1/TS2. Rates above the initial one are reached through Recovery with a speed-change handshake. Equalization is performed per rate from 8 GT/s upward in up to four phases, with results recorded in status registers. Retrain Link and Target Link Speed are software controls at downstream ports.

A link that comes up one rate below the common maximum, with the lower rate's equalization bits all successful and the higher rate's status showing incomplete equalization, failed at the higher rate rather than being capped. A device missing at boot but present after a rescan, with nothing else wrong, trained too slowly for firmware's enumeration window.

The DSP's choice of when to initiate speed changes, receivers' evaluation algorithms, how long each implementation spends in Phases 2/3 within the allowed bounds, whether ports retry failed rates autonomously, and firmware's preset tables all vary by vendor and board.

Each new rate brought new capability structures and TS fields; 5.0 added bypass options and modified TS usage; 6.0 added PAM4 equalization, precoding options and Flit Mode negotiation. Read the capability versions both ports report before interpreting a training capture.

The specification does not say how long training "should" take beyond its timeouts, which presets a particular board needs, or which rate a port should try after a failure. It also does not give a register that records "why the link trained at x2" — that must be reconstructed from evidence.

Never assume: that x4 capability at both ends means x4 lanes are wired; that equalization success at 8 GT/s says anything about 16 GT/s; that a link trained once at full speed will retrain at full speed; that the preset in Lane Equalization Control is the final setting; that Retrain Link is harmless on a production system (traffic stalls, and a marginal link may not come back at the same rate).

Ordering, completion and errors

  • Stages are strictly ordered: no rate change before L0 at the current rate; no Phase 2 before Phase 1; Phase 3 after Phase 2.
  • Completion of a stage is always "enough consecutive matching sets" or "evaluation satisfied", bounded by timeouts. Exceeding a timeout is not reported as an AER error; its trace is in status bits (phase success, equalization complete), in the resulting rate and width, and in vendor logs.
  • Errors that do surface: Receiver Errors during training are expected and not counted as failures in the same way as in L0 (Implementation-dependent how ports log them); Link Bandwidth Management and Autonomous Bandwidth status bits record rate/width changes after initial training.

Beginner lab: predict the training outcome, then compare

Safe lab

  • Objective: from both ends' capabilities and the root port's controls, predict width and rate; compare with LnkSta; classify the result.
  • Prerequisites: Chapter 5 lab; this module's decision rules.
  • Setup: Python 3; values from lspci -vvv or the demo.
"""Predict a link's trained width and rate and classify the observed result (executable).
Speeds in GT/s; widths as integers. Edit DEMO or pass your own values in the dict."""

DEMO = {
    "rp":  {"speeds": [2.5, 5, 8, 16, 32], "max_width": 4, "target": 32},
    "dev": {"speeds": [2.5, 5, 8, 16], "max_width": 4},
    "wired_lanes": 4,
    "observed": {"speed": 8, "width": 4},
}

def expect(cfg):
    common = sorted(set(cfg["rp"]["speeds"]) & set(cfg["dev"]["speeds"]))
    rate = max(s for s in common if s <= cfg["rp"]["target"])
    width_cap = min(cfg["rp"]["max_width"], cfg["dev"]["max_width"], cfg["wired_lanes"])
    width = max(w for w in (1, 2, 4, 8, 16) if w <= width_cap)
    return rate, width

def classify(cfg):
    rate, width = expect(cfg)
    obs = cfg["observed"]
    msgs = []
    if obs["speed"] < rate:
        msgs.append(f"rate degraded: {obs['speed']} < expected {rate} GT/s -> check per-rate EQ status, retimers, Target Link Speed history")
    if obs["width"] < width:
        msgs.append(f"width degraded: x{obs['width']} < expected x{width} -> check lanes, reversal, bifurcation")
    return (rate, width), msgs or ["as expected"]

print(classify(DEMO))

  • Observations: note whether the kernel's "limited by" message agrees.
  • Questions: (1) Why is the root port's Gen5 capability irrelevant here? (2) Why does the model take wired_lanes as an input you cannot read from registers? (3) What would make the demo "as expected" at 8 GT/s?
  • Expected reasoning: the SSD caps the common rate at 16 GT/s; wiring is a board fact (schematic, slot specification); a Target Link Speed of 8 GT/s.

Intermediate lab: simulate width negotiation with reversal and broken lanes

Safe lab Simulation.

  • Objective: model Configuration's width and lane-number decisions for x8/x4 ports with lane reversal support flags and broken lanes, and explain each outcome.
  • Prerequisites: width decision rules above.
  • Setup: Python 3.
"""Teaching model of width/lane negotiation (simulation; simplified rules, no x12/x32).
dsp_lanes: physical lanes at the DSP. wiring[i] = USP physical lane connected to DSP lane i (None = not wired).
broken: set of DSP lanes that cannot lock. reversal: which side can handle reversed numbering."""

VALID = (16, 8, 4, 2, 1)

def negotiate(dsp_lanes, usp_lanes, wiring, broken, dsp_rev, usp_rev):
    usable = [i for i in range(dsp_lanes) if wiring[i] is not None and i not in broken]
    for order, who in ((list(range(dsp_lanes)), "normal"), (list(reversed(range(dsp_lanes))), "DSP reverses")):
        if who == "DSP reverses" and not dsp_rev:
            continue
        for w in VALID:
            if w > min(dsp_lanes, usp_lanes):
                continue
            lanes = order[:w]
            if not all(l in usable for l in lanes):
                continue
            usp = [wiring[l] for l in lanes]
            straight = usp == list(range(w))
            reversed_ok = usp == list(reversed(range(usp_lanes)))[:w] or usp == list(range(w))[::-1]
            if straight or (reversed_ok and usp_rev):
                how = who + ("" if straight else ", USP reverses")
                return f"x{w} on DSP lanes {lanes} ({how})"
    return "no valid width: Configuration times out -> Detect"

x4_straight = [0, 1, 2, 3]
x4_reversed = [3, 2, 1, 0]
cases = [
    ("straight, all good", (4, 4, x4_straight, set(), False, False)),
    ("straight, lane 2 broken", (4, 4, x4_straight, {2}, False, False)),
    ("straight, lane 0 broken", (4, 4, x4_straight, {0}, False, False)),
    ("straight, lane 0 broken, DSP can reverse", (4, 4, x4_straight, {0}, True, False)),
    ("straight, lane 0 broken, both can reverse", (4, 4, x4_straight, {0}, True, True)),
    ("board reversed, USP supports reversal", (4, 4, x4_reversed, set(), False, True)),
    ("board reversed, nobody supports reversal", (4, 4, x4_reversed, set(), False, False)),
    ("x8 SSD in x4-wired slot", (4, 8, x4_straight, set(), False, False)),
]
for name, args in cases:
    print(f"{name:45s} -> {negotiate(*args)}")
  • Procedure: run; add a case for a x8 DSP with only lanes 0–3 wired; add a case where lanes 1 and 3 are broken.
  • Observations: record each outcome and the rule responsible.
  • Questions: (1) Why does a broken lane 2 give x2 rather than x3? (2) How can a broken lane 0 still yield a link? (3) What does a real analyzer show in the "nobody supports reversal" case?
  • Expected reasoning: valid widths only; reversal makes the far lane logical lane 0 (here only if the DSP supports reversal, and the resulting width uses contiguous lanes from the other end); the analyzer shows Configuration.Lanenum TS1 with mismatched lane numbers repeating until the timeout, then Detect.
Solution

Straight all good: x4. Lane 2 broken: x2 on lanes 0–1. Lane 0 broken, no reversal: no valid width — the link fails. Lane 0 broken, only the DSP can reverse: still no link in this model, because using DSP lanes 3 and 2 as logical lanes 0 and 1 also presents reversed numbering to the USP (its physical lane 3 receives lane number 0), which it cannot accept. Lane 0 broken, both can reverse: x2 on DSP lanes [3, 2]. Discuss why real ports specify reversal support per side, and why board designers avoid relying on it. Board reversed, USP supports reversal: x4. Nobody supports reversal: Configuration times out. x8 SSD in x4 slot: x4.

Advanced lab: an equalization search under FS/LF limits and a time budget

Safe lab Simulation with a toy channel model.

  • Objective: implement a Phase 2-style search in which a receiver requests transmitter coefficients, the transmitter rejects out-of-bound requests, and each evaluation costs time; compare a preset-first strategy with a naive coefficient climb, against a phase timeout.
  • Prerequisites: Chapter 54 (FS, LF, presets).
  • Setup: Python 3. The "eye" function is a toy, not a channel simulator.
"""Toy Phase 2 search (simulation). Coefficients are in transmitter units: c_pre <= 0, c_post <= 0, c0 > 0,
|c_pre| + c0 + |c_post| = FS, and c0 - |c_pre| - |c_post| >= LF. The eye score is a made-up smooth function
with an optimum near the channel's ideal de-emphasis and preshoot."""

FS, LF = 48, 12
EVAL_US = 500            # time to apply a setting and evaluate it (illustrative)
TIMEOUT_US = 24_000      # phase budget (illustrative; check the specification for real bounds)
IDEAL = (-5, -12)        # (c_pre, c_post) the toy channel likes

def legal(pre, post):
    c0 = FS - abs(pre) - abs(post)
    return pre <= 0 and post <= 0 and c0 - abs(pre) - abs(post) >= LF

def eye(pre, post):
    return 100 - (pre - IDEAL[0]) ** 2 - 0.5 * (post - IDEAL[1]) ** 2

def search(start, strategy):
    t, best, rejects = 0, start, 0
    tried = {start}
    while t + EVAL_US <= TIMEOUT_US:
        if strategy == "naive":                       # always push post-cursor harder, ignore LF
            cand = (best[0] - 1, best[1] - 3)
        else:                                         # neighbourhood search that respects legality
            cands = [(best[0] + dp, best[1] + dq) for dp in (-1, 0, 1) for dq in (-2, 0, 2)]
            cands = [c for c in cands if c not in tried and legal(*c)]
            if not cands:
                break
            cand = max(cands, key=lambda c: eye(*c))
        t += EVAL_US
        tried.add(cand)
        if not legal(*cand):
            rejects += 1                              # transmitter echoes Reject Coefficient Values
            continue
        if eye(*cand) > eye(*best):
            best = cand
        elif strategy == "smart":
            break                                     # local optimum reached
    return best, round(eye(*best), 1), t, rejects

start = (0, -6)                                       # roughly a moderate de-emphasis preset
for s in ("naive", "smart"):
    best, score, t, rej = search(start, s)
    print(f"{s:6s}: final (c_pre, c_post)={best} eye={score} time={t} us rejects={rej}")
  • Procedure: run; then change IDEAL to (−10, −20) (a channel that wants more than the transmitter can legally give) and rerun; then halve TIMEOUT_US.
  • Observations: the naive search racks up rejects and burns the whole budget; the legal neighbourhood search converges quickly; with an unreachable ideal, both end at a constrained setting.
  • Questions: (1) Which trace above does the naive strategy reproduce? (2) Why does the time per evaluation matter as much as the algorithm? (3) What does a receiver gain from starting at a good preset?
  • Expected reasoning: the "failed" trace (rejections until timeout); every evaluation needs settling and error counting at the receiver, so iterations are expensive; a good starting preset (from firmware's Lane Equalization Control) shortens or eliminates the search.
Solution

With the defaults, the naive strategy improves a few steps and then requests illegal settings (LF violated), collecting rejects until the 24 ms budget ends; the smart strategy reaches the legal optimum near (−5, −12) in a handful of evaluations (a few milliseconds). With IDEAL = (−10, −20), the optimum is outside the legal region; the smart search stops at the best legal boundary point. The engineering lesson: a receiver's search must respect the FS/LF it was told in Phase 1, and board presets should start it close to the answer.

Extension: add two segments (retimer) and a total budget, and show how a slow segment delays the whole link.

Senior forensic lab: "Gen4 in the lab, Gen3 in the rack"

Safe lab Paper exercise with synthetic evidence.

  • Objective: combine registers, logs and configuration knowledge to localise a rate degradation that only occurs in production chassis.
  • Setup (evidence, synthetic):
Lab bench (direct slot):      LnkSta 16GT/s x4; LnkSta2 Retimer-; 16 GT/s EQ complete, phases 1-3 successful
Rack chassis (riser + retimer): LnkSta 8GT/s x4;  LnkSta2 Retimer+; 8 GT/s EQ complete, phases 1-3 successful
                                16 GT/s EQ: Complete-, Phase1+, Phase2-, Phase3-
Rack, same SSD, chassis cooled to 20 C: LnkSta 16GT/s x4 after cold boot (3 of 5 boots)
Root port 16 GT/s Lane Equalization Control: DSP Tx preset P4, USP Tx preset P4 on all lanes (firmware default)
Retimer firmware: version A on failing racks, version B on two racks that always train at 16 GT/s
  • Questions: (1) Which phase fails and what does that imply? (2) What do temperature and retimer firmware add? (3) Which owner(s) should receive the bug?
Solution

At 16 GT/s Phase 1 succeeds (training sets exchanged at the new rate) but Phase 2 fails, so the link drops back to 8 GT/s: the problem is in tuning one direction under a retimer, not basic connectivity. Temperature dependence indicates marginal channel loss; the P4 (no de-emphasis) starting preset is a poor start for a long riser channel; retimer firmware version B succeeding points at the retimer's own Phase 2 handling for its segment. Root cause (medium confidence): retimer firmware A's equalization in the riser segment, compounded by an unsuitable default preset. Actions: retimer vendor (firmware A vs B difference); platform firmware (board-specific 16 GT/s presets). Regression test: 100 cold boots per rack at 20 °C and 40 °C inlet; pass criterion 16 GT/s with all phases successful every boot.

Extension: use Lane Margining at the Receiver (Chapter 54) on the rack systems at 16 GT/s after a successful boot to quantify margin per lane and segment.

Debugging

Pattern 1: narrower link than expected

Symptom
LnkSta width below both ends' LnkCap width.
Possible causes
Lanes not wired (slot or adapter); bifurcation; broken lane; reversal unsupported; one lane failing to lock at 2.5 GT/s.
Evidence
Board/slot documentation; bifurcation settings; LnkCap max width at the DSP (reduced by bifurcation); which lanes returned PAD (analyzer).
First check
Is the DSP's maximum width already the observed width (bifurcation or slot)?
Second check
Is the result a valid width just below a broken lane (x2 when lane 2 or 3 fails)?
Disambiguating experiment
Move the SSD to another slot; swap adapters.
Likely conclusion
Wiring/configuration or a lane fault.
Confidence
High.

Pattern 2: slower link than expected

Symptom
LnkSta rate below common capability.
Possible causes
Target Link Speed cap; failed equalization at the higher rate; retimer limits; Hardware autonomous reduction (thermal); a deliberate firmware limit.
Evidence
LnkCtl2 Target; per-rate equalization status; LBMS/LABS; retimer presence.
First check
Target Link Speed at the DSP.
Second check
Equalization status at the next-higher rate.
Disambiguating experiment
Retrain on a test system (with Perform Equalization); move to a slot without retimer.
Likely conclusion
Equalization failure at the higher rate, or a cap.
Confidence
Medium-high.

Pattern 3: device missing at boot, present after rescan

Symptom
SSD absent from early boot or OS install but appears later or after hot-plug rescan.
Possible causes
Slow device reset release; long equalization (retimers, searches); firmware enumerates before DL_Active.
Evidence
Time from PERST# to L0 (analyzer); firmware enumeration time; DLLSC events in logs.
First check
Does the kernel log a hot-plug Link Up for that slot after boot?
Second check
Training duration measurement.
Disambiguating experiment
Cap the rate at 8 GT/s in firmware: if the device then appears at boot, equalization time is the factor.
Likely conclusion
Training-time versus enumeration-time mismatch.
Confidence
High with timing data.

Pattern 4: full speed but errors in one direction only

Symptom
Receiver Errors only at the SSD (or only at the root port) after training at full rate.
Possible causes
Phase 2 or Phase 3 did not converge (timeouts or rejected requests) leaving one direction on a poor setting.
Evidence
Per-rate phase success bits; rejected coefficient requests in a capture.
First check
Which phase is marked unsuccessful at the current rate?
Second check
Margins in the affected direction.
Disambiguating experiment
Change the starting preset for that direction and re-equalize.
Likely conclusion
Equalization outcome in one direction.
Confidence
Medium-high.

Pattern 5: link trains differently on every boot

Symptom
Rate or width varies between boots on the same hardware.
Possible causes
Marginal channel near a threshold; temperature; non-deterministic equalization search; clocking marginality; device-side timing.
Evidence
Boot-by-boot record of LnkSta and per-rate status; temperatures; margins.
First check
Is the variation in rate (equalization) or width (lane lock)?
Second check
Correlation with temperature or boot type (cold versus warm).
Disambiguating experiment
100-boot loop at controlled temperatures with logging.
Likely conclusion
Marginal channel at the top rate.
Confidence
Medium.

Pattern 6: retraining makes things worse

Symptom
After a software retrain (or a tool's "fix link speed"), the link comes back slower or not at all.
Possible causes
Marginal link whose original equalization was lucky; retrain to a rate requiring fresh equalization; device mishandles Recovery-based speed change.
Evidence
Status before and after; per-rate equalization bits; device response in capture.
First check
Was Perform Equalization set or did the partner request equalization?
Second check
Does a fundamental reset restore the original state?
Disambiguating experiment
Repeat retrains in a loop on a test system and count outcomes.
Likely conclusion
Marginality exposed by retraining.
Confidence
Medium.

Interview

Beginner

  1. At what rate does every PCIe link start training?

    Model answer

    2.5 GT/s.

  2. Which messages carry training information?

    Model answer

    TS1 and TS2 ordered sets on every lane.

  3. What decides the final width?

    Model answer

    The largest valid width of contiguous usable lanes from logical lane 0 that both ports accept, within their maximum widths and the wiring.

  4. Where does Linux show the trained speed and width?

    Model answer

    lspci -vv LnkSta, and sysfs current_link_speed/current_link_width.

  5. Why does training sometimes produce x2 on an x4 link?

    Model answer

    Lane 2 or 3 failed to participate; x3 is invalid, so the link uses lanes 0–1.

Intermediate

  1. Who usually initiates a speed change, and how?

    Model answer

    The downstream port, entering Recovery and sending TS with the speed-change flag; the partner follows.

  2. What is Phase 0 for?

    Model answer

    Before switching rate, the DSP tells the USP which transmitter preset (and receiver hint) to start with at the new rate.

  3. Why is equalization repeated for every rate?

    Model answer

    Channel loss and the optimal coefficients depend on frequency; settings for 8 GT/s do not suit 16 GT/s.

  4. What do Target Link Speed and Retrain Link let software do?

    Model answer

    Cap the rate and force a Recovery so the link renegotiates within the new cap.

  5. How does a retimer change training?

    Model answer

    It participates as a Physical Layer node; equalization is done per segment, training takes longer, and its presence is reported in Link Status 2.

Advanced

  1. What happens if a receiver requests coefficients outside the transmitter's FS/LF limits?

    Model answer

    The transmitter rejects them (Reject Coefficient Values in its echoed TS1) and keeps its previous setting; repeated invalid requests burn the phase's time budget.

  2. Explain the speed-change fallback.

    Model answer

    If lock or equalization cannot be achieved at the new rate within the Recovery timeouts, both ports return to the previous rate through Recovery.Speed; the link stays up at the lower rate.

  3. Why can training time cause a device to be missed at boot?

    Model answer

    Firmware may enumerate at a fixed time; device reset release plus per-rate, per-segment equalization can exceed it. Software should gate on DL_Active.

  4. What evidence distinguishes a Target Link Speed cap from an equalization failure?

    Model answer

    LnkCtl2 Target value; per-rate equalization status for the next rate (incomplete or failed phases mean failure; untouched means it never tried).

  5. Why is lane 0 special in width negotiation?

    Model answer

    Widths are built from logical lane 0 upward; without it no width is valid unless reversal makes another physical lane logical lane 0.

Senior

  1. A product team wants to ship with rate capped at Gen4 on a Gen5 platform "for stability". How do you evaluate?

    Model answer

    Quantify the performance cost for the workload (often small for a Gen4 SSD), the failure data behind the request (margins, equalization outcomes, error rates at Gen5), and whether a board or preset fix would solve it. A cap is a valid mitigation if documented and reversible, not a substitute for root cause.

  2. Design a link-training validation matrix for a new SSD.

    Model answer

    Widths x1/x2/x4 including reversal and polarity cases; every rate pair and directed speed change; equalization from each preset with and without Perform Equalization; phase timeout behaviour with an exerciser that rejects or delays; retimer topologies; temperature corners; thousands of resets of each type; training-time distribution against platform enumeration limits; margin measurements after training.

  3. How would you build fleet telemetry for training health?

    Model answer

    Record at every boot and periodically: LnkSta per port, per-rate equalization status, retimer presence, LBMS/LABS, error counters, and margins where safe; compare with expected values per platform; alert on any deviation and on trends.

  4. When is it right to change Lane Equalization Control presets in platform firmware?

    Model answer

    When board characterization shows a better starting point for that channel class: searches converge faster and more reliably. Changes need validation across devices, because presets apply to every device plugged into that port.

  5. Root port says Phase 3 failed at 16 GT/s. Whose problem is it?

    Model answer

    Phase 3 tunes the SSD's transmitter as evaluated by the root port's receiver. Suspects: SSD transmitter (range, response to requests), the upstream channel, the root port's evaluation. A capture of requests and responses and a second device separate them.

Quiz

  1. Which port proposes link and lane numbers?

    • The downstream port
    • The upstream port
    • Whichever has more lanes
    • Software
    Answer and reasoning

    The downstream port proposes; the upstream port accepts.

  2. Link Status 2 shows 8 GT/s equalization fully successful; the link is at 8 GT/s although both ends support 16 GT/s. Which register do you read next?

    • Device Control
    • BAR0
    • The 16.0 GT/s Physical Layer capability status (and LnkCtl2 Target)
    • MSI-X table
    Answer and reasoning

    The higher rate's equalization status and the cap.

  3. In Phase 2, who evaluates and who adjusts?

    • DSP evaluates, USP adjusts
    • USP evaluates, DSP transmitter adjusts
    • Both evaluate their own transmitters
    • Software
    Answer and reasoning

    The USP's receiver requests changes to the DSP's transmitter.

  4. A x4 link with broken lane 1 and no reversal support trains at:

    • x3
    • x2
    • x4 at lower speed
    • x1
    Answer and reasoning

    Lane 0 works, lane 1 doesn't, so the largest valid contiguous width from lane 0 is x1.

  5. Why are software-directed speed changes back to a previously used rate fast?

    Model answer

    Equalization results for that rate are stored and reused unless redo is requested.

  6. Give two reasons a training capture might show many Phase 2 iterations.

    Model answer

    A poor starting preset far from the optimum; a receiver algorithm that requests illegal coefficients and gets rejected; a lossy channel near the limit.

  7. Why can the presence of a retimer not be inferred from width or speed alone?

    Model answer

    Retimers are transparent to the negotiated result; only the presence bits (or training captures) reveal them.

  8. What should platform firmware check before enumerating a slot?

    Model answer

    That the link is DL_Active (and observe the required post-reset waits), with a bounded wait for slow training.

  9. What does Hardware Autonomous Speed Disable not prevent?

    Model answer

    Speed reductions for reliability reasons (errors).

Teach-back

  1. To a child: explain why the bands start slowly and then speed up.

    Model explanation

    "Marching slowly is easy, so they use it to get in step and agree which rows are marching. Then they try faster. At each new speed the drummers help each other play clearly. If it's too hard, they go back to the speed that worked."

  2. To a junior engineer: explain why the SSD is at 8 GT/s and what you would check.

    Model explanation

    "Training starts at 2.5, picks the width, then steps up through Recovery, equalizing at 8 and then 16. It ended at 8, so either something capped it — Target Link Speed, a retimer, the SSD's own capability — or 16 GT/s equalization failed and it fell back. I check LnkCap2 on both ends, LnkCtl2 at the root port, then the 16 GT/s equalization status and retimer presence."

  3. To a senior engineer: argue how you would make training deterministic across a fleet.

    Model explanation

    "Characterize each board channel class and set presets so searches start near the optimum; qualify retimer firmware; enforce enumeration that waits on DL_Active; collect per-boot training outcomes and margins; set alert thresholds; and keep a documented, reversible rate-cap mitigation for platforms that can't reach margin, with the root cause tracked to the owner."
