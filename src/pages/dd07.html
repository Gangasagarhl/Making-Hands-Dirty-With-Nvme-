This module goes below the "lanes, widths and speeds" of Chapters 5 and 6 into what the Physical Layer actually does with every bit: encoding and block framing, scrambling, ordered sets, clock compensation with SKP, electrical idle, deskew and polarity, and what changes with PAM4 and forward error correction at 64 GT/s. It teaches you to read a symbol-level or block-level analyzer view and to localise errors that never become a single bad TLP.

Orientation

Chapter 5 described the physical link — lanes, differential pairs, transmitters, receivers, reference clocks, width negotiation — and Chapter 6 the generations, encodings and bandwidth arithmetic. This module deepens the logical sub-block of the PCI Express (Peripheral Component Interconnect Express, PCIe) Physical Layer: the transformations applied between a Data Link Layer packet and the serial bits on a lane, and the housekeeping streams (ordered sets) that keep a link synchronised. It also covers enough of the electrical sub-block to explain why errors happen and how the logical layer reports them. The LTSSM that drives these mechanisms is Module 08; the training and equalization exchanges are Module 09.

Read first: Chapter 5 (PCIe Physical Architecture) for lanes, differential signalling, embedded clocking and reference clocks; Chapter 6 (PCIe Generations) for 8b/10b, 128b/130b, NRZ versus PAM4 and Flit Mode at a summary level; Chapter 7 (PCIe Layer Model) for what the Data Link Layer hands down. Related: Chapter 54 (Equalization) for transmitter presets and channel loss.

Kid story

Two children sit at opposite ends of a long garden, each holding a torch. They send messages by flashing. But there is a problem: if the torch stays on for a long time, the receiver loses count — was that five flashes' worth of "on", or six? So they agree a trick: they mix every message with a secret pattern of blinks both of them know, so the light keeps changing and the receiver never loses the rhythm. At the other end, the receiver unmixes it with the same secret pattern.

Every so often the sender also flashes a special "spare" signal that means nothing at all. Why? Because the two children's watches are not quite the same: one counts a tiny bit faster. When the receiver's notebook gets too full, it just skips a spare; when it's running empty, it writes one extra spare. Nobody loses a real message.

And when they want a break, the sender flashes "going quiet now!" and switches off. To start again, she flashes a very obvious wake-up pattern first, so her friend can catch the rhythm before real messages arrive.

Mechanism

Follow one DLLP-and-TLP pair from the Data Link Layer to the wire and back, on the lab system (Gen4, 16 GT/s, x4, 128b/130b). Then see how the 8b/10b and Flit Mode paths differ.

Transmit path at 128b/130b

  1. Frame. Who: transmit logical block. What changes: the TLP is preceded by an STP token carrying its length (and a check on that length); a DLLP is preceded by an SDP token. Where: the data stream. Evidence: analyzer "token" view.
  2. Stripe. Who: same. What changes: consecutive bytes go to lanes 0, 1, 2, 3, 0, 1, … Evidence: per-lane analyzer view shows each lane carrying every fourth byte.
  3. Scramble. Who: per-lane scrambler. What changes: each data byte is XORed with the lane's LFSR output; the LFSR advances. Where: lane logic. Evidence: on the wire the data look random; an analyzer descrambles to display them.
  4. Block. Who: per-lane encoder. What changes: every 16 bytes per lane get a 2-bit sync header marking a data block. Evidence: analyzer block view.
  5. Insert ordered sets. Who: logical block, scheduled by an interval counter. What changes: before an ordered set, the data stream ends with an EDS token; the next block on every lane is an ordered-set block (SKP here); then data resumes. Evidence: SKP blocks at a regular cadence.
  6. Serialise and drive. Who: SerDes. What changes: bits become voltage transitions, shaped by the transmitter equalizer coefficients agreed in training. Evidence: oscilloscope only; analyzers see the decoded result.

Receive path

  1. Recover clock and bits. Who: receiver CTLE/DFE and clock-and-data recovery. What changes: a sampled bit stream in the recovered-clock domain. Evidence: lane margining shows how much margin remains; nothing on the protocol view.
  2. Align and check sync headers. What changes: 130-bit blocks identified; an invalid header is a Receiver Error (and per-lane Lane Error Status at 8 GT/s and above). Evidence: AER correctable status, Lane Error Status register.
  3. Elastic buffer. What changes: crossing from recovered clock to local clock; when the fill level drifts, the receiver drops or adds SKP symbols (in groups of four bytes at 128b/130b). Evidence: received SKP ordered sets of varying length in an analyzer that shows post-buffer data, or unchanged lengths at the wire.
  4. Deskew and descramble. What changes: lanes aligned using ordered sets that were sent simultaneously; data descrambled with LFSRs in lock-step with the transmitter's. Evidence: none unless it fails (framing errors, garbage).
  5. De-frame. What changes: tokens interpreted; TLP and DLLP bytes handed to the Data Link Layer; framing rule violations (bad STP length check, missing EDS before an ordered set, unexpected token) reported as Receiver Errors and typically cause the receiver to direct the LTSSM to Recovery. Evidence: AER Receiver Error, Recovery entries.

How 8b/10b differs (2.5 and 5.0 GT/s)

  • Each byte becomes a 10-bit symbol. Control characters (K-codes) frame packets: STP (K27.7) before a TLP, SDP (K28.2) before a DLLP, END (K29.7) after either, EDB (K30.7) for a nullified TLP; COM (K28.5) starts every ordered set; SKP (K28.0), IDL (K28.3), FTS (K28.1), EIE (K28.7) and PAD (K23.7) appear in ordered sets and training. Verified as the standard 8b/10b PCIe K-code assignments; confirm against the "Symbol Encoding" table in your revision.
  • Transition density and DC balance come from the code itself; the scrambler (polynomial X16 + X5 + X4 + X3 + 1) only whitens the spectrum. The LFSR is re-initialised when a COM symbol is sent or received and is not advanced by SKP symbols; K-codes are not scrambled. Verified as the published rules; check the "Data Scrambling" section for exact seeding and advance rules.
  • Errors show up directly as invalid code words or running-disparity errors on a single symbol, so 8b/10b receivers detect many bit errors at the Physical Layer before any CRC sees them.
  • SKP ordered sets: COM followed by SKP symbols (nominally three), scheduled every 1180 to 1538 symbol times in common-clock or SRNS operation. Receivers add or remove single SKP symbols. Verified as commonly published; confirm intervals in the "Clock Tolerance Compensation" section.

Scrambling at 128b/130b

From 8 GT/s the scrambler carries real weight: there is no code table guaranteeing transitions, so a long string of zeros in a TLP would starve the receiver's clock recovery without it. Each lane uses an LFSR with the polynomial X23 + X21 + X16 + X8 + X5 + X2 + 1, seeded with a lane-dependent value so that adjacent lanes carrying similar data do not radiate in phase. Sync headers are never scrambled; ordered-set blocks have their own rules (for example, the LFSR is reset by EIEOS, and SKP ordered sets report the transmitter's LFSR state in their trailing bytes so an analyzer or receiver can check synchronisation). Verified for the polynomial and per-lane seeding as published; Uncertain for the exact list of which ordered-set symbols bypass scrambling — read "Data Scrambling" and the 128b/130b ordered-set definitions.

Clock compensation in detail

Each end's reference clock is allowed a frequency tolerance (±300 ppm is the commonly cited figure), so in a separate-clock system two ports can differ by up to 600 ppm, before spread spectrum. Spread-spectrum clocking (SSC) deliberately sweeps the clock down by up to about 0.5% at roughly 30–33 kHz to reduce electromagnetic emissions. In a common-clock system both ends share the reference, so SSC moves both together and the long-term rates are identical. In SRIS, both ends spread independently, so the instantaneous difference can approach 5,600 ppm; the elastic buffer must compensate much faster, which is why SRIS requires SKP ordered sets far more often. Uncertain on exact figures: the tolerances and the SRIS SKP intervals are electrical and logical requirements stated in the Base Specification; confirm them for your revision.

Ndrift = Δf × TSKP × R

symbols (or UI) by which the two clocks drift apart between two SKP ordered sets
Δf
fractional frequency difference between the two ends (for example 600 ppm = 600 × 10−6)
TSKP
time between SKP ordered sets, in seconds

symbol rate in symbols per second on one lane

With 8b/10b at 2.5 GT/s (250 M symbols/s), 600 ppm and a SKP every 1538 symbols (6.15 µs): Ndrift ≈ 600 × 10−6 × 6.15 × 10−6 × 250 × 106 ≈ 0.92 symbols. One SKP symbol of compensation per ordered set is enough. With SRIS, Δf can be about ten times larger, so either SKP must come about ten times more often or each must be able to absorb much more. Electrical idle entry and exit

  • Entry: the transmitter sends an EIOS (at 128b/130b an ordered-set block of a fixed identifier byte; at 8b/10b COM followed by IDL symbols), then holds electrical idle. A receiver that sees EIOS knows the silence is intended and does not treat loss of signal as an error.
  • Exit: at 128b/130b rates the transmitter sends EIEOS — a low-frequency pattern of alternating all-zero and all-one bytes — which the receiver's analog detector notices and which also provides block alignment; then TS1/TS2 or FTS. At 2.5 GT/s, squelch detection of differential voltage suffices. Some states rely on "electrical idle inference" when the receiver cannot see the transition: absence of expected traffic for a defined time.
  • Cost: exit latency (from microseconds for L0s to tens of microseconds or more for L1 with substates). This is the hidden reason for slow first accesses after idle in Module 06's slow trace.

Polarity, reversal and deskew

  • Polarity inversion: if a lane's D+ and D− are swapped on a board, the receiver sees inverted training-set identifier symbols during Polling and inverts its own lane. Mandatory support; costs nothing afterwards.
  • Lane reversal: lane 0 at one end wired to lane 3 at the other. Detected in Configuration from lane numbers; support at both ends is not universal (optional for one side), so routing must be checked against both ports' support.
  • Deskew: each lane's path length and receiver latency differ; the receiver buffers early lanes until all lanes' ordered sets align. The allowed lane-to-lane skew grows in UI as rates rise (in nanoseconds it stays in the same small range). Deskew is re-established in Configuration and Recovery and checked on later ordered sets.

What changes at 64 GT/s: PAM4 and Flit Mode

  • PAM4: four amplitude levels, Gray-coded so adjacent levels differ in one bit; 32 GBaud carries 64 Gb/s per lane. Eye height per decision is about a third of NRZ, so the raw bit error rate is many orders of magnitude worse than at 32 GT/s NRZ. Verified as concept.
  • No 128b/130b at 64 GT/s: the PAM4 rate does not add sync headers; data are carried in fixed 256-byte flits (the encoding is sometimes called "1b/1b"). Flit boundaries, not tokens, frame the stream; TLPs are packed into the flit's TLP bytes. Version-dependent (Base Specification 6.0 and later).
  • FEC: each flit carries a small FEC field (described by PCI-SIG as 6 bytes in three interleaved Reed–Solomon-style codewords) that corrects the common single-symbol errors with only a few nanoseconds of latency, and a CRC that detects what FEC cannot correct; a bad flit is retried. Uncertain on codeword details and correction capability; confirm in the Base Specification's Flit Mode sections. See Chapter 6 for the flit byte budget.
  • Error counting changes meaning: at 64 GT/s, corrected FEC events are expected and frequent. Healthy links report many corrections and rare uncorrectable flits; error monitoring shifts from "any Receiver Error is a problem" to "rate of corrections and of retries against a budget". Derived
  • Ordered sets remain (SKP, EIOS, TS1/TS2 in new formats), and Flit Mode can also be used at lower rates when both ports support it. A new low-power width-reduction state, L0p, appears with Flit Mode. Version-dependent

The ladder for the Physical Layer's logical sub-block

Torch messages mixed with a known pattern so the rhythm never disappears, with meaningless "spare" flashes to absorb watch drift, and a "going quiet" signal before switching off.

The Physical Layer turns packets into a self-clocking serial stream per lane: it frames, stripes, scrambles and encodes, and inserts ordered sets for alignment, clock compensation and power management.

SerDes with equalizers and clock recovery; per-lane LFSRs; elastic and deskew buffers; error detectors for invalid symbols and sync headers; electrical-idle detectors.

The Physical Layer Logical Block and Electrical Sub-Block chapters of the PCI Express Base Specification define encodings, scrambling, ordered sets, SKP scheduling, electrical-idle rules, and Flit Mode with FEC from Revision 6.0.

On the wire: data blocks with tokens or flits, ordered-set blocks (SKP at a regular interval, EIOS before idle, EIEOS and TS on exit). None of this is visible to the Transaction Layer.

An analyzer's block or symbol view: per-lane sync headers, token decode, SKP length changes, EDS before ordered sets; error markers for invalid symbols, disparity errors or sync headers.

Receiver Errors concentrated on one lane point at that lane's channel; errors on all lanes in periodic bursts point at clocking; errors only at one rate point at equalization; errors right after idle exit point at electrical-idle timing.

SRIS needs denser SKP because spread spectrum on two independent clocks multiplies the instantaneous frequency difference. Lane-specific error statistics localise a fault to a lane and direction; link-wide periodic errors point at clocking. FEC correction counts at 64 GT/s are not alarms by themselves.

Whether a receiver reports a single invalid sync header as one Receiver Error or many; whether it enters Recovery on the first framing error; how much skew it tolerates beyond the minimum; whether vendor registers expose per-lane error counters or eye margins; analyzer naming of tokens and blocks.

Field layouts of TS1/TS2 at 128b/130b have grown with each revision; Flit Mode ordered sets and the 64 GT/s and 128 GT/s procedures exist only in 6.x and 7.0. Always know which revision both ports claim (the PCI Express Capability version and Link Capabilities 2 supported speeds) before reading a capture.

The specification does not give a "bit error rate per lane" register, does not say how many Receiver Errors per hour are acceptable, and does not specify how analyzers display blocks. This course does not reproduce exact ordered-set byte layouts, SKP interval ranges for every clocking mode, or FEC codeword construction: read them in the Physical Layer Logical Block chapter of the revision in use.

Never assume: that a clean protocol-layer trace means a clean Physical Layer (retries hide errors); that Receiver Errors are caused by the reporting port (they describe what it received, i.e. the partner's transmitter and the channel); that an analyzer interposer is invisible to the channel; that SKP lengths seen after a retimer equal those sent by the root port.

Observable versus internal

  • Observable with an analyzer: decoded symbols or blocks per lane, ordered sets, tokens, error markers, electrical-idle periods, rate changes.
  • Observable with an oscilloscope: eye diagrams, jitter, de-emphasis, electrical-idle voltage, reference clock quality (with suitable fixtures).
  • Observable in registers: negotiated speed and width, AER Receiver Error, Lane Error Status (8 GT/s and above), equalization status, margining results (Gen4 and above, where implemented).
  • Internal, generally unobservable: receiver equalizer settings, CDR phase, elastic-buffer fill, raw pre-FEC error rate unless exposed by vendor registers, the internal eye after the CTLE.

An interposer adds connector transitions and trace length. At 32 GT/s and above it can push a marginal link over the edge, or a retiming interposer can make a bad link look clean because it re-drives the signal. When a problem appears or disappears with the analyzer inserted, record that as evidence, not as noise.

Ordering and completion at the Physical Layer

  • Within a lane, symbols arrive in order; across lanes, order is restored by deskew. There is no Physical Layer acknowledgement: reliability is the Data Link Layer's job (Ack/Nak and replay; per-flit retry in Flit Mode).
  • A data stream at 128b/130b must end with EDS before an ordered-set block (except where an error ends it); after SKP, data may continue; after EIOS, the lane goes idle. Violations are framing errors.
  • Packets from the Data Link Layer are never reordered by the Physical Layer.

Intermediate lab: scrambler behaviour and loss of synchronisation

Safe lab Simulation.

  • Objective: build a 16-bit LFSR scrambler with the 8b/10b-era polynomial, show that it whitens a run of zeros, that descrambling restores data, and that a lost or extra advance corrupts everything until a reset.
  • Prerequisites: the scrambling sections above.
  • Setup: Python 3.
"""Teaching model of an LFSR scrambler with polynomial x^16 + x^5 + x^4 + x^3 + 1, seed 0xFFFF.
It is NOT bit-exact to the PCIe serial description (bit ordering and output tap conventions differ);
it reproduces the behaviour: whitening, self-inverse descrambling, and failure on desynchronisation."""

TAPS = (16, 5, 4, 3)

class LFSR:
    def __init__(self, seed=0xFFFF):
        self.state = seed

    def next_byte(self):
        out = 0
        for i in range(8):
            bit = (self.state >> 15) & 1
            out |= bit << i
            fb = 0
            for t in TAPS:
                fb ^= (self.state >> (t - 1)) & 1
            self.state = ((self.state << 1) | fb) & 0xFFFF
        return out

def scramble(data, lfsr):
    return bytes(b ^ lfsr.next_byte() for b in data)

def max_run(data):
    bits = "".join(f"{b:08b}" for b in data)
    return max(len(r) for r in bits.replace("1", " ").split() + bits.replace("0", " ").split())

payload = bytes(64)                                    # 64 zero bytes: worst case for clock recovery
tx = scramble(payload, LFSR())
print("longest run before:", max_run(payload), "bits; after:", max_run(tx), "bits")
print("descrambled ok:", scramble(tx, LFSR()) == payload)

rx = LFSR()
rx.next_byte()                                         # receiver advanced once too often (e.g. miscounted a SKP)
bad = scramble(tx, rx)
print("desynchronised: corrupted bytes =", sum(1 for a, b in zip(bad, payload) if a != b), "of", len(payload))
  • Procedure: run; then change the payload to random bytes and to a repeating pattern such as 0xAA.
  • Observations: long runs disappear; one extra advance corrupts nearly every byte.
  • Questions: (1) Why must SKP symbols not advance the LFSR at 8b/10b? (2) What resynchronises the scrambler at 8b/10b and at 128b/130b? (3) Why might a single elastic-buffer error cause a burst of LCRC failures rather than one?
  • Expected reasoning: the receiver may add or remove SKPs; if they advanced the LFSR the two ends would diverge. COM resets the LFSR at 8b/10b; EIEOS (and training) at 128b/130b. A dropped or duplicated data symbol shifts framing and LFSR alignment until the next resynchronisation point.
Solution

Output of this model: longest run 512 bits before and 16 bits after (the longest run comes from the all-ones seed at the start); descrambling succeeds; the desynchronised receiver corrupts 63 of 64 bytes (an occasional byte matches by chance). This is why elastic buffers only touch SKP symbols, why ordered sets reset or check the LFSR, and why a physical slip escalates to Recovery instead of being corrected locally.

Extension: implement the 23-bit polynomial X23 + X21 + X16 + X8 + X5 + X2 + 1 with two different seeds and show the outputs of two lanes carrying identical data are uncorrelated.

Advanced lab: a 128b/130b block-stream checker

Safe lab Simulation on synthetic block streams.

  • Objective: write a checker that consumes per-lane block records (as an analyzer export might list them) and flags invalid sync headers, ordered-set blocks not preceded by EDS, SKP intervals out of range, and lane-to-lane mismatches in ordered sets.
  • Prerequisites: mechanism and protocol sections.
  • Setup: Python 3; the generator below makes a stream with injected faults.
"""Check a synthetic per-lane 128b/130b block log (simulation). Record: (index, lane, sync, kind, eds_at_end)
sync: 'D' data, 'O' ordered set, 'X' invalid; kind: 'data', 'SKP', 'EIOS', 'TS1' ...
Interval limits are parameters: set them from the specification for your clocking mode."""
import random

def generate(lanes=4, blocks=2000, skp_every=371, faults=()):
    log = []
    for i in range(blocks):
        is_skp = i % skp_every == skp_every - 1
        for ln in range(lanes):
            kind, sync = ("SKP", "O") if is_skp else ("data", "D")
            eds = (i + 1) % skp_every == skp_every - 1
            if (i, ln) in faults:
                sync = "X"
            log.append((i, ln, sync, kind, eds))
    return log

def check(log, lanes=4, min_gap=370, max_gap=375):
    events, last_skp, prev = [], None, {}
    by_block = {}
    for rec in log:
        by_block.setdefault(rec[0], []).append(rec)
    for i in sorted(by_block):
        recs = by_block[i]
        for (_, ln, sync, kind, eds) in recs:
            if sync == "X":
                events.append((i, ln, "invalid sync header -> Receiver Error"))
            if sync == "O" and prev.get(ln) and not prev[ln][4]:
                events.append((i, ln, "ordered-set block without preceding EDS -> framing error"))
            prev[ln] = (i, ln, sync, kind, eds)
        kinds = {r[3] for r in recs}
        if len(kinds) > 1:
            events.append((i, None, f"lanes disagree on block kind: {sorted(kinds)}"))
        if "SKP" in kinds:
            if last_skp is not None and not (min_gap <= i - last_skp <= max_gap):
                events.append((i, None, f"SKP gap {i - last_skp} blocks outside {min_gap}-{max_gap}"))
            last_skp = i
    return events

random.seed(7)
log = generate(faults={(1203, 2), (1777, 2)})
for e in check(log):
    print(e)
  • Procedure: run as given; then (a) change skp_every to 400; (b) make lane 1 report "data" for one SKP block; (c) remove the EDS flag before one SKP.
  • Observations: each injected fault produces a specific message; record which would be Receiver Errors at a real receiver.
  • Questions: (1) Which faults are lane-local and which link-wide? (2) What would a lane-disagreement on an ordered set mean physically? (3) Why are the SKP gap limits parameters?
  • Expected reasoning: sync-header errors are lane-local (channel or lane SerDes); missing EDS and SKP cadence are transmitter logic issues (link-wide); a lane disagreement means a lane slipped or deskew failed; the limits depend on revision and clocking mode, so hard-coding them would be a truth-policy violation.
Solution

As given, the checker reports invalid sync headers at blocks 1203 and 1777 on lane 2 — the lane-local signature from the slow trace. With skp_every = 400, every gap is flagged: a transmitter scheduling violation (or a different clocking mode than assumed). A single-lane kind mismatch indicates loss of block alignment on that lane. A missing EDS is a framing error reported by the receiver even though all bits were received correctly — a logic bug, not a signal-integrity problem.

Extension: add SKP length tracking per direction and flag lengths that are not the nominal value plus or minus multiples of four bytes.

Interview

Beginner

  1. Why does PCIe encode or scramble data at all?

    Model answer

    The receiver recovers the clock from transitions in the data, needs DC balance through AC coupling, and must find symbol or block boundaries. Raw data cannot guarantee any of those.

  2. What is an ordered set?

    Model answer

    A fixed-format Physical Layer sequence sent on all lanes at once for link management (training, clock compensation, idle entry/exit). It is not a packet and is not acknowledged.

  3. What is the overhead of 8b/10b and of 128b/130b?

    Model answer

    20% and about 1.54%.

  4. What is electrical idle?

    Model answer

    A transmitter state with near-zero differential output, entered after an EIOS, used for low-power states and rate changes.

  5. Where does a Receiver Error get reported?

    Model answer

    At the port that received the bad bits, as an AER correctable error.

Intermediate

  1. Why are SKP ordered sets needed, and why are they sent even when clocks are common?

    Model answer

    They let elastic buffers absorb rate differences. The transmitter cannot know the receiver's clocking, and short-term differences exist even with a common clock, so the schedule is mandatory.

  2. What does EDS do at 128b/130b?

    Model answer

    It ends the data stream and tells the receiver the next block is an ordered-set block.

  3. How does a receiver handle a lane whose D+ and D− are swapped?

    Model answer

    It detects inverted training-set identifiers in Polling and inverts its own lane (polarity inversion), which every receiver must support.

  4. Why is scrambling essential at 128b/130b but secondary at 8b/10b?

    Model answer

    8b/10b's code table guarantees transitions and DC balance; 128b/130b has only a 2-bit header per 128 bits, so transitions come from scrambling.

  5. What is SRIS and what does it change?

    Model answer

    Separate reference clocks with independent spread spectrum. The clock difference can be much larger, so SKP ordered sets must be sent more often and both ends must be configured for it.

Advanced

  1. A receiver's elastic buffer slips one symbol. Trace the consequences up the stack.

    Model answer

    Lost or duplicated data symbol → framing and scrambler misalignment → Receiver Error, corrupted TLP fails LCRC → Nak/replay, or Recovery re-locks; repeated slips → link instability.

  2. Why does PAM4 need FEC when NRZ PCIe did not?

    Model answer

    Three stacked eyes each about one third the height give roughly 9.5 dB less signal-to-noise per decision; the raw error rate becomes too high for retry alone without unacceptable bandwidth and latency cost, so most errors are corrected in place.

  3. How do you tell a clocking problem from a channel problem from counters alone?

    Model answer

    Clocking: all lanes, often both directions, rate-independent, periodic. Channel: specific lanes and directions, strongly rate-dependent, often temperature-dependent.

  4. Why do Receiver Errors at the root port usually implicate the SSD side?

    Model answer

    They describe what the root port received: the SSD's transmitter and the Up channel. The root port's own receiver is the third suspect.

  5. What does a retimer do to SKP ordered sets and why does it matter for analysis?

    Model answer

    It has its own elastic buffer and adjusts SKP lengths; captures on either side of it differ, so SKP lengths cannot be used to reason about end-to-end clock drift across a retimer.

Senior

  1. Design a monitoring policy for Physical Layer health on a fleet of Gen5 NVMe servers.

    Model answer

    Collect per-port correctable counters (RxErr, BadTLP, BadDLLP, Replay) and LnkSta at intervals; alert on rate per hour above a baseline per platform, on any speed/width change from expected, and on Lane Error Status patterns; periodically run margining on idle links where supported; correlate with temperature and firmware versions. Keep thresholds per platform, not global.

  2. The analyzer shows a clean capture, the field shows errors. Plan the investigation.

    Model answer

    Treat the probe as a channel change: check whether it retimes; use in-system evidence (margining, Lane Error Status, counters) without the probe; vary rate and temperature; use a less invasive probe; isolate channel versus transmitter by swapping parts.

  3. What validation would you require before shipping an SSD that claims SRIS support?

    Model answer

    Operation at worst-case ppm offsets with independent SSC at both ends, at every supported rate and width, through every LTSSM transition including L1 exits and speed changes; SKP interval generation at the SRIS rate; tolerance to minimum and maximum SKP lengths; long soak with zero elastic-buffer errors.

  4. How does Flit Mode change your interpretation of error counters?

    Model answer

    Corrected FEC events become normal; what matters is uncorrectable flits and retry rates relative to a budget, and their trend. Per-TLP LCRC errors no longer exist in the same form; retry is per flit.

  5. A board team asks whether they can remove AC-coupling capacitors on an internal link to save cost. Answer.

    Model answer

    No, unless the specification's rules for that interface allow it and both ends are designed for it: receiver detection relies on the coupling, DC levels of the two ends differ, and compliance assumes AC coupling. The cost of an unusable or marginal link far exceeds the capacitors.

Quiz

  1. At 128b/130b, which block property tells the receiver whether it is looking at data or an ordered set?

    • The first byte of the payload
    • The 2-bit sync header
    • The scrambler state
    • The STP token
    Answer and reasoning

    The sync header labels each block; ordered-set identifiers come after.

  2. Which symbol is never advanced through the 8b/10b scrambler?

    • Data bytes
    • TLP payload
    • SKP
    • LCRC bytes
    Answer and reasoning

    SKP symbols can be added or removed by the receiver, so they must not advance the LFSR.

  3. Receiver Errors appear on all four lanes, at 2.5 and 16 GT/s alike, with a period of about 30 µs. Most likely cause:

    • One damaged pair
    • Poor equalization at 16 GT/s
    • A bad TLP from the driver
    • Clocking mismatch with spread spectrum
    Answer and reasoning

    Link-wide, rate-independent, periodic at the SSC modulation period.

  4. What does a 64 GT/s link use instead of 128b/130b?

    • PAM4 signalling with fixed-size flits and FEC
    • 8b/10b
    • 256b/257b
    • NRZ with stronger scrambling
    Answer and reasoning

    Flit Mode with FEC over PAM4.

  5. Why must an ordered-set block at 128b/130b be preceded by EDS during a data stream?

    Model answer

    So the receiver knows the next block is not data; otherwise it is a framing error.

  6. Compute the drift in symbols between two SKPs for 600 ppm, a 6 µs interval, at 250 Msymbols/s.

    Model answer

    600 × 10−6 × 6 × 10−6 × 250 × 106 = 0.9 symbols.

  7. Why can't you trust SKP lengths captured after a retimer to describe the root port's transmitter?

    Model answer

    The retimer's own elastic buffer adds or removes SKP symbols.

  8. Name two Physical Layer errors and one Data Link Layer error.

    Model answer

    Physical: invalid sync header, 8b/10b disparity error, elastic buffer overflow, framing error. Data Link: Bad TLP (LCRC), Bad DLLP, Replay Timer Timeout.

  9. What does "CommClk+" in LnkCtl mean and what does it not guarantee?

    Model answer

    Software set Common Clock Configuration, believing both ends share a reference clock. It does not guarantee the board actually delivers a common clock.

Teach-back

  1. To a child: explain why the torch messages are mixed with a pattern and why spare flashes exist.

    Model explanation

    "If the torch stays on or off for a long time, your friend loses count. Mixing with a known pattern keeps it flickering, and your friend un-mixes it. And because your watches tick slightly differently, you sometimes send a meaningless 'spare' flash that your friend can throw away or copy, so the notebook never overflows or runs dry."

  2. To a junior engineer: walk through how a TLP becomes bits on four lanes at 16 GT/s.

    Model explanation

    "The Data Link Layer gives us the TLP with sequence number and LCRC. We put an STP token in front, stripe the bytes across lanes 0–3, scramble each lane with its LFSR, and every 16 bytes per lane add a sync header saying 'data'. Every ~370 blocks we end the stream with EDS and send a SKP ordered set on all lanes. The receiver locks, checks sync headers, adjusts SKPs in its elastic buffer, deskews, descrambles, and finds the STP to rebuild the TLP."

  3. To a senior engineer: argue how you would localise intermittent Receiver Errors on a Gen5 NVMe link.

    Model explanation

    "First the shape: which port, which lanes (Lane Error Status), which direction, which rates, any periodicity or correlation with power states and temperature. Link-wide and rate-independent means clocking; lane-specific and rate-dependent means channel or equalization; clustered after exits means power-state timing. Then margining per lane, a swap experiment to see whether it follows the device or the slot, and an analyzer only if I know whether it retimes. The report names the segment and the owner."
