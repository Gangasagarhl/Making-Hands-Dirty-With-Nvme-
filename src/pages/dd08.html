Chapter 8 drew the Link Training and Status State Machine (LTSSM) and walked through each state. This module works at substate level: the exit conditions and timeouts that decide every transition, the taxonomy of reasons a link enters Recovery, the handshakes that move a link into and out of L0s, L1, L1 substates and L2, the directed states (Hot Reset, Disabled, Loopback), and how all of this is (and is not) visible to software. It ends with the skill seniors use most: reconstructing a link's history from sparse evidence.

Orientation

The LTSSM is the Physical Layer's controller: it decides what each port transmits (ordered sets, data, electrical idle), interprets what it receives, and reports "link up" to the Data Link Layer. Chapter 8 taught the states and the normal bring-up path. Here we go into the rules that make transitions happen, the evidence each leaves in registers, logs and captures, and the failure signatures that recur in NVMe deployments: links that flap, links that never leave Detect after a reset, slow first I/O after idle, and Recovery storms. The training content of TS1/TS2 (lane numbers, speeds, equalization) is Module 09; the bit-level machinery the LTSSM drives is Module 07.

Read first: Chapter 8 (PCIe LTSSM) for the top-level states and the normal cold bring-up; Chapter 7 (PCIe Layer Model) for how the Data Link Layer depends on "link up"; Chapter 9 (PCIe Link Training) for training sets. Useful: Module 07 for EIOS, EIEOS and FTS.

Kid story

Two walkie-talkie friends play a game with strict rules. First each checks that the other's walkie-talkie is even switched on (a little beep test). Then they say "testing, testing" until each can hear clearly. Then they agree on a channel and who speaks on which button. Only then do they talk properly.

If there is crackle, they don't give up: they say "say again — testing, testing" until it's clear, then carry on (that's going back to the "fix it" step, not starting over). If they want to save batteries, one says "going quiet", and when they wake up they have to do a quick "testing, testing" again first. And if the crackle never clears after many tries, they switch off and start the whole game from the beep test.

The rule book says exactly how many "testing"s you must hear in a row and how long you may wait before giving up. That is why the game always ends the same way, even when the friends have never met.

The pattern to remember: counting (a run of identical received sets) proves the partner is stable; sending enough proves the partner had time to count; timeouts bound everything, so a hopeless link returns to Detect within tens of milliseconds and tries again rather than hanging.

From LinkUp to DL_Active

  1. L0 reached. Who: both LTSSMs. What changes: LinkUp asserted to the Data Link Layer. Evidence: idle data on the wire; Link Status shows the speed and width.
  2. Flow-control initialization. Who: both Data Link Layers. What changes: each sends InitFC1 DLLPs for Posted, Non-Posted and Completion credits, then InitFC2; credit counters set. Evidence: InitFC1/InitFC2 DLLPs on the analyzer.
  3. DL_Active. What changes: TLPs may flow. Data Link Layer Link Active becomes 1 at the downstream port; on hot-plug ports, Data Link Layer State Changed fires. Evidence: Link Status DLLLA, Slot Status, kernel hot-plug messages.
  4. Software waits before configuration. The specification requires software to allow a recovery time after reset or link-up before sending configuration requests (commonly cited as 100 ms, with a related rule for ports above 5 GT/s measured from link training completion); devices may answer early requests with Request Retry Status. Uncertain confirm the exact rules in the Base Specification's reset and configuration timing requirements.

A link that enters Recovery 1,000 times an hour because it leaves L1 1,000 times an hour is healthy. A link that enters Recovery 50 times an hour because of framing errors is sick. Analyzers that summarise "Recovery count" without cause mislead. Seniors look at what happened immediately before each entry.

Power-state handshakes

ASPM L1 (device in D0, link idle): the upstream component (the SSD) decides to enter L1 when its transmit queue is empty and its timers allow, blocks new TLPs, waits for outstanding credits/acks, and sends PM_Active_State_Request_L1 DLLPs. The downstream port, if it agrees, replies PM_Request_Ack and sends EIOS; the SSD sends EIOS; both go to electrical idle in L1. If the downstream port refuses, it sends a PM_Active_State_Nak message and the SSD stays in L0 (and may try L0s instead). Verified as the handshake shape; check the "Active State Power Management" section for the exact rules.

PCI-PM L1 (software puts the device in D3hot): after the PMCSR write, the device sends PM_Enter_L1 DLLPs; the same acknowledge-and-idle sequence follows.

L1 exit: either side leaves electrical idle, sends EIEOS/TS1, and both go through Recovery to L0. The cost is the L1 exit latency (advertised in Link Capabilities as a range), plus, for L1.2, the time to restore the reference clock and common-mode voltage (TPOWER_ON, negotiated through the L1 PM Substates capability).

L2/L3 Ready (system going to sleep or power-off): software sends a PME_Turn_Off message downstream; each device answers PME_TO_Ack; the device then sends PM_Enter_L23 DLLPs, the link goes to L2/L3 Ready, and main power can be removed. A device that never answers PME_Turn_Off stalls the platform's shutdown or forces a timeout.

Directed transitions

  • Hot Reset: software sets Secondary Bus Reset in the downstream port's Bridge Control (or a switch propagates a reset). The port sends TS1 with the Hot Reset bit; the SSD enters Hot Reset, resets its logic as a Conventional Reset (except sticky bits) and both return through Detect. Software must clear the bit and wait the reset recovery time.
  • Disabled: software sets Link Disable in Link Control; the port sends TS1 with the Disable Link bit and then electrical idle; the partner sees link down. Clearing the bit lets the port return to Detect. Used by hot-plug and error recovery.
  • Loopback: a test master directs the partner (TS Loopback bit) to retransmit what it receives, for bit-error-rate testing. Not used in production.
  • Polling.Compliance: transmit compliance patterns for electrical testing; entered when a load is detected but no TS arrive, or when directed (Enter Compliance in Link Control 2, or Compliance Receive). In production, an unexpected entry is a signature of a partner that is electrically present but not training.

The ladder for Recovery

"Say again — testing, testing" until the crackle clears, then carry on talking.

Recovery re-establishes lock and agreement without restarting from scratch; it is also how a link changes speed, re-equalizes, changes width or wakes from L1.

Both ports' receivers re-lock (bit and block alignment, deskew); transmitters may switch rate and equalization; the Data Link Layer pauses and later replays what was lost.

Recovery.RcvrLock, Recovery.Equalization, Recovery.Speed, Recovery.RcvrCfg, Recovery.Idle with normative counts and timeouts in the Base Specification's LTSSM section.

TS1 then TS2 on all lanes, possibly EIOS and electrical idle around a rate change, then idle data and resumed TLP/DLLP traffic; often a Nak and replay afterwards.

On the analyzer: data interrupted by TS1, a gap of microseconds (or milliseconds with equalization), then data again; the event immediately before is the cause.

Classify each entry by cause; healthy causes (L1 exit, intended speed change) are ignored; error-driven causes are localised with Module 07 methods; repeated failures to exit lead to Detect and a surprise-down event.

Specification discipline

Applicable document: PCI Express Base Specification, Revision 6.x/7.0 — Physical Layer Logical Block (Link Training and Status State Machine), with PCI Express Base Specification — Power Management for ASPM, L1 PM substates and L2/L3, PCI Express Base Specification — Data Link Layer for DL_Active and replay-triggered retraining, and the PCI Express Base Specification — PCI Express Capability Structure for Link and Slot registers. The NVMe specifications do not define link states; NVMe power states (Power State Descriptors, Autonomous Power State Transition) are separate and may be correlated with ASPM by device design.

Each port runs its own LTSSM. L1 is always exited through Recovery. Hot Reset, Disabled and Loopback are entered on request carried in training sets. DL_Active follows flow-control initialization after LinkUp. ASPM is controlled through Link Control and is distinct from device power states.

A link that returns to Detect at intervals of roughly 24 to 50 ms after short-lived training is timing out in Polling or Configuration (from the commonly cited timeouts). A Recovery entry immediately preceded by a doorbell after idle is an L1 exit, not an error.

Vendor LTSSM state encodings and history buffers, idle timers for ASPM entry, retry policy after speed-change failure, and the time an SSD's PCIe core takes to start training after PERST# deassertion (within required limits) are all implementation-dependent. So is whether an SSD's internal firmware reset takes the link down.

Flit Mode (6.0+) removes L0s and adds L0p; equalization bypass and "no equalization needed" options arrived in the 5.0 era; substate names and some timeout values have evolved. Know the revision both ports implement.

The specification does not define a standard register that reports the current LTSSM substate, a counter of Recovery entries, or a reason code for Recovery. Software sees only the summaries listed in this module; anything more comes from vendor registers or an analyzer.

Never assume: that "link up" in Link Status means TLPs can flow (check DL_Active); that a link which trained once at full speed will retrain at full speed; that Recovery means errors; that link-down was caused by the link (the device may have reset itself); that every port supports L0s or lane reversal.

Ordering and completion

  • Before entering L1, the requesting port must have no outstanding TLPs that need acknowledgement and must block new TLP scheduling; the downstream port must likewise drain. Transactions are never lost by ASPM, only delayed.
  • During Recovery, TLPs queue; afterwards, unacknowledged TLPs may be replayed. The Transaction Layer sees only delay.
  • When the LTSSM leaves to Detect, the Data Link Layer goes down (DL_Down): outstanding non-posted requests will not be completed, and requesters' completion timers will eventually fire unless software tears them down first.

Beginner lab: decode Link Status like the kernel does

Safe lab Reads a register value you supply (or a demo value).

  • Objective: decode the Link Status register's fields and say what each implies about the LTSSM.
  • Prerequisites: Chapter 8.
  • Setup: Python 3; optionally sudo setpci -s 00:01.1 CAP_EXP+0x12.w (read-only form, no "=") to get a real value.
#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Decode a PCIe Link Status register value (16 bits, at PCI Express Capability + 12h).
Usage: python3 lnksta.py 0x9043   (default: demo value)"""
import sys

SPEEDS = {1: "2.5 GT/s", 2: "5 GT/s", 3: "8 GT/s", 4: "16 GT/s", 5: "32 GT/s", 6: "64 GT/s"}
v = int(sys.argv[1], 16) if len(sys.argv) > 1 else 0x9044
speed = v & 0xF                 # Current Link Speed (index into Supported Link Speeds Vector)
width = (v >> 4) & 0x3F         # Negotiated Link Width
fields = {
    "Link Training (bit 11)": (v >> 11) & 1,
    "Slot Clock Configuration (bit 12)": (v >> 12) & 1,
    "Data Link Layer Link Active (bit 13)": (v >> 13) & 1,
    "Link Bandwidth Management Status (bit 14)": (v >> 14) & 1,
    "Link Autonomous Bandwidth Status (bit 15)": (v >> 15) & 1,
}
print(f"LnkSta = 0x{v:04x}: speed code {speed} ({SPEEDS.get(speed, 'see Supported Link Speeds Vector')}), width x{width}")
for k, bit in fields.items():
    print(f"  {k:45s} {bit}")
if not fields["Data Link Layer Link Active (bit 13)"]:
    print("  note: DLLLA=0 means DL not active, or the port does not implement DL Active reporting")
  • Procedure: run with the demo; then with 0x3044, 0xB024 and a value from your root port.
  • Observations: write down for each value: speed, width, DL active, whether a bandwidth change happened.
  • Questions: (1) Why is the speed field an index rather than a rate? (2) Why might DLLLA read 0 on an SSD's own upstream port even when everything works? (3) What would you do after seeing bit 15 set?
  • Expected reasoning: the speed code indexes the Supported Link Speeds Vector (which, for current devices, maps 1→2.5, 2→5 … in order); DL Active reporting is required of downstream ports in some cases and optional elsewhere, so an upstream port may hardwire it; bit 15 means an autonomous change occurred — correlate with time and device state, then clear it (RW1C, only on a test system) and watch.
Solution

0x9044 (bits 15 and 12 set, bit 13 clear): 16 GT/s, x4, slot clock, autonomous bandwidth change recorded, DLLLA = 0 — on a root port that reports DL Active this would be alarming; check whether the capability bit "Data Link Layer Link Active Reporting Capable" is set in Link Capabilities. 0x3044: 16 GT/s x4, slot clock, DL active — healthy. 0xB024: 16 GT/s x2, slot clock, DL active, LABS set — the link narrowed autonomously: investigate. The bit positions used are the standard Link Status layout; Verified against the PCI Express Capability definition.

Extension: add decoding of Link Capabilities (max speed, max width, ASPM support, L0s/L1 exit latencies, DL Active Reporting Capable).

Intermediate lab: an LTSSM simulator with timeouts and fault injection

Safe lab Simulation.

  • Objective: model the bring-up path at substate granularity with counts and timeouts, inject faults, and observe which state the link ends in and how long it takes.
  • Prerequisites: the substate table above.
  • Setup: Python 3.
"""Teaching LTSSM bring-up simulator (simulation). Time step = 1 µs.
Timeout values are the commonly cited ones and are parameters, not normative constants.
Each lane delivers a TS each step with probability p_good[lane]; consecutive good TS are counted."""
import random

TIMEOUT_US = {"Polling.Active": 24_000, "Polling.Configuration": 48_000,
              "Configuration": 24_000, "Recovery.RcvrLock": 24_000}
NEED = 8

def run(p_good, partner_trains=True, seed=1, max_us=200_000):
    rnd = random.Random(seed)
    lanes = len(p_good)
    state, t_state, t = "Detect", 0, 0
    consec = [0] * lanes
    log = []

    def go(new):
        nonlocal state, t_state, consec
        log.append((t, state, new))
        state, t_state, consec = new, t, [0] * lanes

    while t < max_us:
        t += 1
        if state == "Detect":
            if t - t_state >= 100:                       # detect pulse result after 100 µs (illustrative)
                go("Polling.Active")
            continue
        if state.startswith(("Polling", "Recovery")) or state == "Configuration":
            for ln in range(lanes):
                ok = partner_trains and rnd.random() < p_good[ln]
                consec[ln] = consec[ln] + 1 if ok else 0
            good = [ln for ln in range(lanes) if consec[ln] >= NEED]
            if state == "Polling.Active" and len(good) == lanes:
                go("Polling.Configuration")
            elif state == "Polling.Configuration" and len(good) == lanes:
                go("Configuration")
            elif state == "Configuration" and good:
                width = max(w for w in (1, 2, 4, 8, 16) if w <= lanes and all(l in good for l in range(w)))
                log.append((t, "Configuration", f"width x{width}"))
                go("L0")
                return log, width
            if t - t_state >= TIMEOUT_US.get(state, 1_000_000):
                if state == "Polling.Active" and good:
                    go("Polling.Configuration")          # proceed with the lanes that qualified
                elif state == "Polling.Active" and not partner_trains:
                    go("Polling.Compliance")
                    return log, 0
                else:
                    go("Detect")
    return log, 0

for name, p, trains in (("healthy x4", [0.99] * 4, True),
                        ("weak lane 2", [0.99, 0.99, 0.30, 0.99], True),
                        ("dead lane 0", [0.0, 0.99, 0.99, 0.99], True),
                        ("partner silent", [0.99] * 4, False)):
    log, width = run(p, trains)
    print(f"--- {name}: final width x{width}")
    for e in log[:8]:
        print("   ", e)
  • Procedure: run the four scenarios; then vary p_good for lane 2 from 0.3 to 0.9 and record when the link reaches x4.
  • Observations: the healthy link reaches L0 within a fraction of a millisecond; the weak lane eventually leads to a narrower link; a dead lane 0 makes the link fail to form (logical lane 0 is required); a silent partner ends in Polling.Compliance.
  • Questions: (1) Why does the weak-lane case take so long? (2) Why does a dead lane 0 prevent even x2 in this model, and what do real ports do about it? (3) Which real-world failures does "partner silent" model?
  • Expected reasoning: requiring 8 consecutive good sets is hard at 30% per-set quality, so the link waits until the Polling timeout before proceeding with qualifying lanes; real ports may use lane reversal to make physical lane 3 logical lane 0, so a dead lane 0 can still produce a narrower link on ports that support it; a silent partner models a device whose PCIe core is held in reset (the failed trace above) or a passive test load.
Solution

Healthy: Detect → Polling.Active → Polling.Configuration → Configuration → L0 at x4 in well under 1 ms. Weak lane 2: lane 2 rarely achieves 8 consecutive good sets; after the 24 ms Polling.Active timeout the model proceeds with all lanes that qualified, and Configuration selects x2 (lanes 0–1), because x3 is not a valid width. Dead lane 0: no valid width containing lane 0, and the model returns to Detect repeatedly — the simulation's simplification (no lane reversal) is the reason. Partner silent: Polling.Compliance after the timeout.

Extension: add Recovery: from L0, inject a burst of bad sets; count how many Recovery entries end in L0 versus Detect as the burst length grows past the RcvrLock timeout.

Advanced lab: classify Recovery entries by cause

Safe lab Simulation on a synthetic analyzer event export.

  • Objective: given a time-ordered event list (as an analyzer can export), attribute each Recovery entry to its most likely cause by looking at the preceding events.
  • Prerequisites: the Recovery taxonomy above.
  • Setup: Python 3.
"""Classify Recovery entries in a synthetic event log (executable, standard library only).
Each event: (time_us, source, kind). The rules look back a short window before each Recovery entry."""

EVENTS = [
    (100, "RP", "L1"), (5_000, "RP", "EIEOS"), (5_002, "RP", "Recovery"),
    (9_000, "SSD", "RxError lane2"), (9_001, "SSD", "Recovery"),
    (12_000, "SW", "RetrainLink"), (12_001, "RP", "Recovery"),
    (15_000, "RP", "Nak"), (15_010, "RP", "Nak"), (15_020, "RP", "Nak"), (15_030, "RP", "Nak"),
    (15_031, "RP", "ReplayNumRollover"), (15_032, "RP", "Recovery"),
    (20_000, "SSD", "TS1 speed-change"), (20_001, "SSD", "Recovery"),
    (30_000, "RP", "Recovery"),
]
WINDOW_US = 50

def classify(events):
    out = []
    for i, (t, src, kind) in enumerate(events):
        if kind != "Recovery":
            continue
        before = [e for e in events[:i] if t - e[0] <= WINDOW_US]
        kinds = " ".join(e[2] for e in before)
        if "EIEOS" in kinds and any(e[2] == "L1" for e in events[:i]):
            cause = "L1 exit (normal)"
        elif "RetrainLink" in kinds:
            cause = "software retrain (intended)"
        elif "speed-change" in kinds:
            cause = "speed change requested by " + before[-1][1]
        elif "ReplayNumRollover" in kinds:
            cause = "replay rollover (Data Link Layer; investigate errors)"
        elif "RxError" in kinds:
            cause = "receiver error at " + [e for e in before if "RxError" in e[2]][-1][1] + " (Physical Layer)"
        else:
            cause = "UNEXPLAINED - capture more context"
        out.append((t, src, cause))
    return out

for row in classify(EVENTS):
    print(row)
  • Procedure: run; then add an event list of your own with a Recovery storm (many RxError-driven entries) and one L1-exit entry every second.
  • Observations: the last entry is unexplained; note what you would need to capture to explain it.
  • Questions: (1) Why is a fixed look-back window fragile? (2) How would you tell "partner-initiated" from "self-initiated" Recovery in a real capture? (3) Why should "unexplained" be a first-class output?
  • Expected reasoning: causes may precede entry by more than the window (an L1 entry long before), or several causes overlap; the port whose TS1 appears first in the middle of L0 traffic initiated it; forcing an explanation would hide gaps in evidence.
Solution

Output: 5,002 L1 exit; 9,001 receiver error at SSD; 12,001 software retrain; 15,032 replay rollover; 20,001 speed change requested by SSD; 30,000 unexplained. For the unexplained one, capture both directions with a trigger on the first TS1 in L0 and record which port sent it, plus the 100 µs of traffic before it.

Extension: compute the fraction of time spent in Recovery per cause, and turn that into a health score that ignores normal causes.

Debugging

Pattern 1: link never trains after power-on

Symptom
No device in lspci; root port DL inactive.
Possible causes
PERST# held; no reference clock; no power; receiver detection fails (coupling, termination); partner silent (core in reset); lane mapping unsupported.
Evidence
Analyzer: Detect only, or Polling without received TS, or Compliance; slot power and PERST# timing on a scope.
First check
Does Detect find receivers? (Analyzer, or vendor LTSSM status.)
Second check
Does the partner transmit TS1 at all?
Disambiguating experiment
Known-good SSD in the same slot; the suspect SSD in a known-good slot.
Likely conclusion
Detect failure → power/clock/reset/coupling; Polling without partner TS → device-side reset or firmware; Configuration timeouts → lane mapping.
Confidence
High with an analyzer; medium without.

Pattern 2: link up, Recovery storm, eventual link down

Symptom
Throughput collapse, then Surprise Down and an NVMe controller reset.
Possible causes
Signal integrity or clocking (Module 07); replay rollovers from link-layer issues; a partner repeatedly re-entering Recovery because of an internal fault.
Evidence
AER correctable history before the fatal error; Lane Error Status; analyzer: what precedes each Recovery.
First check
Were correctable errors rising before the link went down?
Second check
Which port initiates Recovery?
Disambiguating experiment
Force a lower speed; if the storm stops, physical margin at the higher rate.
Likely conclusion
Physical margin, unless errors are absent (then device-side).
Confidence
Medium-high.

Pattern 3: link drops cleanly with no prior errors

Symptom
Surprise Down without preceding correctable errors; link returns in tens to hundreds of milliseconds.
Possible causes
Device-side PCIe core reset (watchdog, firmware activation, internal error); power glitch at the device; hot-plug event.
Evidence
Absence of EIOS before idle; device telemetry; presence-detect changes.
First check
Did presence detect change (physical removal or power loss)?
Second check
Device's own logs at the time.
Disambiguating experiment
Correlate occurrences with device firmware operations and workload phases.
Likely conclusion
Device reset; link is the messenger.
Confidence
High with device logs.

Pattern 4: high latency on isolated I/O, normal under load

Symptom
QD1 latency much higher with idle gaps than with continuous I/O.
Possible causes
ASPM L1 or L1.2 exit latency; NVMe autonomous power state transitions (a separate mechanism inside the device); CPU idle states.
Evidence
LnkCtl ASPM; L1 substates enabled; NVMe APST settings; analyzer gap between doorbell intent and delivery.
First check
Is ASPM L1 enabled on this link?
Second check
Is NVMe APST enabled (separately)?
Disambiguating experiment
Disable each mechanism in turn on a test system and measure QD1 latency with idle gaps.
Likely conclusion
Whichever mechanism removes the extra latency when disabled.
Confidence
High.

Pattern 5: device absent after Hot Reset or Secondary Bus Reset

Symptom
After an error-recovery reset, the device never returns until power cycle.
Possible causes
Device mishandles Hot Reset; software does not wait long enough after reset before configuration (device answers RRS, software gives up); link trains at a lower rate and software expects otherwise.
Evidence
Analyzer: Detect, Polling without partner TS, Compliance; or training succeeds but configuration reads get RRS.
First check
Does the link reach L0 after the reset?
Second check
If yes, what do the first configuration reads return?
Disambiguating experiment
Fundamental reset (PERST# or slot power) instead of Hot Reset.
Likely conclusion
Device-side reset handling defect, or host timing violation.
Confidence
High.

Pattern 6: platform shutdown or suspend hangs at the SSD

Symptom
System takes long to suspend or power off; firmware logs a PME_Turn_Off timeout.
Possible causes
SSD does not return PME_TO_Ack; link in a state where messages cannot pass; device busy flushing.
Evidence
Analyzer: PME_Turn_Off sent; no PME_TO_Ack; no PM_Enter_L23.
First check
Was the NVMe shutdown (CC.SHN) completed before PME_Turn_Off?
Second check
Does the SSD answer PME_Turn_Off when idle?
Disambiguating experiment
Compare with a different SSD model on the same platform.
Likely conclusion
Device message handling or host ordering of shutdown steps.
Confidence
Medium.

Interview

Beginner

  1. What is the LTSSM?

    Model answer

    The state machine in each PCIe port's Physical Layer that brings the link up, keeps it working, and manages low-power and special states.

  2. Which state is "normal operation"?

    Model answer

    L0.

  3. Is entering Recovery always a problem?

    Model answer

    No. Speed changes, equalization and L1 exits all use Recovery.

  4. What does DLActive+ in lspci mean?

    Model answer

    The Data Link Layer is active: flow-control initialization completed and TLPs can flow.

  5. What is ASPM?

    Model answer

    Active State Power Management: the link enters L0s or L1 automatically when idle while the device stays in D0.

Intermediate

  1. Why does training rely on counting consecutive training sets?

    Model answer

    A run of identical, correctly received sets proves stable lock and consistent agreement; a single good set could be luck.

  2. What is the difference between LinkUp and DL_Active?

    Model answer

    LinkUp is the Physical Layer's indication; DL_Active requires the Data Link Layer's flow-control initialization to finish.

  3. Describe the ASPM L1 entry handshake.

    Model answer

    The upstream component sends PM_Active_State_Request_L1 DLLPs after quiescing; the downstream port answers PM_Request_Ack (or a Nak message); both send EIOS and go to electrical idle.

  4. How does software cause a Hot Reset?

    Model answer

    By setting Secondary Bus Reset in the downstream port's Bridge Control; the port sends TS1 with the Hot Reset bit.

  5. What does entry into Polling.Compliance during normal bring-up suggest?

    Model answer

    The partner presents termination but does not send training sets — for example a device whose PCIe core is held in reset.

Advanced

  1. Why must L1 exit pass through Recovery?

    Model answer

    Receivers lose lock in electrical idle; Recovery re-establishes bit/block lock and deskew before data resumes.

  2. What determines the extra latency of L1.2 compared with L1.0?

    Model answer

    Restoring the reference clock (CLKREQ#) and common-mode voltage, bounded by T_POWER_ON, before the normal L1 exit through Recovery.

  3. What Data Link Layer event can force a Physical Layer retrain?

    Model answer

    REPLAY_NUM rollover: the same TLPs replayed too many times; the transmitter's Data Link Layer directs the Physical Layer into Recovery.

  4. How do LBMS and LABS differ?

    Model answer

    LBMS records speed/width changes due to software retraining or reliability reasons (non-autonomous); LABS records changes the hardware made autonomously (for example for power). Both are RW1C status bits.

  5. What happens to outstanding non-posted requests when the link goes to Detect?

    Model answer

    They will never complete; the requester's completion timeout fires unless software aborts them; the downstream port reports Surprise Down (if supported) and DPC may contain the error.

Senior

  1. How do you decide whether a link-down event was caused by the link or by the device?

    Model answer

    Look for precursors (correctable errors, Recovery storms) versus an abrupt stop; check whether EIOS preceded idle; read device telemetry for internal resets; check presence and power; correlate with device operations.

  2. Design a validation plan for an SSD's LTSSM.

    Model answer

    Every state and transition entered by every trigger; timeouts verified with a protocol exerciser that withholds or corrupts training sets; directed states; ASPM entry/exit under traffic; L1 substates with clock removal; Hot Reset and fundamental reset loops; speed-change failure fallback; thousands of iterations with error injection; measurement of exit latencies against advertised values.

  3. A customer wants ASPM disabled fleet-wide "because it causes problems". What do you ask and recommend?

    Model answer

    Ask for the evidence (latency, link errors, device incompatibility); quantify the power cost; recommend per-platform or per-device policy, fixing the specific failing combination, and measuring latency with and without it, rather than a blanket rule.

  4. Why is "Recovery count" a poor KPI, and what would you measure?

    Model answer

    It mixes normal and abnormal causes. Measure Recovery entries per cause, time spent in Recovery, correctable error rates, and link-state changes against expectation.

  5. What would you want in a vendor LTSSM debug interface?

    Model answer

    Current substate, a timestamped history of transitions with cause codes, per-lane lock and error counters, equalization results per rate, and counts of ASPM entries/exits with latencies — readable without disturbing the link.

Quiz

  1. After L0 is reached, which exchange must finish before TLPs flow?

    • Equalization
    • SKP ordered sets
    • InitFC1/InitFC2 flow-control initialization
    • PME_Turn_Off
    Answer and reasoning

    DL_Active depends on flow-control initialization.

  2. Which state is always the exit path from L1?

    • Detect
    • Recovery
    • Configuration
    • Polling
    Answer and reasoning

    L1 exit goes through Recovery to L0.

  3. Software sets Secondary Bus Reset at the root port. The SSD sees:

    • TS1 with the Hot Reset bit
    • A PERST# assertion
    • A configuration write
    • An EIOS only
    Answer and reasoning

    Hot Reset is signalled in-band through training sets.

  4. Which Link Status bit indicates an autonomous speed or width change?

    • Link Training
    • Slot Clock Configuration
    • Data Link Layer Link Active
    • Link Autonomous Bandwidth Status
    Answer and reasoning

    LABS (bit 15).

  5. A link returns to Detect about 24 ms after starting Polling, repeatedly. What does that suggest?

    Model answer

    Polling.Active is timing out: lanes fail to achieve lock or the partner is not sending TS (commonly cited 24 ms timeout).

  6. Why can a healthy link log thousands of Recovery entries per hour?

    Model answer

    Each L1 exit goes through Recovery; with ASPM L1 enabled and bursty traffic, that is normal.

  7. Name three causes of Recovery that indicate a problem.

    Model answer

    Receiver/framing errors, replay rollover, L0s exit failure (insufficient FTS).

  8. What evidence separates a device-initiated reset from a signal-integrity link drop?

    Model answer

    No preceding correctable errors, abrupt electrical idle without EIOS, quick clean retraining, and device logs recording a reset.

  9. Why might the SSD's own upstream port show DLLLA = 0 on a working link?

    Model answer

    DL Active reporting is not required on every port; an upstream port may not implement it.

Teach-back

  1. To a child: explain why the walkie-talkie friends say "testing, testing" again after a nap.

    Model explanation

    "While they were quiet, their ears stopped listening closely. Before talking again they do a quick 'testing, testing' so they can hear each other properly — that's quicker than starting the whole game again."

  2. To a junior engineer: explain what happens between PERST# deassertion and the first configuration read.

    Model explanation

    "Both LTSSMs go Detect → Polling → Configuration → L0 at 2.5 GT/s, the Data Link Layers exchange InitFC DLLPs to become DL_Active, then the link goes through Recovery to change speed and equalize. Software must wait the required recovery time before configuration requests; the device may still answer with Request Retry Status if it is not ready."

  3. To a senior engineer: argue how you would triage "the SSD keeps disappearing".

    Model explanation

    "I'd separate never-trains, trains-then-drops, and up-but-retraining. For drops I look for precursors: correctable errors and Recovery causes. Precursors point to the physical path; an abrupt stop without EIOS and no errors points to the device resetting itself, which I confirm in its telemetry. I correlate with workload, temperature, power management and firmware operations, and I pick an experiment — lower speed, ASPM off, device swap — that splits the hypotheses."
