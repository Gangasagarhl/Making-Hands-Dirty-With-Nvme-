The Performance Engineering part taught the equations, the ceiling model and honest benchmarking. This module teaches attribution: taking a latency number or a throughput shortfall and assigning every microsecond and every missing gigabyte to an agent, using trace intervals, in-flight depth timelines and controlled experiments. It goes after the problems that a sweep reveals but cannot explain: tails, regressions, idle-exit penalties and stalls whose owner is not obvious.

Orientation

Performance Engineering gave you latency, IOPS, throughput, Little's law, the PCIe bandwidth derivation, the link-limited IOPS ceiling, a QD 1 latency budget, tail latency and fio methodology. The calculators automate the arithmetic. This module assumes all of that and asks a harder question: who owns this time? You will split command latency into five trace intervals, read in-flight depth from doorbells and completions, distinguish host stalls from device stalls by the order in which gaps appear, and find the hidden per-request limits (Max Read Request Size, outstanding reads, idle-exit latency, thermal management) that make a correct system slow.

  • Performance Engineering: Little's law, ceilings, bandwidth calculations, tail latency, fio method.
  • The Most Important Chapter and End-to-End Write: the TLPs of one Read and one Write.
  • PCIe Tags and Outstanding Requests: the read-request bandwidth-delay product.
  • Power Management: ASPM, APST, exit latencies.
  • Teledyne LeCroy Analyzer Training: what analyzer timestamps measure.

Where it goes next: Validation Engineering turns attributed performance into regression criteria; Automated Forensic Analysis automates interval extraction across captures.

Kid story

A pizza shop promises "your pizza in 20 minutes". One evening a customer waits an hour. Who was slow? The shop keeps a little card for every pizza with five stamps: when the order came in, when the cook picked it up, when it went into the oven, when it came out, and when the delivery rider left. The manager lays out the cards from that evening. Most cards are fine. On the slow card, the gap is between "came out of the oven" and "rider left": the pizza sat on the counter because the only rider was stuck in traffic.

The manager also notices something else. When the oven is broken, the cook stops picking up new orders because the counter is full. When the rider is missing, the cook keeps cooking, and pizzas pile up on the counter. Which pile appears first tells her who started the trouble.

Analogy boundary

What it represents. The stamps are trace timestamps: doorbell, SQE fetch, first data, CQE and MSI-X, and the host's CQ head doorbell. The gaps between them are intervals owned by different agents. The pile that forms first (orders not picked up versus pizzas on the counter) is the order in which submission and completion gaps appear.

What it hides. An analyzer stamps events where the probe sits, not where they happen inside the device or the CPU. The "oven" contains many hidden steps (flash translation lookup, NAND read, error correction, internal queueing) that appear as one interval. And unlike a pizza shop, the host often limits itself: a closed-loop benchmark stops ordering when its own counter is full.

Where it misleads. The story implies that each slow pizza has one cause. Tails are often the sum of two moderate delays. It also implies that the shop sees every card; a capture covers a window of milliseconds to seconds, so rare events may be missing from it entirely, and the one you see may not be typical.

The central concept on the ladder: interval attribution

Five stamps on a pizza card. The longest gap tells you who was slow; which pile grows first tells you who started it.

Split each command's latency into intervals between observable events. Each interval has an owner. Compute percentiles per interval, not just for the total.

I1 covers the doorbell write, the controller's fetch decision and the memory read round trip; I2 the controller's processing and media; I3 the DMA transfer; I4 completion and interrupt generation; I5 interrupt delivery and host completion software.

No specification defines latency targets or intervals. The NVMe Base Specification defines the queue protocol that creates the events (doorbells, SQE fetch, CQE, phase tag, interrupts); the PCI Express Base Specification defines the TLPs and their ordering. Attribution is Derived.

Doorbells (MWr32 to BAR0 + 0x1008 / 0x100C), SQE fetch (MRd64/CplD), data (MWr64 ×16 for 4 KiB at MPS 256), CQE (MWr64 16 B), MSI-X (MWr32 to 0xFEE0_1000, data 0x41).

Pair events by doorbell value, SQE slot, CID and SQ ID. Plot in-flight depth. For each slow command, the dominant interval names the suspect agent.

Confirm with a one-variable experiment that removes the suspect: stop concurrent writes, pin the host thread, disable ASPM, cool the drive. Attribution without an experiment is a hypothesis.

Two limits the part page only touched

Outstanding reads and MRRS

Bwr ≤ Nrd × M / Trt
Bwr
host→device data rate achievable for NVMe Writes, bytes per second
Nrd
memory read requests the device keeps outstanding
M
bytes per read request, at most MRRS
Trt
time from a read request to its last completion, seconds

With Nrd = 64, M = 512 bytes and Trt = 1.5 µs, the bound is ≈ 21.8 GB/s, far above Gen4 x4. With MRRS lowered to 128 bytes, it falls to ≈ 5.5 GB/s, below the ≈ 7 GB/s link ceiling. A platform update that changes MRRS can therefore cost write throughput without changing anything else. Derived; Nrd is Implementation-dependent.

Idle-exit penalties

After an idle gap, the first command may pay several wake-ups at once: CPU C-state exit for the submitting thread, ASPM L1 exit on the link (the doorbell waits in the root port until the link returns to L0), and APST power-state exit inside the controller (Identify Controller's power state descriptors give entry and exit latencies). These are invisible at QD 32 steady state and dominant at QD 1 with think time, the pattern of many interactive and database workloads.

Probe placement changes the intervals. An analyzer at the SSD measures I1 as "doorbell seen → SQE CplD seen", which includes the round trip from the probe into host memory and back. Moving to a switch or adding an IOMMU changes I1 without any change in the SSD. Always state where the probe was.

DRAM-less controllers and HMB. A controller that keeps its mapping table in host memory through the Host Memory Buffer issues its own reads to host memory during I2. On the link these appear as device MRd TLPs that belong to no NVMe data transfer: an I2 that contains host-memory reads is partly a host-memory latency problem. Implementation-dependent.

Ordering facts that make attribution valid. Data MWr TLPs and the CQE MWr are posted writes from the same requester, so the CQE cannot overtake the data under default ordering; if Relaxed Ordering is enabled for data writes, the device must still ensure the CQE is ordered after them (see PCIe Ordering). The MSI-X write follows the CQE. A CQ head doorbell can therefore only be issued by software after it saw the CQE. These rules let you treat interval boundaries as causally ordered. Errors appear here as performance: Nak and replay stretch I3 (see DLLP and Replay); a Completion Timeout stretches I1 or I2 by milliseconds.

Why it is ambiguous. In a closed loop at full depth, the host submits only when completions arrive. If the device stalls, completions stop and then submissions stop. If the host stalls (its completion thread not running), it stops reaping and therefore stops submitting, and the device, with 32 commands outstanding, finishes them and then goes quiet too. At a coarse view both look like "everything stopped". Disambiguation. Look inside the gap at the device's DMA: under a device stall, no data or CQE writes occur during the gap and the gap ends with a burst of device CQE writes. Under a host stall, data and CQE writes continue for up to 32 commands into the first part of the gap, MSI-X writes are sent, and only the CQ head doorbells and new SQ tail doorbells stop. Here, rows 4–6 show nine CQEs written at 1 000.2–1 000.6 ms with their MSI-X, then silence, and the matching CQ head doorbell only at 1 002.050 ms: the host reaped late. A view that plots only doorbells shows a single 2 ms hole and hides this. Conclusion: host stall (medium-high confidence), consistent with a periodic kernel task on the completion CPU; confirm by moving the completion interrupt to an isolated CPU.

Beginner lab: attribute five commands by hand

Objective. Compute I1–I5 and name the owner of the excess in each slow command. Safe lab (paper).

Prerequisites. The architecture figure and protocol table above.

Setup. Synthetic timestamps (µs) for t0, t1′, t2, t3, t4′, t5: A 0, 1.5, 73, 74.3, 74.6, 77; B 0, 1.6, 74, 75.3, 75.6, 1 320; C 0, 290, 362, 363.3, 363.6, 366; D 0, 1.5, 1 940, 1 941.3, 1 941.6, 1 944; E 0, 1.5, 72, 79.5, 79.8, 82.

Procedure. Tabulate the intervals; compare each with A (baseline); name the owner and one likely cause for each excess.

Observations. Each slow command has exactly one dominant excess.

Questions. Which command would a "the SSD is slow" escalation wrongly blame on the device?

Expected reasoning. Excess over baseline, interval by interval.

Solution

B: I5 = 1 244 µs excess, host completion path (preemption, interrupt routing). C: I1 ≈ 288 µs excess, controller wake-up (APST exit) or link exit. D: I2 ≈ 1 867 µs excess, device media or internal queueing (read behind program, GC, throttling). E: I3 = 7.5 µs versus 1.3 µs, link contention, replays or credit starvation. B is the one an "SSD is slow" escalation would wrongly blame; C may be a host power policy choice.

Extension. Add a sixth command where both I2 and I5 are moderately high, and discuss how to report a tail with two contributors.

Intermediate lab: find what drives the p99.9

Objective. From a 50 000-command event log, compute per-interval percentiles and attribute the tail. Safe lab

Prerequisites. Percentiles (Performance Engineering); Python 3.8+.

Setup. The program generates a seeded synthetic log in which rare device stalls and rare host stalls are mixed; it stands in for an analyzer export.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""interval_tail.py - split NVMe command latency into trace intervals and find what drives the tail.
Simulation: a synthetic event log (seeded, reproducible) stands in for an analyzer export."""
import random

INTERVALS = ["doorbell->fetch", "fetch->data", "data span", "CQE->MSI-X", "MSI-X->CQ head"]

def synthetic_log(n=50_000, seed=7):
    rnd = random.Random(seed)
    log = []
    for _ in range(n):
        fetch = max(0.4, rnd.gauss(0.8, 0.1))
        media = max(40.0, rnd.gauss(72.0, 8.0))
        if rnd.random() < 0.002:                 # device: read behind a program/GC event
            media += rnd.uniform(800, 3000)
        span = 1.3
        msix = 0.2
        host = max(0.8, rnd.gauss(2.0, 0.4))
        if rnd.random() < 0.0015:                # host: completion thread preempted
            host += rnd.uniform(400, 2000)
        log.append([fetch, media, span, msix, host])
    return log

def pct(values, p):
    v = sorted(values)
    return v[min(len(v) - 1, int(p / 100 * len(v)))]

if __name__ == "__main__":
    log = synthetic_log()
    totals = [sum(c) for c in log]
    print(f"{'interval':<16}{'p50':>8}{'p99':>9}{'p99.9':>9}   (microseconds)")
    for i, name in enumerate(INTERVALS):
        col = [c[i] for c in log]
        print(f"{name:<16}{pct(col, 50):>8.1f}{pct(col, 99):>9.1f}{pct(col, 99.9):>9.1f}")
    print(f"{'total':<16}{pct(totals, 50):>8.1f}{pct(totals, 99):>9.1f}{pct(totals, 99.9):>9.1f}")
    cut = pct(totals, 99.9)
    slow = [c for c, t in zip(log, totals) if t >= cut]
    blame = {name: 0 for name in INTERVALS}
    for c in slow:
        blame[INTERVALS[c.index(max(c))]] += 1
    print(f"\n{len(slow)} commands at or above p99.9 ({cut:.0f} us); largest interval in each:")
    for name, k in blame.items():
        if k:
            print(f"  {name:<16}{k:>4}  ({100 * k / len(slow):.0f}%)")

Procedure. Run it. Then double the host-stall probability and rerun; then remove device stalls entirely.

Observations. Expected output:

interval             p50      p99    p99.9   (microseconds)
doorbell->fetch      0.8      1.0      1.1
fetch->data         72.0     91.3   1868.4
data span            1.3      1.3      1.3
CQE->MSI-X           0.2      0.2      0.2
MSI-X->CQ head       2.0      3.0   1220.0
total               76.3     96.3   1964.7

50 commands at or above p99.9 (1965 us); largest interval in each:
  fetch->data       44  (88%)
  MSI-X->CQ head     6  (12%)

Questions. (a) Why is the p99 clean while the p99.9 is 25 times the median? (b) Host stalls happen at 0.15%, nearly as often as device stalls at 0.2%; why do they own only 12% of the tail? (c) What would you report?

Expected reasoning. Rarity decides which percentile an event reaches; magnitude decides who owns that percentile.

Solution

(a) Both stall types affect fewer than 1% of commands, so they cannot reach p99 but dominate p99.9. (b) Their magnitudes are smaller (400–2 000 µs versus 800–3 000 µs), so among the slowest 0.1% device stalls win more often. (c) "p99.9 = 1.96 ms; 88% of commands above it are dominated by the device's fetch-to-data interval (media or internal queueing), 12% by host completion; device stalls are the primary target, host stalls the secondary." Then design the one-variable experiments for each. Real logs need pairing and clock care; the simulation skips both.

Extension. Add in-flight depth: simulate a closed loop and plot depth to reproduce the ambiguous trace's "who went quiet first" test.

Advanced lab: interrupts versus polling, measured in cycles

Objective. Measure latency and CPU cycles per I/O for interrupt-driven and polled completion on the same device. Caution lab: reloading the nvme driver with poll queues briefly removes the device; use a test system where no filesystem on that SSD is mounted. All I/O is read-only.

Prerequisites. fio with io_uring; root; the Linux view commands.

Setup. Configure the nvme driver with a few poll queues (module parameter poll_queues; how you set it depends on whether nvme is built in or a module on your distribution).

# Read-only QD 1 jobs, one CPU; run each while perf counts cycles on that CPU
sudo fio --name=irq  --filename=/dev/nvme0n1 --readonly --direct=1 --ioengine=io_uring \
         --rw=randread --bs=4k --iodepth=1 --cpus_allowed=2 --runtime=30 --time_based
sudo fio --name=poll --filename=/dev/nvme0n1 --readonly --direct=1 --ioengine=io_uring \
         --hipri=1 --rw=randread --bs=4k --iodepth=1 --cpus_allowed=2 --runtime=30 --time_based
sudo perf stat -C 2 -e cycles -- sleep 30     # run alongside each job

Procedure. Record IOPS, mean and p99 latency, and cycles on CPU 2 for each job. Compute cycles per I/O.

Observations. Polling usually cuts mean latency by a few microseconds and uses 100% of the core regardless of IOPS. Implementation-dependent: magnitudes vary widely.

Questions. (a) Which interval does polling shorten? (b) Why can cycles per I/O be higher with polling at QD 1? (c) When is polling worth it?

Expected reasoning. Polling trades CPU for I5.

Solution

(a) I5 and the interrupt part of I4: no MSI-X wait, no handler. (b) The core spins at full speed while waiting ~75 µs for media; cycles per I/O include all that spinning. (c) When media latency is low (fast storage-class media or cache hits), so that I5 is a large share of total latency, and when a core can be dedicated.

Extension. Repeat at QD 32 with 4 jobs and compare: polling's cost per I/O falls as useful work fills the spin time.

Senior forensic lab: the 18% write regression

Objective. Attribute a regression to one changed variable, with evidence and a confirming experiment. Safe lab (paper exercise on synthetic evidence).

Prerequisites. The outstanding-reads formula above; Chapter 13.

Setup: evidence (synthetic).

  • 128 KiB sequential write, QD 32: 6.8 GB/s before a platform firmware update, 5.6 GB/s after. Reads unchanged at 7.0 GB/s.
  • LnkSta: 16 GT/s x4 before and after. MaxPayload 256 bytes before and after. MaxReadReq: 512 bytes before, 128 bytes after.
  • Capture after the update: device MRd64 TLPs of 32 DW (128 B); at most 64 outstanding; round trip MRd to last CplD ≈ 1.45 µs. No replays, no Naks, credits never exhausted.
  • CPU utilisation, IOMMU mode, drive firmware and temperature unchanged.

Procedure. List hypotheses for "writes slower, reads unchanged"; use the formula to test the leading one; design the confirming experiment.

Observations. Which variables changed, and which can affect only writes?

Questions. (a) Does the formula predict 5.6 GB/s? (b) Why are reads unaffected? (c) What experiment confirms?

Expected reasoning. Writes are pulled by device reads; MRRS limits those, not the device's data writes.

Solution

(a) 64 × 128 B / 1.45 µs ≈ 5.65 GB/s: yes, within measurement error. Per-request overhead also rises (four times as many MRd TLPs), a smaller effect on the host→device direction, which carries the completions. (b) Reads are carried by device MWr TLPs limited by MPS (unchanged), not by MRRS. (c) Set MRRS back to 512 on a test system (setpci or a platform setting; a Caution operation) and rerun: throughput returns to ≈ 6.8 GB/s. Confidence high after the experiment. Report the platform setting as the root cause, add MRRS to the configuration record of every performance run, and add a regression check on lspci MaxReadReq.

Extension. Estimate what MRRS 128 would cost on a Gen5 x4 link at the same round trip, and whether raising outstanding reads in the device could compensate.

Interview

Beginner

  1. What are the five link-visible intervals of an NVMe Read?

    Model answer

    Doorbell → SQE completion, SQE → first data, first → last data, last data → MSI-X, MSI-X → CQ head doorbell.

  2. Which interval does the host own most directly?

    Model answer

    MSI-X to CQ head doorbell: interrupt delivery and completion software.

  3. Why look at percentiles per interval?

    Model answer

    The total's tail can come from different intervals with different owners; per-interval percentiles show which.

  4. What is in-flight depth and how do you get it from a trace?

    Model answer

    Commands submitted but not completed; count doorbell-advanced slots minus CQEs over time.

  5. Why does the first command after idle often take longer?

    Model answer

    Link L1 exit, controller power-state exit and CPU wake-up add latency.

Intermediate

  1. Why can MRRS affect writes but not reads?

    Model answer

    Write data is pulled by device MRd requests limited by MRRS; read data is pushed with MWr limited by MPS.

  2. What does interrupt coalescing trade?

    Model answer

    Fewer interrupts and CPU cycles per I/O for added latency, up to the aggregation time.

  3. How do you tell a host stall from a device stall in a closed loop?

    Model answer

    Device stall: data and CQEs stop first. Host stall: CQEs and MSI-X continue for outstanding commands; head and tail doorbells stop first.

  4. Where would thermal throttling show in the intervals?

    Model answer

    Mostly in I2 (media and processing) and in slower pacing of write-data fetches; I1 and I3 stay normal.

  5. Why measure cycles per I/O rather than CPU utilisation?

    Model answer

    Utilisation depends on IOPS; cycles per I/O is a cost per unit of work and compares configurations fairly.

Advanced

  1. Derive the write bandwidth limit from outstanding reads.

    Model answer

    B ≤ N × M / T: N outstanding requests of M bytes each, renewed every round trip T.

  2. Why is an analyzer's I1 not the controller's fetch latency?

    Model answer

    It includes probe-to-root-complex propagation, memory read time and return; the probe sees events at one point.

  3. How would HMB show up in a trace?

    Model answer

    Device MRd TLPs to the HMB region during I2 that do not belong to data transfers; higher I2 when host memory is slow or busy.

  4. How many runs do you need to claim a 3% regression?

    Model answer

    Enough that the run-to-run spread (for example standard deviation of the mean) is well below 3%; measure the variance first, then choose the count, and report the confidence interval.

  5. Why can a capture miss the cause of a p99.99 event?

    Model answer

    The window covers a short time; rare events may not occur in it. Use triggers on slow completions, or long low-detail host tracing to find when to capture.

Senior

  1. A customer reports "your SSD has 5 ms tails". What do you ask for first?

    Model answer

    Workload, arrival model, depth, percentiles with counts, temperature, power settings, and host-side intervals; ideally a capture with a slow-completion trigger. Then attribute before accepting ownership.

  2. How do you design performance acceptance criteria?

    Model answer

    Distributions (p50/p99/p99.9) at defined workloads, steady state, thermal conditions, sample counts, run-to-run tolerance, and per-interval bounds where a trace is available.

  3. When is a performance regression not a bug?

    Model answer

    When it is the intended effect of a trade-off (power, safety such as strict IOMMU, thermal protection); then document it and offer the configuration choice.

  4. How do you avoid fooling yourself with one good run?

    Model answer

    Pre-register the question and analysis, repeat runs, randomise run order, keep a control configuration, and explain the result with the ceiling model.

  5. What belongs in every performance report's configuration block?

    Model answer

    Drive model, firmware, fill state, temperature; link speed, width, MPS, MRRS, ASPM; CPU model, NUMA placement, C-state policy; kernel, IOMMU mode, scheduler, queue counts; tool versions and job files.

Quiz

  1. A slow command's excess is entirely between MSI-X and CQ head doorbell. Owner:

    • NAND
    • The link
    • Host interrupt and completion path
    • Controller fetch engine
    Answer and reasoning

    The device's work ended at MSI-X.

  2. MRRS drops from 512 to 128 bytes. Most affected:

    • Sequential reads
    • Sequential writes
    • Admin commands
    • MSI-X latency
    Answer and reasoning

    Write data is fetched with read requests sized by MRRS.

  3. In a closed loop, CQEs continue for a while but doorbells stop. Most likely:

    • Host stall
    • Device stall
    • Link down
    • Thermal throttling
    Answer and reasoning

    The device keeps completing; the host is not reaping or submitting.

  4. Which feature trades latency for fewer interrupts?

    • APST
    • Power Management
    • Temperature Threshold
    • Interrupt Coalescing (08h)
    Answer and reasoning

    Aggregation threshold and time delay the interrupt.

  5. A long I1 after an idle gap with ordered sets before the doorbell suggests:

    • Media read retry
    • Link exit from L1
    • Wrong PRP
    • CQ full
    Answer and reasoning

    The link must retrain to L0 before the doorbell can cross.

  6. Events affecting 0.2% of commands can dominate which percentile?

    • p50
    • p99
    • p99.9
    • None
    Answer and reasoning

    Fewer than 1% cannot move p99; 0.2% exceeds the 0.1% tail.

  7. Polling primarily shortens:

    • I1
    • I2
    • I3
    • I5
    Answer and reasoning

    No interrupt wait; the poller sees the CQE immediately.

  8. Open: why must probe placement be stated in a latency report?

    Model answer

    Intervals include propagation between the probe and each agent; a different placement changes them without any device change.

  9. Open: give a one-variable experiment for "tails appear after 15 minutes".

    Model answer

    Repeat with added cooling and everything else fixed; or repeat on a preconditioned drive at constant temperature to separate thermal from garbage-collection effects.

Teach-back

  1. To a child: explain the pizza cards and the piles.

    Model explanation

    "Each pizza gets five stamps. The biggest gap between stamps shows who was slow. If pizzas pile up on the counter, the rider is late; if orders pile up, the oven is broken."

  2. To a junior engineer: explain how to find the owner of a p99.9.

    Model explanation

    "Export the trace, pair each command's doorbell, SQE fetch, data, CQE, MSI-X and head doorbell, compute five intervals, take percentiles per interval, and see which interval dominates the slowest commands. Then remove that suspect in an experiment and check the tail moves."

  3. To a senior engineer: argue why a regression report needs per-interval evidence and an experiment, not just before/after numbers.

    Model explanation

    "Before/after numbers show that something changed, not what. Intervals name an owner; a one-variable experiment proves causation; variance tells us the change is real. Without all three, we risk fixing the wrong component and shipping the regression."
