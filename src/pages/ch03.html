Flash made storage fast, and then the interface made it slow again. This chapter tells how the industry discovered that the protocols built for spinning disks were wasting most of what solid-state drives could do, and how NVM Express (NVMe) was designed from a clean sheet to fix it. It ends with the full history from SATA to the NVMe 2.4 specification set of August 2026.

Why this chapter exists

Every major design choice in NVMe is a reaction to a specific pain in what came before. Many queues: because there was one. Deep queues: because there were 32 slots. Doorbells written, never read: because reading registers was expensive. Commands fetched by the device in one go: because the old way needed several round trips. Interrupt vectors per queue: because one shared interrupt forced all CPUs through one bottleneck. If you know the pain, you never have to memorize the cure.

This chapter is also your first contact with the idea of protocol overhead: the work that is not moving data but is needed to agree on what data to move. Measuring and reducing that overhead is a recurring theme of the whole course.

Learning objectives

  • Describe how a SATA command is issued and completed through an AHCI host controller.
  • Identify the specific limits of SATA/AHCI: one queue, 32 commands, serialized issue, register reads, a single interrupt path, a fixed-rate half-duplex link.
  • Explain why CPU overhead per command became significant once device latency fell to tens of microseconds.
  • Explain how attaching storage directly to PCIe changed the performance ceiling.
  • State NVMe's design goals and connect each one to a limitation it removed.
  • Narrate the history SATA → AHCI → PCIe SSDs → NVMe → NVMe 1.x → NVMe 2.0 family → NVMe 2.4.
  • Compare an AHCI command and an NVMe command at the level of register and memory transactions.

The story

In the old days, the town reached the warehouse through a little post office. The post office had one counter and a rack with 32 pigeonholes. Every worker in town who wanted something had to queue at that one counter. The postmaster would let you in, you would write your request on a form, put the form in an empty pigeonhole, and flip a little flag to say "pigeonhole 7 is ready." Only one person could use the counter at a time, so everyone else waited outside.

When the warehouse answered, the postmaster rang one big bell that the whole town could hear. Then somebody had to walk to the counter and ask: "Which pigeonholes are done?" The postmaster would check and tell them. Asking took a long time, because the postmaster always had to go and look.

This was fine when the warehouse was a slow old carousel taking ages for every page. Nobody minded a few minutes at the post office if the answer took a week anyway.

But the new warehouse answered almost instantly, and it had dozens of clerks who could all work at once. Now the post office was the slow part. The clerks sat idle while the town queued at one counter.

So the town built something new. It gave every worker their own request box and their own answer box, hundreds of slots deep, in their own office. To send work, a worker drops slips in their box and rings their own little bell at the warehouse; they never have to ask the warehouse anything. The warehouse's clerks come and collect the slips themselves, and write answers straight into each worker's answer box, tapping only that worker on the shoulder. No counter, no queue outside, no asking "which ones are done?" The new arrangement needed a new language too, written for the new warehouse rather than the old carousel. They called it NVM Express.

An old restaurant has one waiter for the whole dining room and a ticket rail with 32 clips. Every table's order goes through that waiter, one at a time. When food is ready, a single bell rings; the waiter has to walk to the pass and ask the chef which tickets are ready. A modern kitchen gives each section its own ticket printer and pickup window. Orders print directly at the station that will cook them, cooks pull tickets themselves, and finished plates go to the window for that section, with a pager buzzing only that section's runner.

What the analogies hide. In real AHCI, the "postmaster" is a host bus adapter chip, and it is not slow at thinking; the cost is that every question the CPU asks it is a register read across a bus, which stalls the CPU until the answer comes back. The "32 pigeonholes" are per SATA port, not per computer. And the NVMe "worker" is usually a CPU core, not a program: many programs share their core's queue.

Where they mislead. The story makes the old system sound foolish. It was not: AHCI was a good design for disks. A disk took milliseconds per request, so a few microseconds of overhead and 32 commands were plenty. The design became wrong only when the device became a thousand times faster. Good engineers judge designs against the problem they were built for.

Beginner explanation

To read from a SATA SSD, the computer's driver talks to a host controller on the motherboard (an AHCI controller). The driver fills in a command in memory, then tells the host controller which slot it used; the host controller turns it into SATA frames and sends them down a cable to the drive. When the drive finishes, the host controller interrupts the CPU, and the driver has to ask the host controller which commands finished.

That arrangement has three problems for a fast SSD:

  1. Too few commands in flight. One queue with 32 slots per drive. An SSD with dozens of flash dies needs more to stay busy.
  2. Too much overhead per command. Issuing and completing each command needs several slow register accesses, plus a lock so CPUs don't collide. At hundreds of thousands of commands per second, that overhead becomes a whole CPU core or more.
  3. Too narrow a pipe. SATA's link tops out around 600 MB/s.

NVMe fixes all three: up to 65,535 I/O queues of up to 65,536 entries each; one cheap register write (a doorbell) to submit, no register reads to complete; and the drive sits directly on PCIe, whose bandwidth scales with lanes and generations. The drive's own controller speaks NVMe; there is no host controller in between.

Engineering explanation

Limitations of SATA

SATA, introduced in the early 2000s as the serial successor to parallel ATA, was an excellent disk interface. For SSDs, its limits are structural:

AHCI

The Advanced Host Controller Interface (AHCI), published by Intel in 2004, standardized how operating systems talk to SATA host controllers, so that one driver could work with any compliant controller. The host controller (host bus adapter, HBA) is itself a PCI/PCIe function whose registers are mapped through a BAR (the AHCI Base Address, often called ABAR). Its design, in outline:

  • Per port (one port per SATA drive): a command list in host memory with 32 command headers (slots); a received-FIS area where the HBA deposits frames from the drive; and per-port registers including Command Issue (PxCI), SATA Active (PxSACT, used for NCQ) and Interrupt Status (PxIS).
  • Each command header points to a separate command table in host memory, which holds the command frame (a Register Host-to-Device FIS carrying the ATA command) and a Physical Region Descriptor Table listing the data buffers.
  • To issue a command, software builds the command table and header, sets the slot's bit in PxSACT for queued commands, then sets the slot's bit in PxCI.
  • On completion, the drive sends a frame back; the HBA updates the received-FIS area and clears bits in PxSACT/PxCI, sets status bits, and raises an interrupt. The driver reads interrupt status registers to learn why it was interrupted, reads PxSACT or PxCI to learn which slots finished, and writes PxIS to clear the status.

AHCI is defined in Intel's Serial ATA Advanced Host Controller Interface (AHCI) specification, revision 1.3.x. Register names above are AHCI's; this course does not teach AHCI bit layouts, because you need AHCI only as the "before" picture.

Command serialization

An AHCI port has one command list. If sixteen CPU cores all want to submit commands to the same drive, they must take turns: each must find a free slot, fill it, and set its bit, without another core claiming the same slot. That requires a lock around submission. Completion handling is similarly funneled: one port, one interrupt status, one place to learn which slots finished. Work that must happen one-at-a-time does not get faster when you add cores. In the language of parallel computing, it is the serial fraction that caps scaling.

ATA adds another form of serialization: non-queued commands. Many ATA commands (some management and cache commands, for example) cannot be queued with NCQ commands; the driver must wait until all outstanding queued commands finish, issue the non-queued command alone, and wait again. A single such command drains the whole queue.

Queue limitations

Thirty-two commands per drive was generous for a disk, where even 32 outstanding requests mainly help the drive reorder seeks. For an SSD with 32 to 64 or more independent flash units, it is too few: by Little's Law, with 80 µs per read, 32 outstanding commands can give at most 32 / 0.00008 = 400,000 IOPS even with perfect parallelism, and in practice some slots are always in transit (being issued, being completed), so the device sees fewer. There is also only one queue, so there is no way to separate, say, latency-sensitive database reads from a background backup's large writes: they all wait in the same 32 slots.

CPU overhead

Each AHCI command involves several register accesses to the HBA. Writes are posted and cheap; reads are not: every register read is a non-posted transaction, and the core stalls until the value returns. Suppose each uncached register read costs about 1 µs of stalled core time (a round trip through the root complex to a device and back). At 4 reads per command, 250,000 commands per second would cost 1 second of stalled core time every second: a full core doing nothing but waiting. NVMe's fast path needs zero reads: commands are fetched by the device, completions are written by the device into host memory, where the CPU reads them from cache-coherent DRAM in tens of nanoseconds.

Latency

When a disk took 10 ms, a storage stack overhead of 20 µs was 0.2% of the total. When an SSD takes 80 µs, the same 20 µs is 25%. Flash turned software and protocol overhead from a rounding error into a major share of latency. Every layer had to be re-examined: the host controller hop, register reads, interrupt handling, locks, even the block layer's design (Linux's multi-queue block layer, blk-mq, grew up alongside NVMe for exactly this reason).

Parallelism

Two kinds of parallelism needed serving at once:

  • Device parallelism: dozens of dies and planes (Chapter 2) need many outstanding commands.
  • Host parallelism: servers went multi-core; each core needs to submit and complete I/O without contending with the others, ideally entirely within its own caches.

NVMe's answer is the queue pair: a submission queue and a completion queue, typically one pair per CPU core, each with its own doorbells and its own MSI-X vector routed to that core. A core submits to its own queue without locks, the device processes all queues in parallel (with an arbitration policy between them), and completions return to the submitting core's completion queue and interrupt. The reference machine has 32 I/O queue pairs for exactly this reason.

PCIe

The other half of the answer was to attach the SSD directly to PCI Express (PCIe). PCIe offered what SATA could not:

  • Bandwidth that scales: per lane, roughly doubling each generation (2.5, 5, 8, 16, 32, 64 GT/s for Gen1 to Gen6, with Gen7 at 128 GT/s), and links of 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16 lanes. A Gen4 x4 SSD has about 13 times SATA's bandwidth. (Chapter 6 covers generations precisely.)
  • Full duplex: each lane carries data both ways at once.
  • Direct attachment: the link runs from the CPU's root complex to the drive, with no host bus adapter in between.
  • Native DMA and MSI-X: any PCIe endpoint can be a bus master and can have up to 2048 interrupt vectors.
  • Memory-mapped registers: doorbells are just addresses; writes are posted.

But PCIe is only a transport. It carries memory reads and writes; it does not say what a storage command is. Early PCIe SSDs (late 2000s) each invented their own register interface and needed vendor-specific drivers. That fragmentation (no in-box drivers, no standard boot support, every vendor re-solving the same problems) was the final push toward a standard: a common register and command interface for PCIe SSDs. Competing proposals also existed, notably SCSI over PCIe (SOP) with the PCIe Queuing Interface (PQI), backed by the SCSI community; NVMe became the dominant one.

"PCIe SSD" and "NVMe SSD" are not synonyms. A PCIe SSD could speak a proprietary protocol or AHCI over PCIe (some early M.2 drives did). An NVMe device could be reached over TCP or RDMA instead of PCIe (NVMe over Fabrics). This course always names both: NVMe semantics, PCIe transport, joined by the NVMe over PCIe Transport binding.

NVMe design goals

NVMe was designed by an industry work group (originally under the name NVMHCI, Non-Volatile Memory Host Controller Interface) for non-volatile memory from the start. Each goal maps to a pain point:

The NVMe I/O path deliberately has very few moving parts: a ring of 64-byte commands, a ring of 16-byte completions, two doorbells and an interrupt vector. Fewer steps means fewer cycles, fewer cache misses and fewer places for bugs. When you read the NVMe Base Specification and find hundreds of pages, remember that almost all of it is administration, features and error handling; the hot path fits on one page.

The central concept, climbed: the queue pair

Every worker gets their own request box and answer box, and their own bell. No queuing at a shared counter, and no need to ask "which ones are done?": answers appear in your own box.

An NVMe queue pair is a submission queue (SQ) of commands written by the host and a completion queue (CQ) of results written by the controller, both circular buffers in host memory, plus a tail doorbell for the SQ and a head doorbell for the CQ. Each CPU core typically has its own pair.

The queues live in host DRAM (on the reference machine, SQ1 at 0x1_2340_0000 with 1024 × 64-byte entries, CQ1 at 0x1_2341_0000 with 1024 × 16-byte entries). Doorbells are 32-bit registers in the controller's BAR0 starting at offset 1000h. The controller has DMA engines that fetch SQ entries and write CQ entries, and an MSI-X table with a vector per CQ.

The NVMe Base Specification defines queues, entry formats, doorbell registers and the rules for head and tail pointers, and the Create I/O Submission Queue (opcode 01h) and Create I/O Completion Queue (opcode 05h) admin commands. The NVMe over PCIe Transport Specification defines how the queues are placed in host memory and how doorbells and MSI-X are used over PCIe. NVM Express Base Specification 2.4 — Queue Mechanism

Submission: host memory write of the entry (not on the link), then one posted Memory Write for the doorbell. Execution: device Memory Read(s) of entries, data transfers. Completion: device Memory Write of the 16-byte entry, device Memory Write of the MSI-X message, later a posted Memory Write for the CQ head doorbell.

In a trace you can separate queues by address: doorbell writes to BAR0 + 0x1008 belong to SQ1, + 0x1010 to SQ2, and so on; fetches from 0x1_2340_xxxx belong to SQ1; completion writes into 0x1_2341_xxxx belong to CQ1. Commands from different queues interleave freely on the link.

Per-queue isolation helps debugging: if only one queue's commands time out, suspect that queue's setup (wrong base address, wrong size, wrong interrupt vector or affinity) rather than the whole device. If all queues stall at once, suspect the controller or the link.

A readable history: SATA to NVMe 2.4

The history is short, by the standards of computing, and it reads like a chase: storage media got faster, the interface became the bottleneck, and the interface was rebuilt. Dates are given to the year where well established; rows marked Uncertain should be confirmed against the original announcements before you quote them.

  1. The disk era (1980s–2000s). Parallel ATA (IDE) connects disks inside PCs; SCSI serves servers. Both are designed around mechanical latency: milliseconds per request, modest queues.
  2. Going serial (early 2000s). Serial ATA 1.0 (1.5 Gb/s) replaces the wide ATA ribbon cable with a thin point-to-point link; products appear around 2003. Serial Attached SCSI follows for the enterprise. In the same period, PCI Express 1.0 (2.5 GT/s per lane) replaces the parallel PCI bus.
  3. A standard host controller (2004). Intel publishes AHCI, giving SATA host controllers a common register interface and native command queuing support: one command list of 32 slots per port. SATA moves to 3 Gb/s.
  4. Flash arrives (mid to late 2000s). SATA SSDs appear, pretending to be disks. They are immediately limited by the interface. PCIe 2.0 (5 GT/s, 2007) and SATA 6 Gb/s (2009) arrive; SATA will go no faster.
  5. The wild west of PCIe SSDs (late 2000s). Vendors put flash directly on PCIe cards for performance, each with a proprietary interface and driver. Fast, but every product needs its own driver and none can boot easily.
  6. The standard is born (2009–2011). An industry work group, first under the NVMHCI name, designs a new interface for PCIe-attached non-volatile memory. Uncertain on the exact formation date. NVM Express 1.0 is published in March 2011. PCIe 3.0 (8 GT/s, 128b/130b encoding) arrives in 2010.
  7. NVMe 1.x grows up (2012–2019). 1.1 (2012) adds features such as multi-path and autonomous power transitions; the Linux kernel gains an in-box NVMe driver (2012). 1.2 (2014) adds namespace management, the Host Memory Buffer and more. NVMe-MI (management interface) appears in 2015. NVMe over Fabrics 1.0 (2016) carries NVMe commands over networks. 1.3 (2017) adds features such as sanitize and virtualization support; 1.4 (2019) adds features such as NVM Sets, Endurance Groups and Predictable Latency Mode. PCIe 4.0 (16 GT/s, 2017) and 5.0 (32 GT/s, 2019) double bandwidth twice. M.2 and U.2 form factors make NVMe ubiquitous.
  8. The 2.0 family restructures (2021). NVMe 2.0 splits one monolithic specification into a Base specification, separate command set specifications (NVM, Zoned Namespaces, Key Value) and separate transport specifications (PCIe, RDMA, TCP), alongside NVMe-MI and a new NVMe Boot specification. This is the structure you will study. PCIe 6.0 (64 GT/s, PAM4 signaling and FLIT mode) follows in early 2022.
  9. New command sets (2024 onward). NVMe 2.1 (2024) brings new specifications including Computational Programs and Subsystem Local Memory. Uncertain on which features landed in which intermediate 2.x revision; check the Revision Changes document. PCIe 7.0 (128 GT/s) is released in 2025.
  10. NVMe 2.4 (August 2026), the course baseline. NVMe Base 2.4 is ratified on 2026-07-31 and the whole set of eleven documents is published together on 2026-08-04. Highlights: post-quantum-ready key exchange, PCIe Exported NVM Subsystem Migration for live migration of virtual SSDs, a TDISP information package, persistent and virtualization-aware lockdown, rate limiting of bandwidth and IOPS between controllers, voltage monitoring, idle I/O exit latency limits, and a manufacturing-default reset. See 2026 NVMe 2.4 Updates.

Hardware view

The move to NVMe changed what is physically on the motherboard and in the drive bay. With SATA, the host controller is a chip (or a block inside the platform chipset), and drives connect to it by SATA cables. With NVMe, the drive's controller connects straight to PCIe lanes from the CPU or chipset; the connector is just a PCIe connector in a storage-friendly shape.

Reasoning. The symptom is a 30-second timeout on queue 2 and the visible anomaly is a doorbell (row 11) with no fetch. A beginner would mark row 11 and start blaming the controller's fetch engine. But reading top to bottom, the first deviation from expected behavior is row 9: the controller refused to create SQ2, with a command-specific error status. The requested size, 4096 entries, is the clue to check: if the controller's CAP.MQES reports a smaller maximum, the request is invalid. Everything after row 9 is a consequence of the driver ignoring that status: the doorbell in row 11 targets a queue that does not exist, so no fetch can follow (the controller may also report the bad doorbell through an asynchronous event, if the host has one outstanding; confirm in the specification). Queue 1's normal traffic proves the link and controller are healthy. Root cause: a driver bug (unchecked admin completion status, combined with a queue size above the controller's limit). Fix: check every admin completion's status, and size queues from CAP.MQES.

The first abnormal event can be far earlier than the symptom: here, two milliseconds and an entirely different queue. Good trace analysts capture enough history before the failure (a deep pre-trigger buffer) to see it.

Code / pseudocode

Issue paths side by side

/* AHCI: one port, shared by every CPU */
void ahci_issue(port *p, request *r)
{
    lock(&p->lock);                          /* every core contends here */
    int slot = find_free_slot(p);            /* one of 32 */
    build_cmd_table(p, slot, r);             /* FIS + PRDT in host memory */
    build_cmd_header(p, slot);               /* points at the table */
    mmio_write32(p->PxSACT, 1u << slot);
    mmio_write32(p->PxCI,   1u << slot);
    unlock(&p->lock);
}

/* NVMe: each core owns its queue; no lock on the fast path */
void nvme_issue(queue *q, request *r)       /* q: the calling CPU's own queue */
{
    sqe *e = &q->sq[q->tail];
    build_sqe(e, r);                         /* 64 bytes, everything in one place */
    q->tail = (q->tail + 1) % q->depth;
    wmb();                                   /* entry visible before doorbell */
    mmio_write32(q->sq_doorbell, q->tail);   /* one posted write */
}

Where do IOPS stop scaling?

This model computes four ceilings for each interface (device parallelism by Little's Law, CPU work that spreads across cores, work serialized under one lock, and link bandwidth) and reports the smallest. The constants are illustrative teaching values, not measurements of any product.


"""queue_model.py - a back-of-the-envelope model of where IOPS stop scaling
for a SATA/AHCI-style interface versus an NVMe-style interface.
Every constant is an illustrative teaching value, not a measurement."""

BLOCK = 4096                 # bytes per command (4 KiB random reads)
DIES = 64                    # independent flash units inside the SSD
T_MEDIA = 80e-6              # seconds per flash read (one unit busy per command)

INTERFACES = {
    #            max in flight, CPU s/cmd, serialized s/cmd, link bytes/s
    "AHCI/SATA": (32,           6.0e-6,    3.0e-6,           600e6),
    "NVMe/PCIe": (65535 * 65536, 2.0e-6,   0.0,              7.88e9),  # queues x entries
}


def limits(name, cores, qd_per_core):
    max_inflight, cpu_per_cmd, serial_per_cmd, link = INTERFACES[name]
    inflight = min(cores * qd_per_core, max_inflight)
    return {
        "device": min(inflight, DIES) / T_MEDIA,       # Little's Law, capped by parallelism
        "cpu": cores / cpu_per_cmd,                     # work that spreads across cores
        "lock": 1 / serial_per_cmd if serial_per_cmd else float("inf"),  # one at a time
        "link": link / BLOCK,                           # bytes per second / bytes per command
    }


def k(x):
    return "   inf" if x == float("inf") else f"{x / 1000:6.0f}"


print("thousands of IOPS each limit allows; the smallest one wins")
print(f"{'interface':<10} {'cores':>5} {'QD/core':>7} | {'device':>6} {'cpu':>6} {'lock':>6} {'link':>6} | result")
for name in INTERFACES:
    for cores, qd in ((1, 1), (1, 32), (4, 32), (16, 32)):
        lim = limits(name, cores, qd)
        worst = min(lim, key=lim.get)
        print(f"{name:<10} {cores:>5} {qd:>7} | " + " ".join(k(lim[x]) for x in lim)
              + f" | {lim[worst] / 1000:,.0f}k ({worst})")

Read the AHCI rows from right to left. The SATA link binds first at about 146k IOPS. If you could magically speed up the link, the lock would bind at 333k; remove the lock and the 32-command limit would cap device parallelism at 400k. Every layer of the old design is a ceiling. For NVMe, the device itself is the limit: exactly where you want it. At queue depth 1, both interfaces are equally limited by media latency: NVMe does not make a single flash read faster.

Worked example

A storage server must deliver 1,000,000 random 4 KiB reads per second. Work out what each interface would need.

With SATA SSDs behind AHCI

  • Link: each SATA drive carries at most ≈ 146,000 4 KiB IOPS (600 MB/s ÷ 4096 B). Needed drives: 1,000,000 ÷ 146,000 ≈ 7, in practice 8 or more.
  • Queues: 8 ports × 32 commands = 256 slots in total. By Little's Law at 100 µs latency, 1,000,000 × 0.0001 = 100 commands must be in flight on average: possible, but each port's 32 slots must stay fairly full.
  • CPU: assume several uncached register reads per command at about 1 µs each, say 3 µs of stalled time per command. 1,000,000 × 3 µs = 3 seconds of stalled core time per second: three cores doing nothing but waiting, on top of the real driver work, plus lock contention on each port. Derived from the assumptions stated.

With NVMe SSDs on Gen4 x4

  • Link: each drive's link could carry ≈ 1.9 million 4 KiB IOPS (7.88 GB/s ÷ 4096 B); the device's internals are the real limit. Two or three drives rated in the hundreds of thousands of IOPS would do.
  • Queues: 32 queues per drive × 1024 entries each on the reference machine, far more than the ≈ 80 in flight needed at 80 µs latency (1,000,000 × 0.00008).
  • CPU: zero register reads; one doorbell write per batch of submissions and one per batch of completions, posted. No cross-core lock on the fast path.

The lesson is not "SATA is bad". It is that every per-command cost gets multiplied by the command rate, and at a million commands per second, microseconds become CPU cores.

Common mistakes

Misconceptions about the move to NVMe
MistakeCorrection
"NVMe is faster because it uses a faster flash."The same flash can sit behind SATA or NVMe. NVMe removes interface and software overhead and allows parallelism.
"NVMe and PCIe are the same thing."NVMe is the storage protocol; PCIe is one transport; the NVMe over PCIe Transport specification binds them. NVMe also runs over TCP and RDMA.
"An M.2 SSD is an NVMe SSD."M.2 is a form factor; M.2 drives can be SATA or NVMe.
"NVMe lowers latency for every workload."At queue depth 1 the flash read dominates. NVMe's biggest wins are at high concurrency and in CPU efficiency.
"More queues are always better."Queues cost memory and interrupt vectors; one per core is typical. More than the controller grants are simply not available.
"AHCI was poorly designed."AHCI fitted disks well. It was outgrown, not mis-designed.
"NVMe 2.0 is a new protocol."2.0 mostly restructured the documents (Base, command sets, transports) and added features; the queue model is the same as 1.0.

Senior engineer insight

The history in this chapter is a sequence of bottlenecks moving: from the media, to the link, to the host controller, to the CPU's register reads and locks, back to the media. A senior engineer reviewing any design asks "when this bottleneck is fixed, what binds next?" The queue model in this chapter is a tiny example of that habit: list every ceiling, not just the first one you hit.

NVMe's speed rests on the host never waiting for the device. That only works because PCIe's ordering rules guarantee that a posted doorbell write does not overtake the memory writes that built the command (with the right barrier on the CPU side), and that the device's completion write is visible before its interrupt. When you see an NVMe bug that "only happens under load on some platforms", suspect the ordering contract first.

Early PCIe SSDs were faster than SATA, but each needed its own driver. NVMe's success came from being a standard that every operating system could ship in-box and every BIOS could boot from. Engineering excellence that cannot be adopted easily often loses to good-enough engineering that can.

Challenge

Add a fifth ceiling to the model: interrupt handling cost per completion on the cores (for example 1.5 µs per interrupt), with interrupt coalescing that handles n completions per interrupt. How large must n be for interrupts not to be the binding ceiling for NVMe at 16 cores?

Hint for question 2

Multiply the link bandwidth by four and recompute the link ceiling; then compare the remaining three numbers in the AHCI row.

Hint for the challenge

The interrupt ceiling is cores × n ÷ 1.5 µs. Set it above the device ceiling.

Solution

Q1. AHCI: one per port (per drive), shared by all CPUs under a lock. NVMe: one per granted I/O submission queue, typically one per CPU.

Q2. With the default constants, the SATA link binds at ≈ 146k IOPS. At 24 Gb/s (≈ 2.4 GB/s with 8b/10b) the link ceiling would be ≈ 586k, so the lock at 333k would bind next for multi-core runs, and the CPU ceiling (167k) for a single core.

Q3. The block layer may map fewer hardware contexts than queues granted (for example, if there are fewer CPUs, or if some queues are reserved for polling or for other queue types), and the driver may request fewer than the maximum. The controller may also grant fewer than requested.

Q4. Interrupt counts rising mainly on one NVMe queue's line, on the CPU where the program runs (or the CPU that queue is affined to). If the scheduler migrates the program, several lines rise in turn.

Challenge. At 16 cores, n completions per interrupt and 1.5 µs per interrupt, the ceiling is 16 × n ÷ 1.5e-6 = 10.7 million × n IOPS. Even n = 1 exceeds the 800k device ceiling, so interrupts do not bind at 16 cores. At 1 core, 1 × n ÷ 1.5e-6 = 667k × n: still above the 400k device ceiling at QD32. Interrupt cost matters more when it is higher or when the device is faster: rerun with 5 µs per interrupt and one core to see it bind.

What a senior engineer notices

That the model and the measurements are two different kinds of evidence. The model tells you what could bind; only measurement (with profiling) tells you what does. A senior engineer uses the model to choose which measurement to take, and never presents model output as a measurement.

Failure variants

Answers

1. AHCI: build the command table (FIS and PRDT), build the command header, set PxSACT and PxCI bits (under a lock). NVMe: write the SQ tail doorbell (after a write barrier).

2. SATA: 600 MB/s ÷ 4096 ≈ 146,000 IOPS. Gen5 x4: 15.75 GB/s ÷ 4096 ≈ 3.85 million IOPS.

3. 32 ÷ 0.00008 = 400,000 IOPS.

4. AHCI 1.0 (2004); SATA 6 Gb/s (2009); NVMe 1.0 (2011); NVMe-oF 1.0 (2016); NVMe 2.0 (2021); NVMe 2.4 (2026).

5. SQ2 tail = 0x1000 + (2 × 2) × 4 = 0x1010 → 0xFE60_1010. CQ2 head = 0x1000 + (2 × 2 + 1) × 4 = 0x1014 → 0xFE60_1014.

6. Reading host memory hits the CPU's cache-coherent memory system (tens of nanoseconds, possibly already in cache because the device's write updated it). Reading an HBA register is a non-posted transaction across the I/O fabric to a device and back (around a microsecond), during which the core stalls. NVMe also delivers the completion to the right core's queue and vector, so no searching is needed.

Quiz

  1. How many commands can SATA NCQ have outstanding?

    • Per-core submission/completion queue pairs

  8. Open question: name four NVMe design goals and the pre-NVMe limitation each one addresses.

    Model answer

    Many queues (single AHCI queue); deep queues (32-command NCQ); no register reads in the I/O path (AHCI status reads); MSI-X per queue (single shared interrupt); native PCIe attachment (600 MB/s SATA ceiling and extra host controller hop); a standard interface (proprietary PCIe SSD drivers).

  9. Open question: why did per-command overhead that was acceptable for disks become unacceptable for SSDs?

    Model answer

    Overhead is judged relative to device latency and multiplied by command rate. Against 10 ms disk accesses at a few hundred IOPS, microseconds were negligible. Against 80 µs flash reads at hundreds of thousands of IOPS, the same microseconds become a large share of latency and whole CPU cores of overhead.

  10. Open question: what is the difference between "a PCIe SSD" and "an NVMe SSD"?

    Model answer

    "PCIe SSD" names the transport: it connects over PCIe, possibly with a proprietary or AHCI interface. "NVMe SSD" names the protocol: it implements NVMe, usually over PCIe, but NVMe also runs over fabrics. Most modern PCIe SSDs are NVMe, but the terms are not equivalent.

Interview questions

Beginner

  1. Why is NVMe faster than SATA?

    Model answer

    Not because of different flash: because NVMe removes interface limits. It sits directly on PCIe (much more bandwidth, full duplex, no host adapter), supports many deep queues instead of one queue of 32, and has a lean command path with no register reads and per-queue interrupts, so it scales with cores and with the SSD's internal parallelism.

  2. What is AHCI?

    Model answer

    The Advanced Host Controller Interface: Intel's standard register and memory interface for SATA host controllers, with one 32-slot command list per port. It let one OS driver work with any SATA controller.

Intermediate

  1. Explain why NVMe uses doorbell writes but no register reads in the I/O path.

    Model answer

    Writes to device registers are posted: the CPU issues them and continues. Reads are non-posted: the CPU stalls for a round trip. NVMe arranges for everything the host needs to know to arrive in host memory (completion entries, including the SQ head pointer), so the host only ever writes to the device on the fast path.

  2. How does NVMe deliver completions to the right CPU?

    Model answer

    Each I/O completion queue is associated with an MSI-X vector when it is created; the OS programs that vector's address and data to target a specific CPU. With one queue pair per CPU, completions arrive in that CPU's queue and interrupt that CPU.

Senior

  1. A team migrates a database from SATA SSDs to NVMe and sees only a 10% improvement. What questions do you ask?

    Model answer

    What is the workload's concurrency? If the database issues one I/O at a time, latency is dominated by flash and software and NVMe's queueing advantage is unused. Is I/O spread across CPUs, or funneled through one thread and thus one queue? Is the bottleneck even storage (CPU, locks, network)? Are the NVMe drives on full-width, full-speed links? Are interrupts affined sensibly? I would measure queue depth actually achieved, per-queue interrupt distribution, and latency percentiles before and after, then decide whether the application needs more concurrency or whether storage was never the bottleneck.

  2. Walk through how you would find a bug where one NVMe queue never completes commands while others are fine.

    Model answer

    Localize first: one queue failing while others work clears the link and most of the controller. Check the queue's creation: did Create I/O CQ and Create I/O SQ complete successfully, with sizes within CAP.MQES and the SQ pointing at the right CQ? Then the doorbell: is it written at the address implied by CAP.DSTRD? On a trace, does a fetch follow the doorbell? If fetches and completions happen but software never sees them, check the CQ's MSI-X vector, its mask state and its CPU affinity, and the phase-tag handling in the driver. I would capture from before queue creation so the first abnormal event is in the trace, as in this chapter's example.

Teach-back challenge

Explain "why NVMe replaced SATA for SSDs" to three audiences.

Model explanations

To a child: "The old way had one counter and 32 pigeonholes for the whole town, and you had to keep asking the postmaster what was done. The new way gives every worker their own big box and their own bell, and the answers just appear in your box."

To a junior engineer: "SATA SSDs go through an AHCI host controller with one 32-command queue per drive, a shared lock, several slow register reads per command and a 600 MB/s link. NVMe puts the SSD straight on PCIe and gives each CPU its own submission and completion queues in host memory, deep enough for thousands of commands, with a single doorbell write to submit and completions written into memory with per-queue MSI-X interrupts."

To a senior engineer: "Flash collapsed device latency by two orders of magnitude, which exposed every per-command cost in the AHCI path: serialized submission, non-posted register reads, two-step command fetch, a single interrupt, a fixed half-duplex link. NVMe's queue-pair model moves all synchronization into posted writes and coherent host memory, scales with cores and device parallelism, and relies on PCIe ordering for correctness. The 2.0 family then separated semantics, command sets and transports so the same model extends to fabrics."

Chapter summary

SATA and AHCI were designed for disks: one queue of 32 commands per drive, a shared command list requiring locks, several non-posted register reads per command, a single interrupt and a 600 MB/s half-duplex link, all behind a host controller. When flash cut device latency to tens of microseconds and offered dozens of parallel units, each of those became a ceiling, and per-command CPU overhead multiplied by command rate became whole cores. Attaching SSDs directly to PCIe removed the link and adapter limits; early PCIe SSDs then fragmented into proprietary interfaces; NVMe (1.0 in 2011) standardized a lean queue-pair interface: per-core queues in host memory, deep rings, 64-byte commands fetched in one read, 16-byte completions written to memory, doorbell writes only, MSI-X per queue. NVMe 1.x grew features; 2.0 (2021) split the specification into Base, command sets and transports; the 2.4 set (August 2026) is this course's baseline.

Do not move on until you can:

  • describe how an AHCI command is issued and completed, and point out each serialization and register read;

  • explain why register reads stall the CPU and doorbell writes do not;
  • compute bandwidth and Little's Law ceilings for SATA and for NVMe on a given PCIe link;
  • explain the queue pair on all seven rungs of the ladder;
